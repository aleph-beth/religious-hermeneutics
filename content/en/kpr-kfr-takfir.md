# The Contemporary Reversal: the Modernity of Extended *Takfīr*
## From semantic operator to instrument of anathema — genealogy of a modern rupture

> **Methodological note.** This article addresses a politically and religiously sensitive subject. Three precautions are to be maintained throughout:
>
> 1. Do not confuse *Islam* with *jihadist Islamism*. The majority of Muslims worldwide do not practise extended *takfīr* and do not use the word *kāfir* in the radical polemical register studied here.
>
> 2. Do not confuse *historical Ibn Taymiyya* (a 14th-century theologian, more complex than his contemporary use suggests) with *recycled Ibn Taymiyya* (the figure used by jihadist currents). The thought proper to the former does not mechanically ground the operations of the latter.
>
> 3. Do not reproduce the Islamophobic mirror. The critique of extended *takfīr* in no way authorizes generalization against Islam or Muslims. Western Islamophobia, which essentializes Islam, reproduces in reverse the very structure of *takfīr* it claims to denounce.

## 1. Position of the problem {#s1}

The previous articles of the dossier have established that the Semitic root *k-p-r / k-f-r* is, on the eve of modernity, an operator of great richness: seven competing registers, held together by all the great inheriting traditions (rabbinic, Christian, classical Islamic, Sufi, falsafian). Restraint in the use of *takfīr* is the *rule* in classical Sunnism; Khārijite maximalism is the *exception* explicitly denounced by the great theologians.

**Yet dominant contemporary usage — in media space, in jihadist networks, in inter-Muslim polemics, and in Western perception — has almost entirely reduced the root to *one only* of its poles: the negative polemical pole.** The word *kāfir* has become, in a significant part of globalized Islamic discourse, a word of *extended anathema*, sometimes uttered against fellow Muslims, and systematically against non-Muslims.

This article reconstitutes **the short history (14th-21st centuries)** of this reduction. It does not claim to represent Islam as a whole — the majority of practising Muslims do not use the word *kāfir* in this radical polemical register, and many reformist, Sufi, moderate traditional and academic currents reject it. It documents how **a doctrinal minority has, over a few centuries, captured public usage of the root**, at the expense of its historical depth.

## 2. The Ibn Taymiyya turn (13th-14th centuries) {#s2}

### 2.1 The context

Taqī al-Dīn Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) writes after the fall of Baghdad (1258) and against the background of Mongol pressure on the Muslim world. His thought is inscribed within an horizon of *civilizational crisis*. The Mongols have officially converted to Islam but continue to apply Mongol *Yāsā* as a parallel law to *Sharīʿa*. Ibn Taymiyya writes in threatened Mamluk Syria, and his tone is polemical.

### 2.2 The Mardin fatwa (c. 1303)

Question posed: are the converted Mongols Muslims or *kuffār*? Ibn Taymiyya's answer: they are in *intermediate* territory (*dār murakkaba*); applying the *Yāsā* in place of the *Sharīʿa* may constitute *kufr*. **This fatwa becomes, six centuries later, one of the founding texts of modern *takfīr*** — reinterpreted by Faraj, Quṭb, Maqdisī, Daesh as legitimating *takfīr* against Muslim rulers who *"do not apply the Sharīʿa."*

### 2.3 What Ibn Taymiyya actually says

The work of Yahya Michot (*Muslims under Non-Muslim Rule*, 2006) and Caterina Bori has shown that the jihadist reading of the Mardin fatwa is *unilateral*. Ibn Taymiyya carefully distinguishes:

- *takfīr al-nawʿ*: qualifying *a type of act* (e.g., refusing to apply the Sharīʿa) as falling under *kufr* in the abstract;
- *takfīr al-muʿayyan*: qualifying *a determinate individual* as *kāfir*.

**He does not authorize the latter without rigorous procedure**, without establishing the conditions (*shurūṭ*) and verifying the absence of impediments (*mawāniʿ*). His reading is *more restrictive* than that of contemporary jihadists.

But the polemical tone of his writings, his sharp critique of speculative Sufism (*Risāla fī al-Tawassul wa-l-Wasīla*), of Imāmī Shīʿism (*Minhāj al-Sunna*) and of falsafism, opens a *discursive space* in which *takfīr* becomes thinkable again. The neo-Hanbalite turn he brings will polemicize against any allegorical reading of the Qur'an. His disciple Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya (d. 1350) prolongs the current.

## 3. Wahhabism and the extension of *takfīr* (18th century) {#s3}

### 3.1 Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb

Ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb (d. 1792) reads Ibn Taymiyya and radicalizes. His central work, *Kitāb al-Tawḥīd* (*"Book of Oneness"*), posits a tripartite distinction:

- *tawḥīd al-rubūbiyya* (oneness of lordship);
- *tawḥīd al-ulūhiyya* (oneness of worship);
- *tawḥīd al-asmāʾ wa-l-ṣifāt* (oneness of names and attributes).

His strong thesis: **the majority of Muslims of his time practise a disguised *shirk*** (visiting saints' tombs, intercession of prophets, Sufism, Shīʿism, *mawlid* of the Prophet) **and are therefore, on a strict reading, *kuffār*.** Wahhabism massively extends *takfīr* to practices widespread in the traditional Muslim world.

### 3.2 The Saudi alliance (1744)

The pact between Ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb and Muḥammad ibn Saʿūd transforms *takfīr* into an *instrument of conquest*. Cities and tribes refusing Wahhabism are declared in *kufr* and therefore to be combated. Massacres of Karbala (1802), Mecca (1803), Medina (1805) — destruction of mausoleums, sacking of tombs. **The doctrine of generalized *takfīr* founds a military-political dynamic unprecedented in classical Sunni history.**

### 3.3 Rejection by the Sunni establishment

Ottoman ulemas, Cairo's al-Azhar, the Mālikī Maghrebi schools and the Sufis sharply contest Wahhabism in the 18th and 19th centuries. Several fatwas declare it *bidʿa* (heretical innovation). Wahhabism remains a *regional sect* until the Saudi unification of the 20th century (1932), then benefits from oil revenues from the 1950s onwards to spread its preaching globally (financing of mosques, Mecca printing presses, scholarships at the Islamic University of Medina, satellite TV and internet).

## 4. Mawdūdī, Quṭp and modern *jāhiliyya* {#s4}

The decisive conceptual transformation occurs with two 20th-century thinkers.

### 4.1 Abū al-Aʿlā Mawdūdī (d. 1979)

Founder of *Jamāʿat-i Islāmī* in the Indian subcontinent, Mawdūdī reinterprets the *jāhiliyya* (the "ignorance" of the pre-Islamic period according to the Qur'an) as an *extensible sociological category*. *Jāhiliyya* is no longer a past historical state: it is *any society that does not recognize divine sovereignty* (*ḥākimiyya*). Including contemporary Muslim societies that apply positive laws inherited from the West. **Kufr becomes a *political* category.**

### 4.2 Sayyid Quṭb (d. 1966)

Ideologue of the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, Quṭb radicalizes Mawdūdī. In his book *Maʿālim fī al-Ṭarīq* (*"Milestones,"* written in Nasser's prison and published in 1964), he posits a thesis without precedent in the tradition: *"All current societies, including Muslim societies, are in jāhiliyya."*

This includes Arab nationalist regimes (Nasser), the petromonarchies, the secular regimes. For Quṭb, these regimes are governed by disguised *kuffār*, and their societies live a collective *kufr*. The way out is the formation of a *vanguard* (*ṭalīʿa*) that performs *hijra* (spiritual and physical secession) and combats modern *jāhiliyya*.

### 4.3 The decisive conceptual inversion

*Takfīr*, which in classical Sunnism was an *exception reserved for extreme cases*, becomes in Quṭb a *general category applied to entire societies*. **This is the most radical inversion**: it is no longer the exception that must be demonstrated for *kufr* to obtain, it is *authentic Muslim faith* that must be demonstrated *against the general presumption of kufr*.

This inversion is, philosophically and theologically, *without precedent in the tradition*. No classical Sunni theologian — not even Ibn Taymiyya — has held this position. Quṭb represents a rupture *internal* to Islamic thought.

## 5. The doctrine of armed *takfīr* (1970-2020) {#s5}

### 5.1 Muḥammad ʿAbd al-Salām Faraj

Ideologue of Egyptian Islamic Jihad, Faraj (d. 1982) in *al-Farīḍa al-Ghāʾiba* (*"The Absent Obligation,"* 1981) operationalizes Quṭb. His thesis: *jihād* against Muslim regimes in *kufr* is an *individual obligation* (*farḍ ʿayn*), even superior to the pilgrimage. He grounds the legitimation of Sadat's assassination (October 1981).

### 5.2 *al-Takfīr wa-l-Hijra*

The group **Jamāʿat al-Muslimīn** (*"Society of Muslims"*) of Shukrī Muṣṭafā, known as *al-Takfīr wa-l-Hijra* (1971-1977), practises literal *takfīr*: Egyptian society is *kufr*, one exits it (*hijra*), one lives in a sectarian group in the desert, one combats the *kuffār*. The movement is dismantled, but its logic survives as a *matrix*.

### 5.3 Abū Muḥammad al-Maqdisī

Born in 1959, Maqdisī is the Jordanian-Palestinian theorist of extended *takfīr*. In *Millat Ibrāhīm* (1984), he reinterprets Ibn Taymiyya's Mardin fatwa to legitimate *takfīr* against the Gulf regimes. Mentor of al-Zarqāwī (al-Qaida in Iraq), he later takes critical positions against the excesses of Daesh — but his basic doctrine remains foundational for the modern takfīrī current.

### 5.4 al-Qāʿida

Under Osama Bin Laden and Zawāhirī, al-Qaida theorizes global *jihād* but remains relatively *restrained* on *takfīr* of ordinary Muslims: the declared target is the "far enemy" (United States, Israel) and allied regimes. Takfīrī doctrine here is more strategic than populist.

### 5.5 Daesh

The "Islamic State" (2014-2019) radicalizes to the extreme: extensive *takfīr* of Shīʿīs, Sufis, Yezidis (declared not *ahl al-kitāb*), *"moderate"* Sunnis, civil servants of Arab states. Reference theologians: Abū Bakr Nājī, *Idārat al-tawaḥḥush* (*"The Management of Savagery,"* c. 2004); Turkī al-Binʿalī. **Daesh marks the endpoint of the takfīrī trajectory**: declared *kufr* against virtually the entire Muslim world.

Daesh does not represent global Islam. It represents a *minoritarian militant fraction*, whose media amplification has distorted external (and sometimes internal) perception.

## 6. Media diffusion and the Islamophobic mirror {#s6}

### 6.1 The media turn (1990-2020)

Satellite channels (from 1991), the Internet (from the 2000s), and social media (from the 2010s) have massively *amplified* the diffusion of radical *takfīr* and turned it into a *current discursive category*. The word *kāfir* has become a term of ordinary debate in certain circles, used against:

- non-Muslims (Jews, Christians, Hindus, Buddhists, atheists);
- Muslims accused of heterodox practices (Sufis, Shīʿīs, Ahmadis, Alawites);
- Muslims accused of lukewarmness, modernism, or accommodation with the West.

Mediatized usage has turned the word into a *sign of polemical identity* — often uttered without the theological background of *kalām*, as a mere communal insult. This *vulgarization* is a *strictly contemporary* phenomenon.

### 6.2 The "clash of civilizations" ratchet effect

On the Western side, the diffusion of an essentialized *kāfir* has been taken up by Islamophobic currents that *mirror* the takfīrī reading: *"Muslims call us *kuffār*, so they hate us essentially."* This double capture — by the jihadists on one side, by their Islamophobic adversaries on the other — has *frozen* the word in a unique polemical sense, obliterating six millennia of semantic richness.

**Both camps have an interest in freezing the word in its polemical sense**: for one, it is the identity weapon; for the other, it is the proof of Islam's essential alterity. These two usages *mutually reinforce* (Cesari 2013, Tibi 2008).

### 6.3 Three amplification factors

- **Media concentration.** Polemical *kāfir* makes noise; peasant, expiatory, mystical *kāfir* does not make headlines (Roy, *L'islam mondialisé*, 2002).
- **Loss of classical formation.** A significant portion of Muslims trained since the 20th century in modernized curricula (state schools, universities, preaching by cassette then by Internet) no longer receives classical formation in *kalām*, advanced *fiqh*, philology. *Kāfir* reaches them amputated of its six other poles (Roy, *La sainte ignorance*, 2008; Lacroix 2011).
- **Crisis of contemporary Muslim societies.** Quṭb writes in Nasser's prison; Maqdisī writes after the Gulf War; Daesh emerges in post-2003 Iraqi chaos. Extensive *takfīr* is tied to a *historical moment* in which collective Muslim identity is perceived as besieged, and the rhetoric of communal purity becomes psychologically attractive.

## 7. Contemporary critical voices {#s7}

The takfīrī inversion has not been passively accepted. Three strong critical lines have constituted themselves in the 20th-21st centuries.

### 7.1 The academic-philological critique

**Mohammed Arkoun** (d. 2010), in *Lectures du Coran* and *La pensée arabe*, argues that the Qur'an has been *"dogmatized"* and that historical-critical reading must reopen it. For Arkoun, Qur'anic *kufr* has been frozen into an ontological category whereas it is, in the text, an *ethical-relational* category. An influential position in French and Maghrebi academia.

**Naṣr Ḥāmid Abū Zayd** (d. 2010), in *Mafhūm al-naṣṣ* (*"The Concept of the Text,"* 1990), applies modern hermeneutics to the Qur'an. Himself declared *kāfir* by an Egyptian court in 1995 (forced to annul his marriage and flee to the Netherlands), his case tragically illustrates the contemporary weapon of *takfīr*.

### 7.2 The theological-reformist critique

**Mohammed Ṭalbī** (d. 2017, Tunisia), in *Plaidoyer pour un islam moderne* (1998) and *Penseur libre en islam* (2002), defends a *historicist* reading of Qur'anic *kufr*, tied to the Meccan and Medinan polemical situation, and therefore *not transposable* as such to modernity.

**ʿAbdolkarim Soroush** (Iran, b. 1945), in *Reason, Freedom, and Democracy in Islam* (2000), distinguishes the *Qur'an-message* (universal) from the *Qur'an-discourse* (situated). *Takfīr* belongs to the latter, not the former. Persecuted by the Iranian regime.

**Khaled Abou El Fadl** (United States), in *The Great Theft: Wrestling Islam from the Extremists* (2005), theorizes a humanist classical Islam against modern *"puritans."*

### 7.3 The Sufi and traditional resistance

Contemporary Sufism (Naqshbandiyya, Tijāniyya, Qādiriyya, Shādhiliyya brotherhoods) massively maintains the inversive reading of *kufr* (cf. article 2 § 10): *true kufr is sectarianism and extended takfīr*. The *Mawlid* (celebration of the Prophet's birth), a millennia-old practice condemned by Wahhabis as *bidʿa*, is still massively celebrated in the Muslim world.

Mainstream academic Sunnism (al-Azhar in Cairo, Zaytuna in Tunis, Qarawiyyīn in Fez, moderate Deobandi) maintains the *classical restraint on takfīr*. **The Amman Declaration** (Jordan, 2005), signed by 552 ulemas from 84 countries, explicitly condemns extensive *takfīr* and recognizes the legitimacy of eight juridical schools (including Jaʿfarī Shīʿī and Ibāḍī) and Sufism. This document constitutes a *collective disavowal* of takfīrī doctrine by mainstream religious authority.

## 8. Synthetic balance: the modern rupture {#s8}

If one looks at the root *k-f-r* over the long term (from the 18th century BCE — Atrahasis — to the 21st century), and isolates the *last three centuries*, one observes a transformation **statistically, doctrinally and discursively unprecedented**:

**On the statistical plane.** The word *kāfir* in 19th-21st-century Muslim public discourse is *quantitatively crushed* by its negative polemical use. Classical uses (peasant, expiation, protective gesture, dialectical pole) become inaudible in public space, preserved only in academic commentaries and Sufi practice.

**On the doctrinal plane.** *Takfīr* has passed from the status of *reserved exception* (Ghazālī) to that of *category applicable to entire populations* (Quṭb, Daesh). This extension is a *rupture* with classical Sunnism.

**On the discursive plane.** The word has been captured by a *rhetoric of identification-secession* (*"we, the true believers, against them, the *kuffār*"*), an inverse structure of Meccan Qur'anic rhetoric (in which *kufr* is a state from which everyone can exit through *shukr* and *īmān*).

**On the hermeneutic plane.** The plurality of registers (material, cultic, juridical, mystical, agrarian, expiatory) has been flattened. The plural semantic operator has become a *univocal label*. Language itself has impoverished.

**Conclusion.** Dominant contemporary usage of the word *kāfir* — that which circulates in the media, in certain militant networks, in the pedagogy of minoritarian but loud groups — is *not the voice of the Islamic tradition* in its breadth. It is a *modern innovation* (18th-20th centuries) that has installed itself at the cost of a rupture with:

- pre-Islamic Arabic (which knew the neutral-positive *kāfir*);
- the Qur'an itself (which preserves the three polarities);
- classical Sunni *kalām* (which calls for restraint);
- Islamic jurisprudence (which maintains the expiatory pole);
- Sufi mysticism (which dialectically inverts the root);
- *falsafa* (which relativizes the category);
- the sister traditions (Judaism, Christianity) that share the same Semitic root.

**This rupture is not Hellenic, not Western, not external to Islam.** It is *internal to modern Islam*, and its critique therefore does not need to come from outside: it can be carried out from the deep memory of the root itself, through the resources of philology, classical theology and comparative reading.

## 9. Four-question grid for analysing a contemporary usage {#s9}

To set out a practical grid, we propose this four-question protocol to be applied to any contemporary occurrence of the word *kāfir*:

**(a) From which discursive tradition does this usage come?** Classical Sunnism? 18th-century Wahhabism? 20th-century Quṭbism? Contemporary jihadism? Sufism? Academic? Mediatized vulgarized?

**(b) Which semantic pole is mobilized?** Material recovering? Expiation? Ingratitude? Denial? Social anathema? Polemical label?

**(c) Is the speaker aware of the historical plurality of the root, or does he reduce it to a single sense?**

**(d) Is the usage in *continuity* with the majoritarian classical tradition (reluctance on *takfīr*, preservation of the expiatory pole), or in *rupture* (takfīrī extension, anti-Muslim polemic)?**

This grid allows one to *locate* a discourse without confusing it with *"Islam."* It avoids essentialization on both the apologetic and Islamophobic sides.
