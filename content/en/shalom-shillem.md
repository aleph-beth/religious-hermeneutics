## 1. Opening — A deliberately damaged letter {#s1}

There exists, in the Hebrew Bible, a deliberately damaged letter.

The passage is violent. A man named Phinehas catches a couple in the act and runs them both through with a single thrust of his spear. The text immediately continues: God grants him "my covenant of peace," **בְּרִיתִי שָׁלוֹם** (*briti shalom*, Numbers 25:12). But for centuries the scribes have copied that word — *shalom* — with a letter broken in its middle: the *vav* of שלום is drawn with its stem interrupted, what the tradition calls a **vav qetiʿa** (וי"ו קטיעה), a "severed vav." A broken stroke, a minuscule fracture at the heart of the word, transmitted from scroll to scroll with scrupulous care.

The practice is ancient and well attested: the Talmud (*Kiddushin* 66b) is usually cited as its earliest mention, and the medieval codifiers record it among the graphic irregularities the copyist must reproduce exactly. The Masorah itself says nothing about what one is supposed to understand by it. It does not comment; it prescribes. No one ever wrote in the margin what the break meant.

But everyone understood it, and the readings have accumulated: a peace obtained by the spear cannot be written intact. It limps. It carries the trace of what it cost.

This broken letter is the best point of departure for understanding a word we think we know, and translate far too quickly. For *shalom* in Hebrew, *salām* in Arabic, *shlama* in Aramaic do not first of all mean "peace." They say something stranger, and far more disturbing.

I want to follow that thread in four movements: what the root actually means; why peace, in these languages, has to be paid for; what becomes of the characters who bear the name; and finally what all this teaches us about a religion that chose this particular word to name itself.

---

## 2. The root Sh-L-M: whole, not peaceful {#s2}

Semitic languages work by three-consonant roots. The one that concerns us, **ש־ל־ם** (*Sh-L-M*) in Hebrew, **س-ل-م** (*S-L-M*) in Arabic, does not mean peace. It means: **to be whole, complete, finished, quits**.

The field is common to the entire Semitic family and goes back to the second millennium. Akkadian has *šalāmu*, "to be intact, in good order," and the noun *šulmu*, which serves as an epistolary opening formula — the Amarna letters begin with a report on the *šulmu* of the king, his house, his horses, his land. Ugaritic has *šlm*, Aramaic *shlama*, Arabic *salām*. Everywhere the same idea: the account is in order, nothing is missing.

Everything starts there, and everything follows from it. A body without a wound is *shalem*. A house in which nothing is lacking is *shalem*. To ask someone "*shalom*?" is not to ask whether they are at peace, but whether everything, in their world, is still standing. It is a status report, almost an inventory.

Two texts show this with cruel clarity. When David is sent to the camp by his father, the errand is phrased thus: to inquire after the *shalom* of his brothers and to bring back their token (1 Samuel 17:18) — the word here designates the equivalent of a situation report, to be brought back in writing or by witness. And when the Shunammite woman, whose child has just died on her knees, meets Elisha's servant, who ritually asks her "*shalom* to you? *shalom* to your husband? *shalom* to the child?", she answers: *shalom* (2 Kings 4:26). She lies, and yet the word is not exactly a lie: it designates a declared state, the provisional closing of an account she refuses to open in front of a subordinate. She reserves the opening for the prophet.

That is what the word does, before any peace at all: it declares that a set is complete.

## 3. *Shillem*: to repay, to discharge, to avenge {#s3}

Now, what does one do when one wants to make a thing whole again? One pays. And that is exactly the meaning of the intensive form of the verb: **שִׁלֵּם** (*shillem*) is to reimburse, to discharge a debt, to render what one owes. The thief, in the Bible, must *shillem* — restore in full, and sometimes fourfold or fivefold (Exodus 21:37; 22:2-6). Hebrew here uses a characteristic doubling, *shallem yeshallem*, "he shall surely pay": all the way, leaving no remainder.

Here is the tipping point. For the same operation, applied no longer to a stolen object but to an offence suffered, is no longer called reimbursement: it is called vengeance. Deuteronomy sets the two words side by side, in the Song of Moses: **לִי נָקָם וְשִׁלֵּם** (*li naqam ve-shillem*), "vengeance is mine, and recompense" (Deut 32:35). Isaiah announces "a day of vengeance for YHWH, a year of *shillumim* for the cause of Zion" (Isa 34:8) — a year of settlements, literally, and the word is the same as the word for peace. Hosea speaks of "the days of *shillum*" come upon Israel (Hos 9:7).

There are not two words here that happen to resemble one another, nor a homonymous root. There is a single operation, seen from the two sides of the ledger: **to close an account**. To be reconciled and to settle scores are, in this language, the same gesture. It is not the same world, but it is the same verb.

Modern Hebrew has forgotten none of it. *Leshallem*, still today, is to pay; a *tashlum* is a payment. And the reparations agreement signed in 1952 between Israel and the Federal Republic of Germany is called **הסכם השילומים** (*heskem ha-shillumim*): the root that serves to greet the living serves to name the money paid for the dead. No speaker perceives any contradiction in this — and that is precisely what should stop us. The language treats as a single thing what our morality carefully keeps apart.

---

## 4. The *shelamim*: peace as what is left of the sacrifice {#s4}

The ritual says it more bluntly still. The sacrifice Leviticus calls **שְׁלָמִים** (*shelamim*) — from the same root — is rendered in English as "peace offering" or "offering of well-being" (Lev 3; 7:11-21). It is a slaughtered animal, whose blood is dashed against the altar, whose fat is burned, and whose flesh is then shared out: a portion to the priest, the rest eaten by those who brought it.

Peace here is not an atmosphere, nor a precondition. It is **what is left once the debt has been paid in bodies**. One eats together because an animal has died. One is reconciled because something has been settled.

That the *shelamim* is a discharging sacrifice, a verse of Proverbs states outright, putting both forms of the root in the same sentence: *"I had *shelamim* offerings to make; today I have paid (shillamti) my vows"* (Prov 7:14). The peace offering is the means by which one discharges an obligation. Lexicographers still disagree about the term's primary sense — closing offering, covenant offering, votive offering — but every hypothesis turns around the same core: something is rendered, and it is over.

This casts a retrospective light on the Phinehas scene, and makes it logical to the point of discomfort: a man runs someone through, therefore an account closes, therefore there is "peace." The mechanism is impeccable. And the scribe breaks the letter — because the tradition senses, obscurely, that this mechanism is the problem and not the solution.

---

## 5. *Shalem*: what iron has not touched {#s5}

But the same root also offers a way out, and it is a magnificent one.

Deuteronomy commands that the altar be built of "whole stones" — **אֲבָנִים שְׁלֵמוֹת** (*avanim shlemot*), the same word again — and specifies why: no iron tool is to be lifted upon them (Deut 27:5-6). Exodus says the same thing negatively: if you build an altar of hewn stones, "you have lifted your chisel upon it, and you have profaned it" (Ex 20:25). The dressed stone is profaned. Iron, which serves to make war, cannot serve to build what reconciles.

"Whole," then, means here, very concretely: **what iron has not cut into**. In its most material sense, peace is the state of what has not been cut.

And Solomon's Temple draws from this a staggering architectural consequence: throughout its construction, neither hammer nor axe nor any iron tool was heard on the site; the stones had been finished elsewhere, at the quarry, and arrived ready (1 Kings 6:7). The king who carries peace in his name builds a house in which the noise of iron is forbidden. This is the dossier followed in detail by the article "The Stone, the Hewing, and the Rock," to which the present one is joined by its very root: *shalem* is the word that articulates the two.

Yet this beauty has a reverse side, and it must be named. If "peace" means "intact," then the word also becomes the name of a fantasy: that of what would never have been mixed, never cut into, never dressed. Every nostalgia for a pure beginning draws on that same water table. "Complete" means "with no remainder" — and something always has to be done with the remainder.

---

## 6. A name of God, a word of greeting {#s6}

One last usage completes the word's charge: *shalom* is a divine name.

Gideon, after his theophany, builds an altar and names it **יהוה שָׁלוֹם** (*YHWH Shalom*, Judg 6:24). The Talmud draws a practical consequence: "Shalom is a name of the Holy One, blessed be He," whence the prohibition on greeting someone in an unclean place such as the bathhouse (*Shabbat* 10b). The everyday word is a sacred name; one does not pronounce it just anywhere. The midrash goes further: "Great is peace, for the name of the Holy One is peace" (*Leviticus Rabbah* 9:9).

Arabic makes exactly the same gesture. Among the divine names the Qur'an enumerates stands **السَّلَام** (*as-Salām*) — "the King, the Most Holy, the Peace" (Q 59:23). And the greeting formula, *as-salāmu ʿalaykum*, is in Arabic as in Hebrew (*shalom aleikhem*) a verbless nominal sentence: neither a wish nor a promise, but a declaration. One does not say "may peace be upon you"; one says "peace upon you," that is to say, literally: *nothing is open between us*.

To greet, in these languages, is to declare an account settled. Ordinary courtesy is a receipt.

## 7. Those who bear the name: Absalom and Solomon {#s7}

The biblical characters expose this ambivalence better than any treatise.

**Absalom** — **אַבְשָׁלוֹם** (*Av-shalom*), "the father is peace" — is the son who has his brother Amnon killed, revolts against David, and dies caught by his head in the branches of a terebinth, where tradition reads his hair (2 Sam 13; 18:9-15). The text itself plays on the name, and heavily: from the ramparts the father watches for news of the battle and asks twice **הֲשָׁלוֹם לַנַּעַר לְאַבְשָׁלוֹם** (*ha-shalom la-naʿar le-Avshalom*), "is it peace with the young man Absalom?" (2 Sam 18:29, 32). The first messenger evades; the second must answer. And the answer, deferred and then terrible, is that his son's peace is his death. The account of the house of David, opened by Amnon, is settled there.

**Solomon** — **שְׁלֹמֹה** (*Shlomo*), the peaceful one — seems the exact opposite. The Bible even supplies the etymology, in David's own mouth: "Solomon shall be his name, and I will give peace and quiet to Israel in his days"; and it is for that reason that he will build the Temple, which his father, having shed too much blood, is not permitted to build (1 Chron 22:8-9; 28:3).

Except that his reign opens with three political executions — Adonijah, Joab, Shimei — and the banishment of the priest Abiathar (1 Kings 2). The text is explicit about what those deaths accomplish: it is after them that "the kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon" (1 Kings 2:46). Solomonic peace is not the absence of a settling of scores: it is its product. It is a balance struck before it is a peace. Only afterwards comes the silent building site, with no iron tool.

The opposition, then, is not between a villain and a hero. It is between **two ways of closing an account**: by revolt, which reopens it and kills; by execution, which closes it and builds on that silence.

## 8. Jerusalem: the city of completion {#s8}

The city's name probably carries the same root, but not in the sense piety lends it.

**יְרוּשָׁלַ͏ִם** (*Yerushalayim*) is attested long before Israel: the Egyptian execration texts of the early second millennium know a *Rushalimum*, and the Amarna letters, in the fourteenth century, an *Urusalim*. The most common reconstruction reads it as "foundation of Shalim" — from the West Semitic verb *yrw*, "to found," and the name of a Ugaritic deity, **Shalim**, the dusk, twin brother of Shaḥar, the dawn (KTU 1.23). The city of completion thus owes its name to the god of evening — that is, to the moment when the day is complete.

Hebrew, for its part, hears "peace," and works it. Psalm 122 piles up the alliterations to the point of vertigo: *shaʾalu shlom Yerushalayim… yehi shalom be-ḥeilekh* — "ask for the peace of Jerusalem… may there be peace within your walls" (Ps 122:6-7), where the verb "to ask" (*shaʾal*) itself echoes the name. The midrash, for its part, recounts that Abraham had named it *Yireh*, "he will see," and Shem *Shalem*, and that God, so as to offend neither of the two righteous men, combined the two names into *Yerushalem* (*Bereshit Rabbah* 56:10).

The city of completeness is also the most besieged city in the world. Jesus will weep over it, saying that it did not recognise "the things that make for peace" (Luke 19:42) — and will announce, in the same breath, that not one stone will be left upon another within it. This is not an irony of history: it is the logic of the word. A city named "completion" is a city whose account has never finished being settled.

## 9. The two Salomes: what the text says, what the culture heard {#s9}

It is readily said that there are in the Gospels two women of the same name, and that they are exact inverses of one another. One, Salome, follows Jesus to the cross and comes to the tomb on Easter morning (Mark 15:40; 16:1). The other, Salome, dances before Herod and obtains the head of John the Baptist. And the name comes from the same word as peace: Salome is simply the Greek form of the Hebrew *Shlomit*.

The symmetry is too good. It has to be dismantled before it can be used.

For the dancer is not named. Open Mark, open Matthew: one reads only "the daughter of Herodias" (Mark 6:22; Matt 14:6). No Gospel gives her a name. That name comes to us from elsewhere — from the Jewish historian Flavius Josephus, who, in an entirely different book and for entirely different genealogical reasons, mentions in passing that the daughter of Herodias was called Salome (*Jewish Antiquities* XVIII, 136). It was later Christian culture that brought the two pieces of information together; then, century by century, painting by painting, opera by opera — as far as Wilde and Strauss — it made Salome the very name of murderous seduction.

Add to this the onomastic argument, which is decisive. The census of Jewish names in Second Temple Palestine shows that *Shelamzion / Salome* is, along with Mary, the most widespread female name in the Judea of the period: between them, the two forms cover close to half of all known women. To meet two Salomes in a single corpus therefore means no more than meeting two Marys there — and that is precisely what happens.

So much for rigour. But the matter does not stop there: it begins there.

For one must now ask why. Why did collective memory spontaneously give the name of peace to the woman who has a head brought down? Why did this doublet, which the text does not contain, impose itself so self-evidently that everyone believes they read it in the Gospel?

Because the scene is, in reality, a scene of payment — and the culture heard that without knowing it.

Read it again. Herod has sworn. He has made an oath in front of his guests; the girl asks for a head; and the text specifies that he was grieved — "but because of his oaths and of those reclining at table, he ordered it to be given to her" (Mark 6:26). No one in this scene hates John; Mark even says that Herod feared him, protected him, and heard him gladly (Mark 6:20). What kills John is a debt of honour contracted in public. Herod pays his word with a head.

That is exactly the operation the root carries: an account opens, it has to be settled, and it is settled in bodies. Salome's name is not in the text — but the logic of that name is there in full. The tradition was not mistaken; it simply heard the word before it knew how to read it.

## 10. Shlom-Tsion: the queen whose peace ended in occupation {#s10}

And if one wants a better documented case, history supplies one a century earlier.

Salome Alexandra, in Hebrew **שְׁלוֹמְצִיּוֹן** (*Shlom-Tsion*), "peace of Zion," reigned over Judea from 76 to 67 BCE. Her memory, in rabbinic literature, is that of a golden age: it is said that under her reign the rains fell on time and grains of wheat reached the size of olive pits (*Taʿanit* 23a). The name also appears in the Qumran manuscripts, which guarantees its actual use.

At her death, her two sons, Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II, disputed the throne; their fratricidal war called on Rome to arbitrate, and Pompey, having arbitrated, entered Jerusalem in 63. The queen who bore the peace of Zion in her name bequeathed to Zion the end of its independence.

The pattern is the same as with Solomon, but unfolded over time: a peace that is only an account held closed during a lifetime, and that reopens the instant the hand holding it withdraws.

## 11. To transmit and to betray: *ashlem*, *masar*, *tradere* {#s11}

In Aramaic and Syriac — the languages of the Christian Near East — the same root produces a verb meaning **to place into the hands**: **ܐܫܠܡ** (*ashlem*). And from that verb are born two twin words: **ܡܫܠܡܢܘܬܐ** (*mashlmānūtā*), *tradition*, what is handed on from one generation to the next, and **ܡܫܠܡܢܐ** (*mashlmānā*), *the one who hands over* — the traitor.

It is the word the Peshitta uses to designate Judas: *haw d-ashlmeh*, "the one who handed him over." Greek already had the same equivocation — παραδίδωμι, "to hand over" and "to transmit," whence παράδοσις, tradition — and Latin bequeathed it to us: *tradere* yields both *traditio* and *traditor*, whence English "traitor." Church history even turned this doublet into a crisis: during the Diocletianic persecution, those bishops who had **handed over** the Scriptures to the authorities were called *traditores*, and the quarrel over the validity of their sacraments produced the Donatist schism. A librarian's word became a criminal charge.

But Hebrew offers a still more troubling parallel, on a different root. **מָסַר** (*masar*), "to hand over, to transmit," yields **מָסֹרֶת** (*masoret*), tradition — and also yields **מוֹסֵר** (*moser*), the informer, the one who hands a Jew over to non-Jewish authorities, a figure halakhah treats with extreme severity. The Masorah, literally "what is handed on," and the *moser*, the one who hands someone over: the same gesture, the same word.

The loop can then be measured. The body of scribes that transmits the broken vav of Phinehas is named after the very act of handing over — and that name carries within it the possibility of denunciation.

To transmit and to betray are a single gesture: to place something into hands that are not one's own. What decides is the hands.

## 12. Arabic: *salima*, *sallama*, *aslama* {#s12}

Arabic inherits all of this intact, and with a remarkable morphological clarity, each verbal form taking charge of one moment of the operation.

**سَلِمَ** (*salima*), the simple form: to be safe and sound, intact, unharmed. **سَلَام** (*salām*), the noun: peace, but first of all integrity. **سَلَّمَ** (*sallama*), the intensive form: to hand over, to transmit, to deliver an object — and to greet, whence the *taslīm* that closes the ritual prayer, literally the final "handing over." **أَسْلَمَ** (*aslama*), the causative form: to hand oneself over entirely — but also, in pre-Islamic usage well attested in early poetry, to abandon someone to their fate, to leave them defenceless. One can *aslama* oneself. One can *aslama* another. The verb does not make the distinction; only the complement does.

Arabic does, downstream, distinguish what the root conflates upstream: *istislām* is military surrender, and *mustaslim*, the one who surrenders, is not *muslim*. The language felt the need for a separate form for capitulation — a sign that it knew the risk of confusion.

Finally, the Arab lexicographers note a curiosity worth a whole treatise: a man who has been bitten by a snake is called **سَلِيم** (*salīm*), "the safe and sound one." It is a deliberate euphemism, what Arabic rhetoric calls *tafāʾul*, optimistic naming: one names the thing by its opposite in the hope that the word will avert the fact. The language knows how to say the reverse of what it names — and it knows it from the very root that says peace.

## 13. A religion named after a gesture: into whose hands? {#s13}

Islam is one of the very few major traditions to have named itself with a **noun of action** — a *maṣdar*. Not a people, not a founder, not a place, not a book: a gesture. And that gesture, as we have just seen, carries two directions in a single verb — to hand oneself over, and to hand another over.

The usual question — "does islam mean peace or submission?" — is therefore badly framed. It is a symmetrical trap, in which one side softens and the other hardens, but in which both assume that a root possesses a single meaning. The real question is a different one: **into whose hands?**

For a gesture of handing over never exists in the air. It has an addressee, and the addressee decides everything. Handed to God, this gesture is trusting abandonment: the Qur'an uses it of Abraham, whom it calls *ḥanīfan musliman* when no community yet exists (Q 3:67) — the word designates a movement of the soul, not a membership. Handed to an institution, a body, a power, the same gesture becomes allegiance. And the next step, historically documented, is that the one who has handed himself over may in turn hand over others: denounce, exclude, deliver. It is the *moser* reappearing, in another language.

The Qur'an itself perceived this ambiguity, and formulated it. In a remarkable passage, bedouins declare "we have believed," and the answer comes: *"Do not say you have believed; say rather: we have handed ourselves over (aslamnā) — for faith has not yet entered your hearts"* (Q 49:14). The founding text thus distinguishes handing oneself over from believing, and makes the first the **exterior** term: the threshold, not the heart.

This observation displaces a burning question. One cannot apostatise from a gesture; one can only apostatise from a membership. So long as the word designates a handing over of the self, it offers no juridical purchase: there exists no register in which an interior withdrawal could be inscribed. It is by hardening the noun of action into a noun of institution — the handing over becoming adherence, adherence becoming membership, membership becoming status — that apostasy is made so much as thinkable. The slide is of the same order as the one described elsewhere on this site, which leads from the plural *umma* of Medina to the imperial *dhimma*: a word of relation congealing into a word of status.

And one must have the honesty to turn this blade against the more sympathetic camp. Against violent instrumentalisations, it is tempting to reply that etymology does not lie and that islam originally means peace and justice. Etymology does not lie, indeed. But neither does it decide. It opens a field of possibilities, within which history, relations of force, and the needs of institutions select one branch. To believe that the true meaning is the gentle meaning is still to seek a purity of origins — that is, the very gesture historical analysis must undo. It is to seek, precisely, an intact letter.

## 14. Conclusion — The fidelity of a damaged letter {#s14}

The root is more honest than its defenders and its accusers alike. It says three simple, hard things.

That peace is a settled account: *shillem* and *shalom* are the same verb seen from the two sides of the ledger, and the communion sacrifice is what remains once the debt has been paid in bodies.

That completion is paid for: *shalem* names both the stone iron has not touched and the fantasy of an origin without admixture — and "complete" means "with no remainder," which always obliges one to do something with the remainder.

That handing over can save or betray according to the hands that receive: *ashlem*, *masar*, *tradere*, *aslama* — a single gesture, two directions, and no grammar to separate them. Only the addressee decides.

There remains the scribe. For centuries he has conscientiously broken a letter at the Phinehas verse. He does not comment, he does not correct, he does not erase this covenant of peace granted for a killing. He transmits it — and, in the very gesture of transmitting it, he flags its painful point. He makes the word illegible just enough that one stops.

Perhaps that is the only fidelity worth the name: a deliberately damaged letter, which refuses to be intact in order to remain true.

## 15. Threads left open {#s15}

A few questions this article leaves open.

Is the peace promised by the prophets the end of time or the end of accounts? Does Isaiah's eschatological *shalom* — the wolf with the lamb, swords beaten into ploughshares — describe a new state, or only a ledger finally closed? The difference is not slight: in the first case peace is a creation, in the second it is merely a balance, and nothing prevents a new account from opening the next day.

Is the ban on iron at the altar a critique of sacrifice by sacrifice itself? A ritual arrangement that requires the instrument of killing not to touch the site of ritual killing may be saying, in its own language, something the prophets will say in plain speech: "I desire mercy and not sacrifice."

And if the word that says "transmit" also says "betray," what becomes of the idea of a perfectly faithful transmission? Could a tradition that knew this about itself — that knew transmitting is always handing over into other hands — still be fundamentalist? The broken vav suggests not: it inscribes into the transmitted text the admission that transmission has a cost.

- **Psalm 122:6-7** — *shaʾalu shlom Yerushalayim*; **Bereshit Rabbah 56:10** — Abraham, Shem, and the composition of the name; **Luke 19:41-44** — the tears over the city and "the things that make for peace." On the pre-Israelite attestations of the name: the Egyptian execration texts and the Amarna letters (*Urusalim*); on the deity Shalim, the Ugaritic text **KTU 1.23**.
- **Mark 6:17-29** and **Matthew 14:3-12** — the unnamed daughter of Herodias and Herod's oath; **Mark 15:40** and **16:1** — Salome at Golgotha and at the tomb; **Flavius Josephus**, *Jewish Antiquities* XVIII, 136 — the name of Herodias's daughter.

- **Flavius Josephus**, *Jewish War* I, 107-119 and *Jewish Antiquities* XIII, 405-432 — the reign of Salome Alexandra; **Babylonian Talmud, *Taʿanit* 23a** — the prosperity of her reign; **Emil Schürer**, *The History of the Jewish People in the Age of Jesus Christ*, rev. ed. *A note on method: the reconstruction of the root and its forms belongs to historical linguistics and is solidly attested. The readings of the broken vav, by contrast, are interpretations — ancient ones, but later than the graphic fact, which the Masorah transmits without explaining. To distinguish them is not to disqualify them: it is to understand what each of them does.*
