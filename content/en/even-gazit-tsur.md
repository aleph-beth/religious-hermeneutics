## 1. The silent building site {#s1}

There is a sentence in the first book of Kings that one reads too quickly. Solomon's Temple is under construction — a colossal site, cedars imported from Lebanon, Phoenician workmen, levies of tens of thousands of men. And the text specifies: "The House, when it was built, was built of stone finished at the quarry; neither hammer nor axe, no tool of iron was heard in the House while it was being built" (1 Kings 6:7).

The greatest building site in the history of Israel is therefore a silent one. Not because the stones were not dressed — they were, and expertly — but because the dressing took place elsewhere. The noise of the tool was expelled beyond the sacred perimeter.

This oddity is not isolated. It belongs to a whole set of rules, words, and narratives that distinguish three things which English readily confuses under the single word "stone": the stone one shapes, the stone one refuses to shape, and the rock, which cannot be shaped at all. Three regimes, three Hebrew vocabularies, three theologies.

This article follows that path in four movements: first the word *even*, the handleable stone, and what the rabbis were able to read in it; then the strange ban on hewing, and the midrashim that attempt to explain it; then the dossier, biblical and evangelical, of the stones that hear, testify, and end by crying out; and finally *tsur*, the rock, which is no longer a material but a name of God — and which, against all expectation, begins to give birth.

---

## 2. *Even*: the stone that passes from hand to hand {#s2}

Hebrew **אֶבֶן** (*even*) designates stone as an available object. It is a very ancient word, common to the whole Semitic family — the Akkadian of Mesopotamia already said *abnu* — and it carries, originally, no religious charge whatsoever. An *even* is what one picks up, what one throws, what one piles, what one makes a wall out of.

It is also, very concretely, a weight. In the biblical world one weighs with calibrated pebbles kept in a pouch, and Deuteronomy forbids owning two sets of them: "You shall not have in your bag *even* and *even*, a large one and a small one" (Deut 25:13). The word therefore designates simultaneously the material of construction and the instrument of measure — that is to say, the exact point at which fraud becomes possible. The stone of *even* is caught up in the human economy, with everything that implies of the convenient and the corruptible.

A grammatical detail that will matter: *even* is a **feminine** word, despite its plural in *-im*, which misleads beginners. One says *avanim shlemot*, "whole stones," with feminine agreement.

### The son inside the stone

Ancient readers did not practise historical linguistics; they practised listening. And to the ear, *even* (א־ב־ן) contains two words: **אָב** (*av*), the father, and **בֵּן** (*ben*), the son. The midrashic tradition made ample use of this decomposition: the stone is the place where father and son hold together, which makes it the very image of transmission.

Is this an etymology? No, and it must be said plainly: historically, *even* is not formed from *av* and *ben*. But the procedure is neither an accident nor a naïveté. The biblical text itself plays on these sonic proximities. Psalm 118 rhymes the stone with the builders — "the *even* which the *bonim* (builders) rejected has become the head of the corner" (Ps 118:22) — and the verb בנה (*banah*), to build, sounds like *ben*, the son. To build and to beget share, in Hebrew, the same acoustic neighbourhood. The midrash merely carries to its conclusion a possibility already deposited in the language.

### The stone that gives birth

This kinship between stone and childbirth is not, moreover, merely a matter of sound. The dual **אָבְנַיִם** (*ovnayim*), "the two stones," designates in biblical Hebrew the birthing stool — the two blocks upon which the woman in labour braced herself. Pharaoh orders the Hebrew midwives to watch the births "upon the *ovnayim*" (Exodus 1:16). The same word, in Jeremiah, designates the potter's wheel: the prophet goes down to the potter's house and finds him "doing his work upon the *ovnayim*" (Jer 18:3).

A feminine word naming the machine for bringing to birth, and one that the rabbis break down into father and son: the commonplace stone, the most manipulable of materials, turns out in Hebrew to be the privileged site where generation is spoken of.

## 3. The ban on hewing: *gazit* against *shlemah* {#s3}

Let us turn to what the Bible forbids. Two texts, brief and categorical.

Exodus 20:25: "If you make me an altar of stones, you shall not build them of hewn stones (*gazit*); for you have lifted your chisel upon it, and you have profaned it."

Deuteronomy 27:5-6: "There you shall build an altar to YHWH your God, an altar of stones; you shall not bring iron upon them. It is of whole stones (*avanim shlemot*) that you shall build the altar."

The dividing line, then, does not run between stone and rock, but within stone itself: **גָּזִית** (*gazit*), cut stone, from the root גזז which also gives the shearing of sheep — the idea of cutting away — against **שְׁלֵמָה** (*shlemah*), the whole stone, complete, intact. The same root as *shalom*: that which is *shalem* is that to which nothing is lacking.

The Mishnah will harden the rule further. For the altar, the stones are drawn from the virgin soil of the valley of Beit Kerem, digging down to unworked rock, and if iron touches a stone, it is disqualified (tractate *Middot* 3:4). Contact suffices; it is not even necessary that the stone should have been cut into.

### The midrash of peace

Why? The most celebrated answer is given by the Mekhilta of Rabbi Ishmael, an early commentary on Exodus, in the name of Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar. It speaks neither of idolatry nor of ritual purity, but of contradictory functions: iron was created to shorten the days of man — it is the metal of the sword — and the altar to lengthen them. It is therefore not right that what shortens should be lifted upon what lengthens.

Then comes the *a fortiori* argument, which is the summit of the passage: the stones of the altar do not see, do not hear, do not speak; and yet, because they establish peace between Israel and its Father in heaven, the Torah says not to lift iron upon them. How much more so the one who establishes peace between a man and his family, between one town and another, between two nations — upon that one, no punishment shall come.

This reasoning deserves to be pondered, because it inverts what one expects of a religious text. The point of arrival is not worship: it is conduct among human beings. The rule about pebbles counts only for what it allows one to deduce about the living.

### How Solomon managed it

A practical problem remained, and the rabbis did not dodge it: if iron is proscribed, how could Solomon have dressed the monumental blocks of the Temple?

The answer is one of the most astonishing midrashim in the Talmud (tractates *Gittin* 68 and *Sotah* 48). There existed a creature the size of a barleycorn, the **shamir**, capable of splitting stone by mere contact — created, it is said, at the twilight of the sixth day of Creation, among those objects made *in extremis* before the entry of the Sabbath. Solomon had to go and fetch it at the end of an extravagant expedition involving Ashmedai, king of the demons. The shamir could not be carried in a metal vessel, which it would have pulverised: it was wrapped in wool, inside a lead tube filled with barley bran.

Beneath the marvellous, the coherence is perfect. The narrative does not say that Solomon circumvented the rule: it says that the Temple required a tool that would not be a tool, a cutting edge that would not be iron, an instrument drawn from the world that preceded human beings. Where one ought to have found a technique, the tradition installs a creature.

### An architecture that draws back from itself

Let us gather the pieces. At the periphery of the sanctuary, the stone is perfectly dressed, and by foreign craftsmen: Hiram of Tyre supplies workmen and cedars. Inside the House, dressing is permitted but its noise is banished. Upon the altar, dressing is forbidden. And at the centre of the Holy of Holies, the Mishnah (*Yoma* 5:2) reports that after the disappearance of the Ark there remained only a stone protruding three fingerbreadths above the floor, "from the days of the first prophets."

The sanctuary is therefore a machine for making human labour decrease as one advances towards its heart. This is the exact inverse of what any prestige architecture does, where ornament concentrates at the centre. Here value follows the inverse of labour. The holiest point is the least fabricated point — and very nearly the least visible.

In other words: holiness is not in the matter. It is the same stone everywhere. It is in the **prohibition** that withdraws that matter from the hand. The sacred, here, is a negative modality: the place where one ceases to produce.

## 4. The stones that cry out {#s4}

The Mekhilta's reasoning rested on a premise: the stones of the altar "do not see, do not hear, do not speak." It was this muteness that made the argument effective — if iron is spared to what feels nothing, how much more so to what feels.

Yet the Bible, elsewhere, says exactly the opposite. There exists a whole dossier of stones that hear, that testify, and that cry out. And it is this dossier that the Gospels will take up.

### The stone that has heard

At the end of the book of Joshua, the covenant is concluded at Shechem, and Joshua sets up a stone under the oak of the sanctuary. His formula is striking: "Behold, this stone shall be a witness against us, **for it has heard** all the words that YHWH has spoken to us" (Josh 24:27). The stone is not a memorandum: it is a witness in full standing, because it was there and it listened.

The procedure is ancient and has nothing metaphorical about it in its juridical use. When Jacob and Laban conclude their pact, they pile up a heap of stones that Jacob names *Galed*, "the heap of testimony," and the heap is summoned as an interested party: "this heap is witness between me and you" (Gen 31:48). In a world without archives, the mineral serves as memory — and a memory, by definition, may one day speak.

### The stone that cries out from the wall

It speaks in Habakkuk, and under very precise conditions. The prophet addresses a series of curses to the one who enriches himself by plunder, and he announces: "For the stone shall cry out from the wall, and the beam from the woodwork shall answer it" (Hab 2:11). The following verse explains everything: "Woe to him who builds a town with blood, and founds a city upon injustice" (Hab 2:12).

The stones therefore cry out against the building of which they form part, because they know where they come from and what they cost. The cry of the material denounces the mode of extraction. And the verb employed, זעק (*za'aq*), is the very verb of Abel's blood rising from the ground: "the voice of your brother's blood **cries out** to me from the earth" (Gen 4:10). The mineral cry belongs to the family of the victim's cries.

Here one measures what is at stake behind the ban on iron. A hewn stone is not merely a transformed stone: it is a stone that has undergone something, and that keeps the trace. An altar of whole stones is an altar that has no one to testify against.

### Luke 19: the cry and the ruin

Jesus' saying is the point of arrival of this chain. At the entry into Jerusalem, some Pharisees ask him to silence his disciples; he answers: "I tell you, if they keep silent, the stones will cry out" (Luke 19:40).

What is rarely quoted is the immediate sequel. A few verses further on, in the same movement, he weeps over the city and announces: "they will not leave in you one stone upon another" (Luke 19:44). The same block of text therefore contains both: the stones that cry out and the stones that fall. This is no accident of composition. Habakkuk's formula was a curse addressed to an unjust building; Luke puts it back into service against the Temple. The stones cry out first, and that cry is the announcement of their own unsealing.

The antithesis with the Mekhilta is perfect, and it is worth formulating sharply, for the two traditions work the same material in opposite directions. For the rabbis, the stone is protected **because** it is mute. For Luke, it takes the floor **because** human beings keep silent. In the one case the silence of the stone founds an ethics of restraint; in the other, the speech of the stone is the last recourse when human speech is prevented.

### Stones and sons: the Aramaic pun

There remains the passage most interesting for our purpose, because it brings back into the middle of the Gospel the paronomasia we encountered in the midrash. John the Baptist apostrophises those who lay claim to their ancestry: "Do not say within yourselves, we have Abraham for our father; for I tell you that **from these stones** God is able to raise up **sons** to Abraham" (Luke 3:8; Matt 3:9).

In Greek, the sentence is merely paradoxical. In Aramaic, the likely language of the saying, it is a pun: stones are *abnayya* (אבניא), sons are *benayya* (בניא). A single sound separates the stone from the son — exactly the relation that the midrashim heard between *even* and *ben*. The wordplay therefore belongs neither to rabbinic Judaism nor to nascent Christianity as such: it circulates in a common language, and two traditions draw divergent conclusions from the same acoustic effect.

There is more. This sentence answers point by point to Isaiah 51, which we shall read in a moment: "look to the rock from which you were hewn… look to Abraham your father." Isaiah expressed filiation as an extraction within rock; John the Baptist turns the image around and asserts that the quarry is still open, that God can draw new sons from it, and that genealogy guarantees nothing. The descent from Abraham ceases to be an inheritance and becomes an operation again.

### The stone not cut by hands

One last echo deserves mention, because it closes the theme of iron. In the book of Daniel, Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a statue made of gold, silver, bronze, iron, and clay. A stone detaches itself from the mountain **"without the aid of any hand"** (Dan 2:34), strikes the statue at the feet, pulverises the metals — then grows until it becomes a mountain filling the whole earth.

The unworked stone destroys the empire of metals, and becomes bedrock again. The New Testament knows this text and combines it with that of the psalm: after quoting "the stone which the builders rejected," Luke adds that whoever falls upon that stone will be broken, and that the one upon whom it falls will be crushed (Luke 20:17-18). The cornerstone, Isaiah's stone of stumbling, and Daniel's uncut stone have fused into a single image.

## 5. *Tsur*: the rock, which is not a material {#s5}

With **צוּר** (*tsur*), we change category. The *tsur* is not a larger stone: it is the rock mass in place, that which has never been detached from anything. One can extract a stone from it; one cannot move it. Alongside it, Hebrew has **סֶלַע** (*sela*), the cliff, the escarpment — the word gave in Greek *Petra*, the Nabataean city — and **חַלָּמִישׁ** (*ḥallamish*), flint, the hardest of rocks.

Now it is this vocabulary, and not that of stone, that becomes a name of God. The great poem of Deuteronomy, the song of Moses, hammers it out: "The Rock, his work is perfect" (Deut 32:4), and then, concerning foreign gods: "their rock is not as our Rock" (Deut 32:31).

Why this choice? Precisely because the rock is inanimate and unmade. To name God "Rock" is to say the unshakeable, the immutable, that which is not negotiable — and it is to refuse, in passing, every human figure. The mineral metaphor works as an antidote to anthropomorphism: it states a necessity, not a person.

A grammatical detail, again: contrary to what the imagination suggests, **the rock is masculine in Hebrew, and the stone is feminine**. The unbegotten massif is a masculine; the handleable object, a feminine. In Arabic the distribution is exactly reversed: the *ṣakhra* (صخرة), the crag, is feminine, and the *ḥajar* (حجر), the stone, masculine. Proof, if it were needed, that grammatical gender is not a truth about things.

### The rock that gives birth

And yet the biblical poets take their own grammar against the grain. A few verses further on in the same song: "The Rock that begot you, you neglected; you forgot the God who bore you in labour" (Deut 32:18). The second verb, מְחֹלְלֶךָ, comes from a root that describes the contractions of childbirth. The grammatical masculine receives the verb of the woman in labour.

Isaiah goes further still, and makes the procedure explicit: "Look to the rock from which you were hewn, and to the hollow of the quarry from which you were dug; look to Abraham your father, and to Sarah who bore you" (Isa 51:1-2). The parallelism distributes the roles without ambiguity: the rock is the father; **the hollow** is the mother. And the word employed for that hollow, מַקֶּבֶת (*maqqevet*), comes from the root נקב, to pierce — the same root that gives נְקֵבָה (*neqevah*), the ordinary word for "woman, female."

This is the most interesting reversal in the whole dossier. The feminine, in this language, is neither the stone nor the rock: it is **the void hollowed out within the rock**. What is missing from the mass. One then understands why Semitic holy places are so often cavities: the cave of Machpelah where the patriarchs are buried, the cleft of the rock in which Moses is placed to see the glory pass by (Ex 33:22), the cave beneath the rock of Jerusalem.

### The rock one had to strike, and then strike no longer

The rock, finally, is what gives water. Two nearly twin narratives, and it is their difference that counts. At Horeb, God orders Moses to strike the rock, and water gushes forth (Ex 17:6). Much later, at Meribah, the order changes: "you shall **speak** to the rock" (Num 20:8). Moses strikes — twice — and the water comes all the same, but he will not see the Promised Land.

The fault is variously explained by the commentators, and the ambiguity of the text is real. But one reading imposes itself at the level of the gesture: what was permitted at the beginning — force applied to rock — becomes at the end a transgression. The same prohibition as that of the altar, transposed from the chisel to the staff. There is an age in which one strikes the stone, and an age in which one must speak to it.

## 6. The sacred as a withdrawal of the hand {#s6}

Three words, three regimes. The stone one handles, which weighs and which builds, and in which the tradition hears the father and the son. The stone one refuses to hew, and whose integrity counts, according to the Mekhilta, as a lesson about peace among human beings. And finally the rock, which one does not shape at all, and which becomes for that very reason a name of God — a masculine rock that gives birth, and whose hollow is feminine.

And, running through all three, a voice. Jacob's heap testifies, Joshua's stone has heard, Habakkuk's cries out from the top of a wall built by violence, and Luke's take the floor at the very moment they are told they are going to fall. One tradition protects the stone because it is mute; the other gives it speech because human beings keep silent. Both presuppose the same thing: that the material remembers what was done to it.

What runs from one end to the other is a single gesture: **to withdraw the hand**. Not to despise technique — the Temple is saturated with it, and Solomon mobilised the best craftsmen of the Levant — but to halt it at a threshold. The sacred, in this tradition, is defined neither by a particular substance nor by a supplement of ornament: it is defined by a point at which one ceases to produce. An architecture that culminates in an outcrop of raw rock, and beneath that outcrop, according to the Talmud (*Sukkah* 53), the waters of the abyss that must be held back.

A seventeenth-century reader, Spinoza, would doubtless have recognised something here: the distinction between what nature produces and what produces nature. The centre of the sanctuary is not a thing more divine than others. It is the place where one encounters the given, and not the made.

## 7. Threads left open {#s7}

A few questions this article leaves open, and that are worth the detour.

Is the ban on iron at the altar to be connected to a broader biblical mistrust of metallurgy? The first city is founded by Cain, and it is a descendant of Cain, Tubal-Cain, who forges bronze and iron. The suspicion would then be less ritual than political: metal is the weapon.

Did the arrangement survive the destruction of the Temple? The Dome of the Rock, in Jerusalem, is a sumptuously decorated octagon built around a bare outcrop that it does not touch. The Kaʿba in Mecca inverts the scheme: a masonry cube, with an unworked stone set into a corner, on the outside, at hand height. The same grammar, different places granted to the unmade.

And on the Christian side, once the stones have begun to cry out, they never stop moving: the first epistle of Peter calls the faithful "living stones" (1 Pet 2:5), a man receives as his name the Aramaic *Kepha*, "the rock" — which Greek must masculinise into Πέτρος because one does not name a man with a feminine — and the resurrection narrative places at its centre a tomb whose stone has been **rolled away** from the centre, which remains empty. The series is coherent: the stone speaks, then lives, then becomes someone, then steps aside.

Finally, a question for those curious about comparative etymology: if the biblical feminine lodges in the hollow rather than in the mass, should the whole set of sacred caves of the ancient Near East be reread as a coherent arrangement, or is it a convergence without intention?

## 8. Bibliographical references {#s8}

- **1 Kings 6:7** — the silent building site of the Temple; **Exodus 20:25** and **Deuteronomy 27:5-6** — the ban on iron and on hewn stones at the altar.
- **Mishnah**, tractate *Middot* 3:4 (the extraction of the altar stones and disqualification by contact with iron) and tractate *Yoma* 5:2 (the *even shetiyah*, the stone protruding three fingerbreadths in the Holy of Holies).
- **Mekhilta of Rabbi Ishmael**, on Exodus 20:25, in the name of Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar — iron that shortens, the altar that lengthens, and the *a fortiori* argument concerning peace among human beings.
- **Babylonian Talmud**, *Gittin* 68 and *Sotah* 48 — the *shamir*, its creation at the twilight of the sixth day, the expedition to Ashmedai, and its transport in lead and barley bran; *Sukkah* 53 — the waters of the abyss beneath the foundation stone.
- **Genesis 31:48** (*Galed*, the witnessing heap); **Joshua 24:27** (the stone that has heard); **Habakkuk 2:11-12** (the stone that cries out from the wall); **Genesis 4:10** (the blood that cries from the ground) — the dossier of the witness-stone.
- **Luke 3:8** / **Matthew 3:9** (the stones and the sons of Abraham); **Luke 19:40, 44** (the cry and the ruin); **Luke 20:17-18** and **Psalm 118:22** (the stone rejected by the builders); **Daniel 2:34** (the stone cut without hands); **1 Peter 2:5** (the living stones).
- **Deuteronomy 32:4, 18, 31** — the song of Moses: the perfect Rock, the Rock that bore in labour; **Isaiah 51:1-2** — the rock and the hollow of the quarry, Abraham and Sarah.
- **Exodus 1:16** and **Jeremiah 18:3** — the *ovnayim*, birthing stool and potter's wheel; **Exodus 17:6** and **Numbers 20:8** — striking the rock, then speaking to it; **Exodus 33:22** — the cleft of the rock.
- **Spinoza**, *Ethics*, Part I — the distinction between *natura naturans* and *natura naturata*, the background of the conclusion.

*Note on method: the sonic associations exploited by the midrashim (av + ben within even, for example) are not etymologies in the sense of historical linguistics. They belong to another practice of language, in which the resemblance of sounds is taken as an index of meaning. To distinguish them from real filiations is not to disqualify them: it is to understand what they do.*
