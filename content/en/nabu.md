## 1. A root as guiding thread {#s1}

In the great pantheon of Babylon, Nabu does not hold the first rank. He has neither the cosmic sovereignty of Marduk, his father, nor the antiquity of Enlil or Anu. And yet it is around him that one of the most continuous stories of the entire ancient Near East is woven: the history of writing as an instrument of power, memory, and judgment. For Nabu is the god of scribes. He holds the reed stylus and the clay tablet, and upon that tablet, once a year, he inscribes the destiny of men and of kings.

The god's name rests on a common Semitic root, *n-b-ʾ*, meaning "to call, to name, to proclaim." From this same root will issue, centuries apart and in several languages, the word for **prophet** — Hebrew *nāvîʾ*, Aramaic *nəvîyā*, Arabic *nabī* — and the name of **Nebuchadnezzar**, the most famous of Babylon's kings. The root therefore links, in a single gesture, *the one who proclaims* (the prophet, spokesman of God) and *the one who inscribes* (Nabu, the divine scribe). To follow Nabu is to follow this thread: how humanity, by learning to write in order to count sacks of barley, equipped itself with the instrument that would end up counting souls.

This article proposes to unwind that thread from one end to the other. From the scribe-god to the prophetic root; from the tower of Babel as a symbol of technology to the accounting origin of writing; and from that primitive bookkeeping to the religious imagination of Judgment, where salvation is expressed, in the Quran as in the Bible, in the vocabulary of a ledger kept up to date.

---

## 2. The god Nabu {#s2}

Nabu (Akkadian *Nabû*, sometimes rendered in Sumerian by local theonyms) is the god of writing, of scribes, of wisdom, and of vegetation. Son of Marduk, the great city-god of Babylon, and of the goddess Zarpanitu, he has as wife Tashmetu, "she who hears favorably." His principal sanctuary is the **Ezida**, the temple of **Borsippa**, a city neighboring Babylon: the geographical proximity doubles the divine filiation, the son residing within reach of the father. His popularity grows sharply in the first millennium, in the Neo-Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian periods, to the point that his cult rivals that of Marduk himself; the kings of the Chaldean dynasty — among them Nebuchadnezzar II and Nabonidus — carry his name within their own or multiply their homages to him.

His attributes declare his function. He is represented by the **stylus** (the reed pen) set upon a clay tablet, or by the engraved wedge, the elementary sign of cuneiform writing. During the great Babylonian New Year festival, the *Akitu*, Nabu plays a decisive role: he leaves Borsippa to join Marduk in Babylon, and it is within this ritual frame that the **destiny of the year** is fixed. Mesopotamian mythology indeed knows the **Tablet of Destinies** (*ṭuppi šīmāti*), an object of absolute power: whoever holds it governs the order of the world. Marduk wins it in the *Enūma eliš*, the poem of creation; Nabu, as scribe of the gods, is the one who holds its pen. To inscribe destiny and to proclaim the divine will are one and the same operation: the scribe-god is also the spokesman of the council of the gods.

It is this double face — **inscriber** and **proclaimer** — that makes Nabu the ideal figure for thinking the relation between writing and speech. He does not merely record: what he records *comes to pass*. With him, writing is not the shadow of speech; it is performative, it fixes the real. This idea will run, as we shall see, through all the traditions that inherit from him.

## 3. The root *n-b-ʾ*: to call, to name, to proclaim {#s3}

The god's name is rooted in the Akkadian verb **nabû(m)**, one of the oldest and best-attested in the language, from the third millennium onward. Its meaning covers a tight and powerful field: "to call, to name, to designate, to proclaim, to decree." One *nabû*s a name to a child, a destiny to a man, a decree to a kingdom. To name and to decree belong to the same act: in a culture where the name carries essence, to call is to bring into being. The theonym **Nabû** can then be read in two ways, and this indecision is fertile: "the Proclaimer" (he who announces the word of the gods) or "the Called / the Designated" (he whom the gods have instituted). Both readings coexist in the documentation and structure the entire afterlife of the root.

For this root *n-b-ʾ* did not remain Babylonian. It is common to Semitic, and in the Northwest and Arabic families it produced the vocabulary of **prophecy**. The reconstructed form is a noun of the *qatīl* pattern (Arabic *faʿīl*), namely *\*nabiʾ-*. This pattern deserves attention: in Semitic, it frequently serves to form participles with a **passive** value (Hebrew *māšîaḥ* "anointed," *ʾāsîr* "captive"). This is the central morphological argument of a great hypothesis, defended notably by William F. Albright: the prophet would be, etymologically, *the one who has been called*, designated by the divinity. But the verbal sense of the root — "to proclaim" — pulls toward the **active**: the prophet would be *the one who announces*. The whole history of the word oscillates between these two poles, and this oscillation is not a defect: it expresses the very structure of the prophetic function. The *nabī* is the one who, *because he has been called*, becomes the one *who proclaims*.

Its diffusion is wide. Hebrew gives **נָבִיא** *nāvîʾ* (plural *nəvîʾîm*, feminine *nəvîʾāh* for Miriam, Deborah, Huldah). Notably, Hebrew has no simple verb "to prophesy": the verb is denominative, derived from the noun, and appears only in the *niphal* (*nibbāʾ*) and *hitpaʿel* (*hitnabbēʾ*) forms — which confirms that the noun comes first. Aramaic and Syriac give *nəvîyā* / *nḇiyā*, by which the term reaches the Christian East. Arabic, finally, gives **نبي** *nabī*, whose attachment the grammarians will debate: to *nabaʾa* "to announce" (root *n-b-ʾ*, with *hamza*, consistent with common Semitic) or to *nabā* "to rise" (a weak root) — the first etymology prevails, confirming the unity of the family. The god of Babylon and the prophet of Israel and Arabia thus share one and the same semantic cell: all are men — or gods — of the **transmitted word**.

## 4. The nabī: the figure of the prophet {#s4}

That the root *n-b-ʾ* should designate the prophet is no inconsequential etymological accident: it is the key to what a prophet *is* in the Semitic traditions. The *nabī* is not first a diviner who predicts the future, nor a sage who reasons. He is a **spokesman** — the mouth through which a word that is not his own makes itself heard. The function matches the root exactly: *called* by God, he *proclaims* in His name.

The Hebrew Bible gives the clearest definition, and it does so in a scene that lays the word bare. When Moses objects that he is not a ready speaker, God answers: "Aaron, your brother… shall be your mouth, and you shall be to him as God" (Ex 4:16). And further, more strikingly still: "See, I make you a god to Pharaoh, and Aaron your brother shall be your **nabī**" (Ex 7:1). The text here hands over, in plain terms, its own definition of the prophet: the *nabī* is to the one who sends him what Aaron is to Moses — his **mouth**. The prophet of Israel does not invent his message; he *transmits* it. As Aaron speaks for Moses, the prophet speaks for God. The structure of the root — the one who, *because he is called*, *proclaims* — is here dramatized in the narrative itself.

This function of transmission distinguishes the *nabī* from older figures. Hebrew knew the *roʾeh*, the "**seer**," and the *ḥozeh*, the "**visionary**" — men of sight, who *saw* the invisible. A crucial verse records the shift: "Formerly in Israel, when a man went to inquire of God, he said, Come, let us go to the seer (*roʾeh*); for he who is now called a prophet (*nabī*) was formerly called a seer" (1 Sam 9:9). The change of vocabulary marks a change of emphasis: from **vision** to **word**, from the eye that perceives to the mouth that proclaims. Israelite prophecy defines itself more and more as a matter of *transmitted speech*, not of divination — which sharply separates it from the Mesopotamian diviner, the *bārû*, who *reads* signs (the liver of victims, the flight of birds, the configuration of the stars). The *nabī* does not decipher the world like a hidden text; he **says again** a word received. And here too Nabu is not far off: the scribe-god is likewise the spokesman of the council of the gods, the one who states abroad the decision taken within.

Islam inherits this figure and refines it with a decisive distinction: that of the *nabī* (prophet) and the *rasūl* (**messenger**, "the sent one"). Every *rasūl* is a *nabī*, but not the reverse: the prophet receives revelation, whereas the messenger is *sent* bearing a Law or a Book to a given community. Tradition thus counts a multitude of prophets and a small number of messengers bearing Scripture (Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, Muhammad). And it is from the prophetic root, not from that of the messenger, that the title closing the series is formed: Muhammad is called **khātam al-nabiyyīn**, the "**Seal of the Prophets**" (Quran 33:40). The word is striking for our purpose: the *seal* is an instrument of writing, the imprint that *closes* and *authenticates* a document. The chain of God's spokesmen therefore ends on a scribal metaphor — that of the seal that shuts the register. The prophet and the scribe, one last time, are one.

Thus the notion of prophet is held entire within the double value of the root from which the name of Nabu springs. To be a prophet is to have been *called* (the passive pole) in order to *proclaim* (the active pole) a word that comes from elsewhere. The *nabī* is the man of the transmitted word as Nabu is the god of the inscribed word: at the two ends of the root *n-b-ʾ*, the same conviction that the word which carries authority is not invented — it is received, and said again.

## 5. The name of the king: Nebuchadnezzar, writing made power {#s5}

The most famous bearer of the root is not a prophet but a king. **Nebuchadnezzar** transcribes the Akkadian **Nabû-kudurri-uṣur**, which breaks down into an invocation: "O Nabû, protect my *kudurru*." The meaning of the word *kudurru* has been debated, and this debate is itself instructive. Recent scholarship favors the reading "heir, firstborn": the king asks the god to protect his offspring, his line. But an older reading, long dominant, understood *kudurru* as the **boundary stone**, the stone stele that marks a property limit and on which one **engraves** the divine decree guaranteeing that limit.

This ambivalence is precious for our purpose. Whether *kudurru* designates the heir or the boundary stone, the king's name remains wholly placed under the sign of **inscription** and **fixing**. If it is the heir, the king entrusts his line to the god who holds the tablet of destinies — the very one who can *write* the future of a dynasty. If it is the boundary stone, then the name invokes the scribe-god to guard a **written object**, an engraved stone that states the law. In both cases, royal power leans on the power of writing. The most powerful king of Babylon carries, engraved in his very name, the name of the god who engraves. Political sovereignty presents itself as an extension of scribal sovereignty: to reign is to make limits, lines, and decrees hold — that is, to write and to enforce what is written.

The royal name and the prophetic name thus meet under the patronage of the same god. *Nabī* and *Nabû-kudurri-uṣur* are two ways of saying that the word that carries authority — that of the prophet, that of the king — passes through Nabu, through the gesture of proclaiming and inscribing.

## 6. Babel: the gate of the god turned into confusion {#s6}

Babylon was called, in Akkadian, **Bāb-ili(m)**: "the Gate of the god" (or "of the gods"). The name is a self-glorification; it makes the city the threshold by which the divine enters the world, the cosmic center. Now the biblical tradition performs on this name a reversal of great symbolic violence. The story of the tower, in Genesis 11, ties **בָּבֶל** *Bāvel* to the verb **בָּלַל** *bālal*, "to mix up, to confuse, to mingle": "Therefore it was called Babel, because there the Lord **confused** the language of all the earth" (Gen 11:9). The capital that proclaimed itself the Gate of the god becomes, on Israel's lips, the place of **babble**, of undone language. The procedure is typically midrashic: one does not refute an empire by arms, one **deconstructs its name** through a pun. Babylonian prestige is dissolved in a play of sounds.

The detail of the narrative is explicitly **technical**, and this is what authorizes reading Babel as a symbol of technology. "Come, let us make bricks, and burn them thoroughly… And they had brick for stone, and bitumen for mortar" (Gen 11:3). The **fired brick** is the standardized unit par excellence: a reproducible, calibrated, interchangeable element — the ancient equivalent of the industrial component. Each brick can be read as a brick of knowledge, and the tower as the first great **collective project** founded on coordination, technique, and a common language. It is precisely this unity of the code that makes the project possible: "they all have one language," the text frets, "and now nothing they propose to do will be impossible for them" (Gen 11:6).

The divine response is not the thunderbolt but **linguistic fragmentation**. What is struck is the code itself: the unity of language shatters into a multiplicity of mutually opaque idioms. The narrative therefore states, in a single movement, both the promise and the vertigo of science. The promise: a shared code allows endless building. The vertigo: that same power, left to itself and without measure, calls forth its own dispersion. George Steiner made *Babel* the very name of the human condition after the loss of the single language: we think and translate only from within that original confusion. The technology that raises the tower is also the one that, grown excessive, scatters. And it is not indifferent that the instrument of the fall should be **language** — that is, the very material of writing, the domain of Nabu.

## 7. Writing born of accounting {#s7}

Here is the heart of the inquiry, and its most firmly established point in archaeology: writing was born neither of prayer, nor of poem, nor of law. It was born of **accounting**. Mesopotamian cuneiform, the oldest fully developed writing system, sinks its roots in the administrative management of goods.

Before the inscribed tablets, we know the *calculi*: small **clay tokens** of various shapes, used from the Neolithic onward to record quantities — heads of cattle, measures of grain, jars of oil. The Assyriologist **Denise Schmandt-Besserat** showed how these tokens, first handled loosely, were then enclosed in hollow clay envelopes, the *bullae*, to seal a transaction or a debt. To know what the envelope contained without breaking it, people took to impressing the tokens on its surface, then to tracing the corresponding signs directly. The three-dimensional token became a two-dimensional sign: writing was born, and it was born to **count**.

The earliest tablets of archaic **Uruk**, around 3300 BCE, confirm this genealogy: the overwhelming majority of proto-cuneiform documents are **administrative and economic accounts** — ration registers, herd inventories, delivery records, accounts of temples and granaries. Lexical lists, hymns, and mythological narratives will come later. Writing answers first a simple and imperious question: *who owes what to whom?* It is the instrument of debt, of contract, and of stock before being that of memory and the sacred.

This fact changes how we view all that follows. When religion lays hold of writing, it inherits a tool **already shaped by the logic of the account**. The mental form it adopts — the register, the balance sheet, credit and debt — is not a metaphor laid on after the fact; it is the very structure of the tool. The scribe-god Nabu, who keeps the accounts of destiny, is the divine heir of the humble scribe-accountant of Uruk who kept the accounts of the granary. Between the two, the tool has not changed: only the object counted has shifted, from sacks of barley to the deeds of men.

## 8. Religion as a great ledger of accounts {#s8}

It is here that the chain closes. If writing was born of accounting, then religion, by adopting it, makes **accounting the very form of moral responsibility**. The individual becomes an ethical subject by becoming *inscribable* — literally *accountable*, the one whose acts are *counted* and kept in a register.

Islam gives this intuition its sharpest formulation. The word by which the Quran designates the Last Judgment is not first "punishment" but **ḥisāb**: the "**reckoning**," the "settling of accounts." The Day of Judgment is the *yawm al-ḥisāb*, the **Day on which accounts are rendered**, and God is there said to be *sarīʿ al-ḥisāb*, "swift in reckoning" (Quran 2:202; 3:19). Salvation is decided on a **balance**, the *mīzān*, where deeds are weighed: "As for him whose deeds weigh heavy in the balance, he will enjoy a pleasant life" (Quran 101:6-7). The whole eschatology is ordered to an operation of **weighing and balance-keeping**.

And who keeps the register? Two scribe-angels, the **Kirâm al-Kâtibîn**, "the noble scribes" (Quran 82:11). The Quran evokes them in two decisive passages: "Over you there are guardians, noble scribes who know what you do" (82:10-12), and "when the two recorders record, seated one on the right and one on the left… he utters not a word without an observer beside him ready to inscribe it" (50:17-18). Tradition names them **Raqîb** and **ʿAtîd**: the one on the right records good deeds; the one on the left, the bad. The hadith even specifies the "bookkeeping" of grace — a good deed is worth ten credits, a bad one a single debit, and the sinner who repents sees the line erased before inscription. One could not state more clearly that moral existence is a **keeping of books**.

This imagination is not peculiar to Islam; it extends a very long Near Eastern tradition. In Egypt, the Judgment of the dead is a **psychostasis**: the heart of the deceased is weighed on a balance against the feather of Maat, goddess of justice and truth, and it is **Thoth**, the ibis-headed scribe-god, the functional twin of Nabu, who **records the verdict**. Weighing and writing, here again, form a system. On the biblical and Jewish side, the imagination of the **book** is omnipresent: the **book of life** (*sefer ha-ḥayyim*) in which the righteous are inscribed; the **open books** of Daniel's heavenly tribunal ("the court sat in judgment, and the books were opened," Dan 7:10); the maxim of *Pirqei Avot* (2:1): "Know what is above you: an eye that sees, an ear that hears, and **all your deeds are written in a book**." The Jewish liturgy of Rosh Hashanah makes the New Year a great act of **registration**: one is "written and sealed" for the coming year — a striking echo of the Babylonian *Akitu* in which Nabu fixed the destiny of the year to come.

Everywhere, then, the same schema: the individual is constituted as a responsible subject because he is **written**. Responsibility is, in the proper sense, an *accountability* — the capacity to answer for what, of oneself, has been inscribed. The English phrase *to give an account* keeps the exact trace of it.

## 9. The reed pen and the tablet: "to be is to be written" {#s9}

The loop closes on the motif that opened our course: Nabu's **stylus** inscribing destinies. For this imagination of cosmic writing is found again, almost term for term, at the heart of Islam.

The Quran opens, at sura 68, with an oath upon the scribal instrument: **"Nūn. By the Pen and by what they inscribe"** (*Nūn wa-l-qalami wa-mā yasṭurūn*, 68:1). The sura bears the name *al-Qalam*, "the Pen." A famous hadith makes the **Pen the first creature**: "The first thing God created was the Pen; He said to it: *Write.* The Pen asked: *What shall I write?* He said: *Write what will be until the Day of Resurrection.*" Writing precedes the world; it is its condition. And what the Pen inscribes is preserved on the **Preserved Tablet**, *al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ* (Quran 85:22), the heavenly archetype of all writing, the uncreated matrix of the Quran itself and the register of all destinies.

The kinship with Babylon is striking. The Mesopotamian **Tablet of Destinies** (*ṭuppi šīmāti*), the Islamic **Preserved Tablet**, the biblical **book of life**: three names of one and the same imagination, in which the real is first a text, in which the future is already written, and in which to exist is to figure in a divine register. *To be is to be written.* The scribe-god Nabu, who inscribed on clay the fate of men, is the first great figure of this intuition that will be found again, transformed, all the way into the theology of the Pen and the Tablet.

And at this summit we recover the double value of the root *n-b-ʾ* from which we set out. The **proclaimer** — the prophet, *nabī*, who speaks the word of God — and the **inscriber** — Nabu, the scribe, who fixes it — are only the two faces of one and the same gesture. The revealed word must be proclaimed in order to be heard, and written in order to last. The prophet and the scribe stand at the two ends of one and the same axis: that of the transmission of the word that carries authority, from heaven down to the register where it will, one day, be read back against us.

## 10. Conclusion — The stylus, from the granary to the tribunal {#s10}

The chain holds from one end to the other, and it holds by a single tool. The root *n-b-ʾ* links the prophet who proclaims and the god who inscribes. Writing, born to **count** measures of barley in the granaries of Uruk, has become the instrument that **counts souls** in the registers of Judgment. And the responsibility of the individual — his capacity to *answer* for himself — is expressed, in Mesopotamia, in Egypt, in Israel, and in Islam, in the same vocabulary of the **debt kept current**, of the balance sheet, the scale, and the book.

The passage from the granary to the divine tribunal takes place without changing instrument. It is always the same **stylus** — the reed pen of Nabu, the feather of Thoth, the *qalam* of sura 68, the stylus of the Uruk scribe — that traces the signs. Only the inscribed object has shifted: from stock to soul, from economic debt to moral debt, from what man owes his neighbor to what he will answer for before his judge. In this sense, Nabu is no minor god. He is the discreet patron of an anthropological revolution that endures still: that of a humanity which constituted itself as a subject — accountable, responsible, judgeable — on the day it learned to write.
