## 1. Introduction: a city's name, a programme of destruction {#s1}

Can the name of a destroyed city contain, in hollow form, the programme of all destructions to come? The question seems excessive. It is no longer so if one admits that Semitic languages work on consonantal skeletons, and that the midrashic tradition reads these skeletons as intentional structures: not etymologies, but signals.

This is the method applied here. It differs from historical linguistics, which has its own rules and its own refusals. Each rapprochement will therefore be marked with its status: established etymology, wordplay internal to the text, or acknowledged midrashic reading.

The path follows six stages. The first part establishes what the name of Sodom permits and what it forbids. The second follows the blood that links Adam, Abel and the end of time. The third and fourth parts bring out a single fault, the *ga'on*, in two directions: the horizontal closure of Sodom and the vertical ascent of Babel. The fifth compares the two eschatologies, Jewish and Islamic, around an unexpected discriminator: money. The sixth reads the Dajjal as the inverse of Babel: the one who no longer ascends toward the gate of God, but claims to be God, and bears K-F-R on his forehead. The conclusion leaves three questions open.

## 2. Sodom, sod, dam: the root that is not one {#s2}

A preliminary observation is in order. *Sod*, the secret, is written סוֹד, with a waw. Sodom is written, in the Masoretic text, סְדֹם, without a waw: skeleton S-D-M. The two words are not related. Linguists connect Sodom to an uncertain root; among the hypotheses is the cultivated field. The biblical text itself plays on this meaning: Deuteronomy 32:32 associates the "vine of Sodom" with the *shadmot*, the fields of Gomorrah. Any derivation from *sod* to Sodom is therefore a retrospective construction.

Once this refusal is stated, the intentional reading becomes possible, provided one names its procedure. Isolating the segment D-M in Sedom is not morphology: s- is not a Hebrew prefix. It is a notariqon-type segmentation, one of the hermeneutic rules recognized by the tradition (the 32 *middot* attributed to R. Eliezer b. Yose ha-Gelili). Thus segmented, the segment opens onto three distinct roots:

| Reading | Root | Meaning | Textual anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silence | D-M-M (*demamah*, דְּמָמָה) | to be silent, to stand still | 1 Kings 19:12, *qol demamah daqqah*; Lev 10:3, Aaron is silent before the death of his sons |
| Image | D-M-H (*demout*, דְּמוּת) | to resemble, to imagine | Gen 1:26, "in our image" |
| Blood | D-M (*dam*, דָּם) | shed blood | Gen 4:10, the blood of Abel |

Three Sodoms emerge: the city of the hidden secret, the city of the lost image, the city of blood. The silence of Leviticus 10:3 deserves to be noted: it follows a death by fire, like that of Sodom.

The third branch is the best endowed with narrative context. Genesis 19 shows a city whose entire population besieges the visitors' house. Ezekiel 16:49, defining the fault of Sodom, chooses a vocabulary of satiety and refusal. We will therefore retain the reading *sod* + *dam*, "the secret of blood", without giving up the other two: a grid is meant to be equipment, not verdict.

## 3. Adam, dam, adamah: the progression of murder {#s3}

The name of Adam has received three readings, which do not belong to the same layer of the tradition. The first is biblical and rabbinic: *adamah*, the clay of which he is formed (Gen 2:7), a derivation taken up by Genesis Rabba. The other two are later and belong to medieval exegesis, Kabbalistic and Hasidic: aleph + *dam*, the blood he carries; *adameh*, "I will resemble", an echo of the pride of Isaiah 14:14 (*eddameh le-'elyon*). All three share the skeleton D-M.

The fruitful anomaly is in Genesis 4:10: "the voice of your brother's bloods cries out to me from the ground". Blood becomes earth again in accusing; the readings of Adam's name converge in the first murder. The plural *demai*, "bloods", is already commented on by the Mishnah (Sanhedrin 4:5): the blood of Abel and that of all his descendants. The first murder contains all murders to come.

This mechanism allows a progression in three degrees:

- **Abel:** one kills one's brother. The blood is visible, the victim close, the motive identifiable.
- **Sodom:** one kills the stranger. The blood is collective, socialized, diluted into the norm of an entire city.
- **The end of time:** murder loses its meaning. The Islamic tradition describes the *harj*, the killing that multiplies (Bukhari, Muslim). It specifies that a day will come when "the killer will not know why he killed, nor the victim why he was killed" (Muslim 2908). The same corpus adds an inner death, without bloodshed: the morning believer becomes an unbeliever by evening, selling his religion for a good of this world (Muslim 118).

Blood keeps flowing, but what disappears is its legibility. Violence becomes massive and senseless on one side, transactional and silent on the other. The corresponding destruction will progress, as we shall see, from the visible to the illegible.

## 4. The ga'on of Sodom: the closed door {#s4}

Ezekiel 16:49 gives the juridical definition of Sodom: *ga'on*, pride; satiety of bread; idle tranquility; and the hand of the poor that is not supported. The fault is neither lust, a late reading, nor even physical violence, but closure: accumulated greatness that does not circulate. The first word of the list deserves attention: *ga'on*, the very root that the next part will find again at Babel.

The narrative of Genesis 19 is built around a door. Lot sits at the gate of the city (*sha'ar Sedom*, 19:1), the place of judgment and welcome. He closes the door of his house behind him (19:6). The attackers, struck with blindness, exhaust themselves searching for the entrance (19:11). The Qur'an, in echo, reproaches Lot's people with "cutting off the road" (29:29): the city closes itself to travellers.

The Islamic corpus of the signs of the Hour describes exactly this retraction:

- **Selective greeting.** Among the signs of the Hour, one will greet only one's acquaintances (Musnad of Ahmad, from Ibn Mas'ud). This is the exact opposite of the prophetic rule: greet "whom you know and whom you do not know" (Bukhari 12, Muslim 39).
- **The harj.** Killing increases; the Prophet specifies: "killing, killing" (Bukhari, Muslim).
- **The intimate reversal.** Tribulations fall in the midst of houses "as rain drops fall" (Bukhari, Muslim 2885).

The first symptom is not violence, but the narrowing of the word of peace: closure before murder. The Mishnah condenses the same diagnosis: "In the footsteps of the Messiah, insolence will increase and prices will rise […] a man's enemies will be the people of his own house" (Sotah 9:15, citing Micah 7:6). This final passage of Sotah is in fact often considered a late addition to the Mishnah text, which brings it, chronologically, close to apocalyptic literature. Three traditions, one diagnosis: the horizontal bond tears from within.

## 5. Babel: the gate of the god {#s5}

To Sodom's horizontal closure answers Babel's vertical ascent: "let us build a tower whose top is in the heavens, and let us make a name for ourselves" (Gen 11:4). The text plays on Bavel and *balal*, "to confuse" (11:9). This etymology is controversial. The real name, in Akkadian, is *bāb-ili*, "the gate of the god". The city named itself as a threshold between earth and heaven; its ziggurat was called "house of the foundation of heaven and earth". The biblical narrative reverses this name: the gate of the god becomes the place of confusion.

Hebrew has no separate word for greatness. *Ga'on* designates both human pride and divine majesty (Ex 15:1, *ga'oh ga'ah*); *ga'avah*, pride, belongs to the same root G-A-H, "to rise". Everything lies in orientation: greatness mounted toward oneself is pride, greatness received from above is majesty. Ezekiel 16:49 has already placed this *ga'on* at the root of Sodom's fault. Sodom and Babel are therefore not two faults but one, projected in two directions: toward the horizontal, it closes; toward the vertical, it rises.

The same contrast bears on the name. Babel wants *na'aseh lanu shem*, "let us make a name for ourselves". In the next chapter, God says to Abram: "I will make your name great" (Gen 12:2). The manufactured name opposes the received name.

The sanction itself is ambivalent. The builders build "lest we be scattered" (*pen nafuts*, 11:4); God scatters them (11:8). Yet the scattering fulfils the initial command to "fill the earth" (Gen 1:28; 9:1). The sanction realizes the blessing that the tower refused: curse and blessing lodged in the same gesture.

The hadith of Jibril (Muslim 8) foretells that barefoot shepherds, poor, will compete in the height of buildings. The word is not the big house, but the house that rises: poverty of origin turned into stone height, a compensatory *ga'on* erected as a trophy. Isaiah's objection is ready: "enlarge the place of your tent" (Isa 54:2). The tent spreads, the tower rises; two greatnesses that the text distinguishes by the direction of movement, not by vocabulary.

## 6. The two ends of time: the reasoned, the narrated, and the sign of money {#s6}

The asymmetry of the two eschatological corpora is real, but it must be formulated precisely. Judaism too has narrative apocalypses, contemporary with the nascent Islam: the Sefer Zerubbabel (7th century), the figure of Armilus, the Otot ha-Mashiah. The Talmud itself tells: it shows the Messiah sitting at the gates of Rome among the lepers (Sanhedrin 98a). The asymmetry therefore does not separate two traditions, but two statuses of the text within each of them.

| | Rabbinic Jewish tradition | Sunni Islamic tradition |
|---|---|---|
| Normative corpus | Sparing Bible (Daniel, Ezekiel 38-39, Zechariah); Talmud | Sparing Qur'an; canonical hadith collections, rich in *fitan* |
| Place of the apocalyptic narrative | Outside the normative canon (medieval apocalypses) | Integrated into the normative corpus |
| Stance | Open debate; curse on "those who calculate the end" (Sanhedrin 97b) | Identification of fulfilled signs |
| Shared materials | Gog and Magog, family division (Micah 7:6 in Sotah 9:15) | Gog and Magog, the descent of Jesus, family division |

The transmission channels are multiple. The *isrā'īliyyāt* are one; Syriac Christian apocalyptic (Pseudo-Methodius, the Antichrist, Gog and Magog) is another, probably as important. One corpus kept the narrative within its norm; the other kept within its norm the reasoning about the narrative.

Now comes money, the most precise discriminator of the comparison. On the Jewish side, two texts make currency the sign of the approach:

- "The son of David will not come before the small coin (*peruta*) has disappeared from the purse" (Sanhedrin 97a).
- "In the footsteps of the Messiah, prices will rise; the vine will give its fruit, but the wine will be beyond price" (Sotah 9:15): the produce exists, the value escapes.

On the Islamic side, the sign is inverted:

- The treasures of the earth follow the Dajjal "like swarms of bees"; he commands the sky to rain and the earth sprouts (Muslim 2937).
- He has water and fire with him, but his water is a fire and his fire a cool water (Muslim 2934).

The corpus also contains its own reserve: to those who say he will have mountains of bread and water, the Prophet answers that he is more insignificant than that in God's sight (Muslim 2939, hadith of al-Mughira ibn Shu'ba). The Dajjal's abundance is an illusion as much as a power.

Two readings present themselves; one should keep the fork rather than choose a barycentre.

**Branch A.** The same ordeal, abundance as a tempting market. The Dajjal personifies satiety: his reign is a market where faith is bought. The Jewish tradition displaces the same mechanism into the economic structure: dearness is a satiety that collapses, money evaporates from the purse like wine from the vine. Islam theatricalizes abundance as a seducer; Judaism analyzes it as an evaporation of value.

**Branch B.** Two complementary moments. The Dajjal precedes the end; the disappearance of the *peruta* accompanies it. Fraudulent abundance is the ordeal, scarcity its purge. One sells one's faith in the morning for worldly gain; by evening neither faith nor money remains.

In both branches, the last battle is not fought on the field of violence, but on that of exchange. Satiety makes faith alienable; alienable faith makes a person purchasable; the purchasable person closes his door, and the closed door already exists in Sodom.

## 7. The Dajjal: the gate and the forehead {#s7}

### Anti-Babel: the one who says he is God

Babel sought the gate of the god by rising toward it. The Dajjal no longer rises: he presents himself as god. The hadiths describe him proclaiming himself first a prophet, then saying "I am your Lord"; and the Prophet immediately opposes: "you will not see your Lord before you die" (Ibn Majah 4077). The criterion given to believers is physical: he is one-eyed, and your Lord is not one-eyed (Bukhari 7131).

The tradition therefore thinks of him as the inverse of Babel. Babel is the man who wants to force God's gate from above; the Dajjal is the one who claims to be the gate, and brings down to earth the signs of heaven: rain, growth, treasures. The vertical *ga'on* has changed shape: it no longer builds a tower, it usurps majesty itself.

The corpus contains a remarkable irony: doors remain closed to him. He cannot enter Medina, whose approaches are guarded by angels (Bukhari 1881). He is killed by Jesus at the gate of Lod, *bab Ludd* (Muslim 2937). The one who claims to be the gate of God dies before a gate. The motif completes the two preceding ones:

| Figure | The gate | The movement |
|---|---|---|
| Sodom | Gate of the city and gate of the house, closed to the stranger | Horizontal closure |
| Babel | *Bab-ili*, "gate of the god", aimed at by the tower | Vertical ascent |
| The Dajjal | Claims to be the gate; turned back at Medina, killed at *bab Ludd* | Usurpation: the vertical brought down to earth |

### K-F-R on the forehead

The hadiths describe an inscription between his eyes: *kafir*. One version spells the letters one by one, K-F-R, and specifies that every believer will read it, "whether he can write or not" (Muslim 2933). The choice of the word deserves attention, because the root has several faces:

| Reading | Meaning | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| *Kafara*, to cover | to cover, to bury | the farmer covers the seed |
| *Kafir*, to deny | the denier | the Dajjal's frontal inscription |
| *Kuffar*, sowers | the ploughmen | Qur'an 57:20: the rain makes vegetation grow that delights the *kuffar*, then it withers |
| *Kofer*, pitch | to coat, to protect | Gen 6:14: Noah coats the ark with *kofer* against the waters |
| *Kipper*, to expiate | to wipe away the fault | Yom Kippur; Ezekiel 16:63 |

A caveat is in order: the primary meaning of Hebrew *kipper* is debated. Akkadian *kuppuru* means "to wipe, to clean", and part of scholarship translates it as "to purge" rather than "to cover". The "cover" chain is solid in Arabic, more discussed in Hebrew; the reading that follows is therefore midrashic, not etymological.

This status stated, the mechanism is of remarkable coherence. The same action changes meaning according to its object: to cover the seed is to make it fruitful; to cover the ark is to save it from the waters; to cover the truth is to deny it; to cover the fault through the rite is expiation. The Dajjal is an inverted sower: as in Qur'an 57:20, he makes rain fall and the earth grow, but his harvest is denial. The denier is not sterile; he is productive, and it is his productivity that seduces.

**Branch A.** K-F-R deprived of its expiatory face. To cover without ever uncovering again. Pure, closed negation: the mechanism of Sodom brought to its perfection, the closed door become a face.

**Branch B.** A warning, not a condemnation. To choose as a label a word that also contains the seed, the ark and pardon is not indifferent. Ezekiel 16 offers the model: the chapter that defines Sodom's fault promises its restoration (16:53-55) and ends with *be-khapri lakh*, "when I will make expiation for you" (16:63). The blessing remains in the very word of the curse, in accordance with our central hypothesis.

## 8. Conclusion: destroy or deconcentrate? {#s8}

Three mechanisms run through the whole corpus: blood (D-M), closure (Sodom), verticality (Babel). The last two proceed from a single root, the *ga'on* of Ezekiel 16:49, and the Dajjal is its ultimate form: it no longer merely closes its door, no longer rises toward God's, it claims to be that gate.

Each time, the divine sanction appears less as a destruction than as a deconcentration. At Babel, God scatters a people too concentrated, and the scattering fulfils the refused blessing. At the end of time, loyalties divide within the very household, while towers are rebuilt.

The covenant of the rainbow (Gen 9) closes the weapon of water, but says nothing of fire. The rabbinic tradition noticed this, with a precision worth preserving: to kings who fear a flood of fire, Balaam answers that the divine oath covers "destroy all flesh", in whatever manner; there will therefore be no more universal flood, neither of water nor of fire, but fire remains a weapon against the nations (Zevahim 116a, on Isaiah 66:16). Sodom is already the precedent, destroyed by a rain of brimstone and fire (Gen 19:24): a fire punishment, local, never total. The Dajjal offers the deceptive version: his water is a fire. The last punishment, in the branches followed here, no longer needs to be visible, because the last murder has lost its meaning.

Isaiah's tent widens; Babel's tower rises. The two ends of time, the reasoned and the narrated, say the same thing from two shores: greatness that rises toward itself is the place of danger, and money is its most legible sign, seductive abundance in one, evaporation in the other.

---

## 9. Threads left open {#s9}

- If murder progresses from the visible to the senseless, has the corresponding destruction already begun wherever faith is sold, without any fire being needed?
- Does the promise of the rainbow close a single element, water, or does it call for a response proportionate to each mechanism of fault: water for corruption, fire for Sodom, scattering for Babel?
- Is the K-F-R inscription on the Dajjal's forehead a definitive designation of evil, or the trace, even in evil, of an expiation still possible?
