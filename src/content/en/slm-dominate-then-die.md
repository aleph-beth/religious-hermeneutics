> **Author's note on the word "religion".** I use the word "religion" in this article purely for convenience of translation; in the body I set it in *italics* to mark each use. Yet the word does not exist in the ancient Semitic world: no Semitic language designates a confessional institution separate from life. What exists is the path, the way — Hebrew *dérekh* (דֶּרֶךְ), Arabic *ṭarīq* (طَرِيق) — and above all *dîn* (Arabic دِين), the path of justice and of debt owed to God, which embraces at once worship, law and way of living. Qur'an 109:6 says it in so many words: "to you your *dîn*, to me my *dîn*" — not "your religion" but "your path". When I speak here of a "distinct religion" or a "religion of the name," I mean a political construction of a path, never an essence. The modern category of "religion" is a late import, via Latin *religio*.

## 1. Introduction {#s1}

An empire that expands until it has no frontier left has, at the same stroke, no mirror: it can no longer see itself as different from what it subdues. This apparent paradox is developed here as a structural necessity. The thesis has three propositions: "islām" is a theological construction posterior to the advent of Islam (documentary mechanism); the name designates an act, not a community (etymological mechanism); a totalized act no longer has a bearer, so the global domination of a construction thus named would carry its immediate collapse within it (eschatological mechanism). The three mechanisms chain together: each prepares the validity of the next.

## 2. Documentary mechanism: a community of believers, not of Muslims {#s2}

Contemporary historical criticism, in particular Fred Donner (*Muhammad and the Believers*, 2010), establishes three points of fact:

1. The Qur'an almost always calls the companions *mu'minūn*, "believers"; the term *muslim* is rare there and used otherwise — not as the label of the group, but as a trans-historical category (see section 3).
2. The material evidence of the seventh century — coins, papyri, inscriptions — contains nothing distinctively Islamic: *bismillah*, names of governors, no mention of the Prophet. The successors style themselves "Commanders of the believers" (*amīr al-mu'minīn*), not caliphs of an Islam.
3. The shift from "believers" to "Muslims" occurs toward the end of the seventh century, under the Umayyads once stabilized. This semantic shift is the trace of a conceptual revolution: the construction of a distinct *religion*, separated from the other monotheisms.

Mechanism: "islām" is not the name of the founding event; it is the name the event retroactively received, when a power needed a distinct *religion* to legitimate itself. Islam is a theological construction posterior to the advent of Islam.

**Concrete examples.**

- At the beginning of the seventh century, coins struck by the Arab governors still imitated Byzantine and Sasanian models, cross included; only with the monetary reform of ʿAbd al-Malik, around 696-697, do aniconic dirhams appear bearing the profession of faith "there is no god but God, Muhammad is the messenger of God."
- The inscription of the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem (691-692, ʿAbd al-Malik) pairs the same profession of faith with Qur'anic verses on Jesus (Qur'an 19:88-93; 4:171): the Umayyad power there asserts a distinct *religion* in monumental space for the first time.
- Seventh-century administrative papyri carry "in the name of God," the names of governors and the title "Commander of the believers" (*amīr al-mu'minīn*) — never "caliph of an Islam."
- The shift lies concretely under the reign of ʿAbd al-Malik (685-705), once the Umayyad power was stabilized after the second fitna.

Sources: Fred Donner, *Muhammad and the Believers: At the Origins of Islam*, Harvard University Press, 2010; "When Believers Became Muslims," *Oasis* 23.

## 3. Etymological mechanism: the name designates an act, not a community {#s3}

The root S-L-M (cf. part 1) names an act — giving oneself whole — and not a set of persons. Three consequences:

1. In the Qur'an itself, "Muslim" is not a communal label: Abraham is a *muslim*, the disciples of Jesus are *muslimūn*. The word crosses the entire history of the prophets; it therefore cannot name the group founded by Muhammad, of which it would be only a particular case.
2. A community of acts is open; a community of name is closed. The first movement, that of the *mu'minūn*, was a community of the act of faith — open, ecumenical in Donner's sense, welcoming Jews and Christians. The renaming as "Muslims" closes the set.
3. The first movement called itself under the root A-M-N — the root of "amen": trust, the interiority of faith. The renaming under S-L-M transfers identity from interiority to totality: from what I believe to what the whole world must submit to.

Mechanism: the passage from *mu'min* to *muslim* is the passage from the religious to the political. To name the community "Islam" is to define it by the operation of the world's submission, not by the faith of its members.

**Concrete examples.**

- Qur'an 3:67: "Abraham was neither Jew nor Christian; he was a *ḥanīf*, a *muslim*" — the first patriarch is called *muslim* before Muhammad.
- Qur'an 2:136: the believers of all the revelations "are submitted to God" (*muslimūn*) — the word designates a posture, not a membership.
- Qur'an 5:111: Jesus himself prays "make us *muslims*," and the disciples answer "we are the helpers of God" (Qur'an 5:112).

## 4. Eschatological mechanism: death by fulfilment {#s4}

From what precedes follows the central proposition: "Islam must die" is neither a wish nor a prediction, but a consequence of the structure.

1. An identity defined by a border dies with its border. *Dār al-islām* exists only against *dār al-ḥarb*; if submission becomes total, the outside cancels itself and the category that named the difference cancels itself with it. When there is no more abode of war, there is no more abode of submission — there is a world without remainder.
2. A *religion* that names the act of submission has its telos in accomplished submission. When *shalom* is reached, the operation *islām* no longer has an object: realized totality suppresses the tension that made it necessary. The root that carried the two hands, adherence and reduction, has nothing left to seize.
3. The scenario "domination one day, collapse the next" is therefore not paradoxical but logical. What collapses is not the faith of believers — the root A-M-N, which survives every political construction — but the S-L-M construction: a name of an act become a name of empire, which dies at the exact moment the act is consummated.

**Concrete examples.**

- Qur'an 5:3 states the fulfilment in the text itself: "This day I have perfected for you your *religion*, completed My favour upon you, and chosen for you Islam as *religion*" — the Arabic word being *dîn*, the path."
- In 1855, Saʿid Pasha's Egypt abolished the *jizya*, and the Ottoman Hatt-ı Hümayun of 1856 proclaimed the equality of all subjects: the *dhimmi* status, which only makes sense within the border between *dār al-islām* and *dār al-ḥarb*, dissolved from within.
- In 1295, Ghazan, khan of the Mongols, converted to Islam: inhabitants of the *dār al-ḥarb* entered the *dār al-islām*, and the border changed sides without disappearing.
- In 1924, Turkey abolished the caliphate: the claim to a global Islamic empire disappeared with the institution that carried it.

## 5. Synthesis {#s5}

The three axes chain by necessity:

- the document shows that the name is posterior to the thing (the construction has a date of birth);
- etymology shows that the name designates an act and not a community (the construction has a logical form);
- eschatology shows that a totalized act no longer has a bearer (the construction has a condition of death).

Islam dies of its victory, like every construction that names the process of its own disappearance. It can dominate the world one day and collapse the next, not despite its strength, but by the exact virtue of its definition.

## 6. Opening {#s6}

The corpus now holds three roots: K-F-R, exclusion, which carries redemption within it (*kafir* / *kippur*); S-L-M, total inclusion, which carries its own end within it; A-M-N, trust. Open question to close the triptych: is A-M-N the only one of the three roots that does not contain its own contradiction — and would that be why a *religion* of the name could alone not die?
