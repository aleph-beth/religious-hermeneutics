**Type**: Strategic analysis — medical transfer
**Thesis**: Modern European medicine is a daughter of Cordoba. Al-Zahrawi (Abulcasis) invents systematic surgery, Ibn Zuhr (Avenzoar) founds experimental clinical medicine, Maimonides synthesizes Greco-Arabic knowledge. Their texts, translated in Toledo, become the required textbooks of European universities for five centuries — then Europe erases their names.

---

## 1. The three giants of Cordovan medicine {#s1}

### Al-Zahrawi (Abulcasis, 936-1013) — The father of surgery

| | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Full name** | Abu al-Qasim Khalaf ibn al-Abbas al-Zahrawi |
| **Location** | Madinat al-Zahra, suburb of Cordoba |
| **Major work** | *Kitab al-Tasrif li-man 'ajiza 'an al-ta'lif* (The Method, 30 volumes) |
| **Latin translation** | Gerard of Cremona, Toledo, 12th century |

**What he invents**:

The thirtieth volume of the *Tasrif*, devoted to surgery, is a revolution. Al-Zahrawi describes and illustrates more than **200 surgical instruments** — most of his own invention:
- Catgut for internal sutures (observed after watching a monkey eat the strings of an oud)
- Arterial ligation to control hemorrhage (six centuries before Ambroise Pare)
- The speculum, obstetric forceps, the probe, the cautery
- The first descriptions of thyroidectomy, lithotomy, craniotomy
- The use of cotton as a medical dressing (the word *cotton* comes from the Arabic *qutn*)

**European lifespan of the text**: the *Tasrif* was used as a textbook at Salerno, Montpellier, Bologna and Paris from the 12th to the 17th century — **five hundred years**.

### Ibn Zuhr (Avenzoar, 1094-1162) — The experimental clinician

| | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Seville (Al-Andalus) |
| **Major work** | *Kitab al-Taysir fi al-Mudawat wa al-Tadbir* (The Book of Simplification) |
| **Key innovation** | Separation of medicine and pharmacy; animal testing before human application |

**What he invents**:
- The first clinical description of tracheotomy (tested on a goat)
- The first description of pericardial abscess and mediastinitis
- The first tube feeding (nasogastric and rectal)
- The insistence on **direct clinical observation** over argument from authority
- Systematic physician-pharmacist collaboration (with Ibn Rushd/Averroes, who wrote the *Kulliyyat* as a theoretical complement to the *Taysir*)

The **Ibn Zuhr (practice) + Averroes (theory)** tandem prefigures the modern division between clinical medicine and theoretical medicine.

### Maimonides the physician (1138-1204) — The Greco-Arabo-Jewish synthesis

Maimonides, beyond his philosophical work, was the personal physician to the court of Saladin in Cairo. His medical treatises — written in Arabic — cover asthma, hemorrhoids, poisons, dietetics, and above all mental health.

His *Treatise on Asthma* is the first text to note the psychosomatic component of the disease. His *Medical Aphorisms* (1,500 aphorisms organized in 25 chapters) circulated throughout Latin Europe.

## 2. The chain of transmission {#s2}

```
Galen/Hippocrates (Greece)
    -> Hunayn ibn Ishaq (Baghdad, 9th c.): Arabic translation
        -> Al-Zahrawi (Cordoba, 10th c.): systematic surgery
        -> Ibn Zuhr (Seville, 12th c.): experimental clinical medicine
        -> Averroes (Cordoba, 12th c.): medical theory (Kulliyyat/Colliget)
        -> Maimonides (Cordoba -> Cairo, 12th c.): synthesis and aphorisms
            -> Gerard of Cremona (Toledo, 12th c.): Latin translation
                -> School of Salerno (12th-13th c.)
                    -> Montpellier, Bologna, Paris (13th-17th c.)
                        -> Guy de Chauliac (1363): cites Al-Zahrawi 200+ times
                        -> Ambroise Pare (16th c.): "rediscovers" ligation
                            -> Modern European medicine
```

## 4. The mechanism of occultation {#s4}

| Phase | Period | Process |
|-------|--------|---------|
| **Reception** | 12th-13th c. | Guy de Chauliac (*Chirurgia Magna*, 1363) cites Al-Zahrawi by name, acknowledges him as master |
| **Assimilation** | 14th-15th c. | The texts are integrated into the curriculum — "Abulcasis" and "Avenzoar" are familiar names |
| **Erasure** | 16th-17th c. | Ambroise Pare "rediscovers" arterial ligation. Vesalius modernizes anatomy. Arabic sources disappear from citations |
| **Occultation** | 18th-19th c. | The history of medicine begins with Hippocrates and resumes at the Renaissance — the Islamic millennium is a "black hole" |
| **Rediscovery** | 20th c. | Contemporary historiography (Pormann, Savage-Smith) restores the lineage |

## 5. The colonial irony {#s5}

When French military doctors landed in Algeria in 1830, they brought a medicine descended from Al-Zahrawi to "civilize" the descendants of Al-Zahrawi. Colonial medicine — an instrument of legitimation for the civilizing project — is itself a legacy of the very world it claimed to civilize.
