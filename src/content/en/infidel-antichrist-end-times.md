## 1. Introduction {#s1}

The word "infidel" seems self-evident: the one who does not believe. Yet it was not the word of the first crusaders. The chroniclers of 1095-1099 speak above all of "pagans". *Infideles* only became dominant in the course of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, when the papacy and the canonists stabilized the vocabulary of the crusade.

This shift is the starting point of this study. The term that prevailed condenses three mechanisms: a juridical and scholastic category, a collision of theological grids, and an eschatological reading of history. The last element, without being the only key, explains the intensity of the vocabulary. Part of the contemporaries read the expansion of Islam as a sign of the times.

The plan follows the construction of the word layer by layer: the lexicon, law and scholasticism, the genealogy of peoples, the grid of law and faith, the syllogism of the Antichrist, and finally the eschatological perspective. The chronological scope runs from the First Crusade (1095) to the middle of the thirteenth century.

---

## 2. The lexicon: from "pagans" to "infidels" {#s2}

The narratives of the First Crusade call the enemy *pagani*, *gentiles*, *Turci* or *Saraceni* — as in the *Gesta Francorum*, Fulk of Chartres and Robert the Monk¹. The vocabulary is ethnic or biblical, rarely doctrinal.

*Infideles* gains ground in the twelfth century and becomes common in the thirteenth, in the crusade bulls and among the canonists². This shift expresses a change of gaze: one ceases to name a people in order to name a relation to the faith. The study that follows therefore concerns the word as it stabilized, not the spontaneous usage of the first crusaders.

The instability of the lexicon remains strong throughout the period: pagan, Saracen, heretic and infidel coexist. The respective share of each term would deserve a corpus study (chronicles, chansons de geste, sermons, bulls).

---

## 3. The juridical and scholastic layer: the one who has no faith {#s3}

In medieval Latin, *infidelis* means "who has no faith", not "who has betrayed a faith". The distinction between infidels (the unbaptized), apostates (the baptized who have renounced Christ) and heretics (the baptized who have deformed the faith) is real. It was, however, only systematized in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, among the decretists and then in Thomas Aquinas³. It is therefore posterior to the First Crusade.

In Thomas, the tripartition is moreover not a juxtaposition of boxes. *Infidelitas* is a genus that encompasses heresy: the heretic is a species of infidel, and the gravest one, for he rejects a faith already received (*Summa Theologiae*, IIa-IIae, q. 10, a. 6).

Thomas distinguishes above all two forms of unbelief. "Negative" unbelief is a simple absence of faith in one who has never heard of it; it is not a sin⁴. "Contrary" unbelief is a refusal of the faith once heard. This distinction exactly overlaps the tension, examined further on, between the Muslim who would have received nothing and the Muslim who refuses.

The choice of *fides* as the criterion remains Pauline. Christian theology was built on the priority of faith and grace over the law (Romans, Galatians)⁵. If salvation comes through faith, the boundary between human beings runs through faith.

---

## 4. The overlooked genealogy: a category that ignores history {#s4}

The definition "never received the faith" does not correspond to the history of the lands of Islam. Before the Arab conquests, Egypt, Syria, Mesopotamia and North Africa were largely Christian. Some Arab tribes were too, such as the Ghassanids and the Lakhmids.

Islamization was, however, slow and diverse. According to Richard Bulliet's estimates⁶, a Muslim majority is reached in most regions only around the tenth century, later still in Egypt. A considerable share of the converts came moreover from Zoroastrian Iran, not from Christianity. One can therefore affirm that a significant fraction of the Muslims of the Near East and North Africa descended from Christians. One cannot affirm that this was the case for the majority of Muslims in the first centuries.

Among the Franks of the eleventh century, this prior Christianity is not so much forgotten as unknown. The word *infidelis* does not proceed from a deliberate erasure, but it entrenches an ignorance and makes it durable. It posits the Muslim as a total outsider.

Two readings of Islam coexisted. John of Damascus (eighth century), living under Muslim rule, classes it among the heresies, under the name of the "heresy of the Ishmaelites"⁷. The first crusaders, for their part, see pagans in it. One might think that geographical distance arbitrates between these readings. But Peter the Venerable, abbot of Cluny, very far from the East, also qualifies Islam as a heresy, and Dante places Muhammad among the sowers of schism⁸.

The decisive criterion is therefore less distance than knowledge of the texts. Peter the Venerable had the Qur'an translated into Latin in 1143⁹; once it is read, one recognizes in it a deformation of Christianity. Heresy is the reading of those who know; paganism, that of those who do not.

Finally, two senses must be distinguished. The juridical sense designates the one who has never been baptized. The polemical sense designates the one who refuses to believe. The Qur'an explicitly denies the divinity of Christ and his crucifixion (Qur'an 4:157). An instructed Christian therefore knows that the Muslim does not ignore this: he denies it. It is the "contrary" unbelief of Thomas Aquinas.

## 5. The two-axis grid: lex and fides {#s5}

The medieval classification of non-Christians articulates two criteria, law and faith. But Islam does not fit into it as simply as one might believe.

| Group | *Lex* (law) | *Fides* (Christian faith) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jews | Revealed law, but "carnal" and surpassed | No |
| Muslims | *Lex Machometi*: a law held to be human or forged | No |
| Pagans | No revealed law | No |

The Jew occupies a paradoxical place. He has the Law, the Scriptures and Antiquity, but he refuses the faith. The Augustinian doctrine of the "witness people"¹⁰ grounds his toleration: his survival attests to the truth of the Scriptures. The *lex* axis explains this toleration; the *fides* axis explains the hostility.

The Muslim is not, for the Latins, a man without law. The texts speak readily of *lex Machometi* or *lex Sarracenorum*, and the translation of the Qur'an commissioned by Peter the Venerable is entitled *Lex Mahumet pseudoprophete*¹¹. The Latins judge this law human, forged and carnal. The problem is therefore not the absence of law, but a law without revealed authority, which moreover claims a single God and honors Jesus as a prophet.

This hybrid position has no box of its own in the grid. It partly explains the instability of the vocabulary. The *Chanson de Roland* makes the Muslims worshippers of a triad of idols, Mahomet, Apollo and Tervagant¹². The absurdity is factual, but it is revealing: when the texts are ignored, the pagan is the only available box.

Any classification grid produces an enemy all the more unsettling because he escapes its boxes.

## 6. The Antichrist syllogism: denying the Father and the Son {#s6}

The idea that Islam would be the work of the Antichrist is not a gratuitous insult: it is a deduction. It appears early, among Christians living under Muslim rule. John of Damascus sees in Islam a "forerunner of the Antichrist". In the ninth century, at Cordoba, Euloge and Paul Alvar make Muhammad a forerunner of the Antichrist¹³.

The premise comes from the First Epistle of John (2:22): "Who is the liar but he who denies that Jesus is the Christ? This is the antichrist, he who denies the Father and the Son."¹⁴ A precision here is decisive. The Qur'an does not deny that Jesus is the Christ: it calls him *al-Masīḥ*, the Messiah¹⁵. Islam therefore does not fall under the first half of the verse.

It does, however, fall under the second. The Qur'an denies that God has a son (Qur'an 19:35; 112) and that Jesus was crucified (Qur'an 4:157). It therefore refuses what Christian polemic understands by "the Father and the Son". The First Epistle of John adds that every spirit that does not confess Jesus is the spirit of the antichrist (4:3). It is on these verses, and not on the sole mention of the Christ, that the reasoning rests.

Note also that the epistle speaks of "many antichrists" (2:18). The Johannine antichrist is first a type, that of the denier, before becoming the unique eschatological figure that the medieval tradition will construct. The passage from one to the other is precisely what medieval exegesis accomplishes.

The reasoning is valid; its force depends entirely on the axiom, which Islam rejects. Christians and Muslims do not contradict each other on logic, but on the starting point.

The symmetry is exact. Islam has its own cardinal sin, *shirk*, the act of associating partners with God, which the Qur'an declares unforgivable (Qur'an 4:48). It explicitly condemns those who say that God is "the third of three" (Qur'an 5:73). Each theology thus places the other at the top of its scale of the worst. Islam fulfills the Christian definition of the antichrist; Christianity fulfills the Islamic definition of the unforgivable sin. The conflict is not a misunderstanding: it is the collision of two coherences, each closed on its axiom.

## 7. The eschatological perspective: crusading as the end approaches {#s7}

The eschatological reading illuminates the intensity of this vocabulary without being its only explanation. It must be handled with care.

The "terrors of the year 1000" largely belong to a romantic construction, popularized by Michelet. Historians such as Richard Landes and Johannes Fried¹⁶ have however shown that the expectation of the end was indeed present around the year 1000, without general panic. It is not a collective anguish, but an available horizon, mobilizable in certain contexts.

This horizon has its texts. The Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius (seventh century)¹⁷, translated into Latin, announces a last emperor who will deposit his crown in Jerusalem before the reign of the Antichrist. Adso of Montier-en-Der (c. 950)¹⁸ specifies that the Antichrist will sit in the Temple of Jerusalem. The city is therefore the obligatory stage of the end.

This framework appears in Guibert of Nogent's account of Clermont¹⁹. Urban II is said to have affirmed there that the Antichrist must find Christians in Jerusalem to fight them. The city must therefore become Christian again: not to prevent the coming of the Antichrist, but so that the scenario of the end can be accomplished. The reconquest does not abolish the end; it makes it possible.

The bull *Quia maior* of Innocent III (1213)²⁰ explicitly formulates the link. It assimilates Muhammad to the Beast of the Apocalypse and relates the number 666 to the duration of Islam, whose end would be near. A power that denies the Son and holds Jerusalem then becomes more than a political adversary: a sign of the times.

One must not, however, reduce the crusade to this reading. The indulgence of Clermont is explained first in a penitential logic, as Jonathan Riley-Smith and others insist. The importance of eschatology in the First Crusade remains debated: Jay Rubenstein places it at the center²¹, others judge it secondary. The thesis adopted here is that it is not the cause of the crusade, but that it gives the word "infidel" its charge: it transforms a conflict of power into an episode of the final combat between Christ and his denier.

---

## 8. Conclusion {#s8}

The word "infidel", examined closely, is an archive of mechanisms. It is a late term, which replaces the ethnic lexicon of the first crusaders. It is a scholastic category that hesitates between absence and refusal of the faith. It entrenches an ignorance of the Christian genealogy of the Islamized peoples. It fits into a grid of law and faith in which Islam has no box of its own. It rests on a Johannine syllogism that makes the Muslim the denier of the Father and the Son. Finally, an eschatological reading, present without being universal, gives it its urgency. Each layer is coherent in itself; it is their stacking that produces the violence of the vocabulary.

The comparison with the Arabic *kāfir* opens a final perspective. The root *k-f-r* evokes the act of covering, concealing²². The Qur'anic *kāfir* is the one who covers a sign already received, or an innate disposition to recognize God. The Latin *infidelis*, on the contrary, designates the one who would never have received the gift. The two words each deny, by construction, the other's anteriority.

One question remains open. Is a reconciliation possible without asking one of the two systems to renounce its axiom, that is, what each defines as renunciation itself? Here the historian steps out of his role to suggest a path: the work perhaps begins not with theology, but with the restitution of the shared history that the two vocabularies held apart.
