## 1. Introduction: reading layer by layer {#s1}

The first article of the dossier, *The Root of Recovering*, established the synchronic structure of the root *k-p-r / k-f-r*: a unique gestural core, seven attested poles, a threefold polarity preserved within the Qur'anic canon itself. This second article engages the *diachronic* dimension. It reconstitutes, layer by layer, how each great tradition inheriting the ancient Semitic background has *received, reworked and redeployed* the operator — from second-millennium BCE Akkadian to the threshold of modernity (18th century).

The aim of this genealogy is to make visible a massive and too-often forgotten fact: **no pre-modern tradition has reduced the root to a single one of its poles**. All of them — rabbinic, Christian, Sufi, falsafian, classical Sunni, classical Shīʿī — have practised a *restraint* on the polemical use of *kufr*, and have preserved at their core the expiatory and merciful pole. Khārijite maximalism, which appears as early as the first *fitna* (656-661), is the exception explicitly disavowed by the entire great tradition.

This datum is crucial for understanding the modern rupture, whose archaeology will be carried out by article 3 of the dossier. Before the rupture, what *held together* must be carefully established. That is the object of the present article.

## 2. Proto-Semitic and Akkadian stratum (3rd-1st millennia BCE) {#s2}

### 2.1 The material gesture

In Akkadian, the root *k-p-r* unfolds into two related verbs: *kapāru* (*"to wipe, to rub, to ritually purify"*) and the noun *kupru* (*"bitumen"*). Both are attested as early as Old Akkadian. The practice is concrete: one applies a coating, one wipes, one makes disappear by covering. The gesture is *technical* before being *moral*.

### 2.2 The ritual gesture

From the middle of the second millennium, Mesopotamian texts attest a ritual use of *kapāru*: the *kuppuru*-ceremony is a rite of impurity-elimination through *rubbing* and *transfer* onto an animal substitute then cast outside the city (cf. Wright, *The Disposal of Impurity*, 1987). This Mesopotamian rite is the direct antecedent of the Hebrew scapegoat rite of Lev 16: one *kappers* on the head of the goat *Azaʾzel* and sends it into the wilderness. The Hebrew ritual gesture thus inherits an older Mesopotamian *ritus*, with its semantics of effacement-by-transfer.

### 2.3 The anthropological gesture

On every front — caulking the ark, ritual expiation, temple purification — the Mesopotamian *kpr* gesture is *positive*: it saves, it purifies, it brings across. No pejorative pole (*"to deny, to refuse"*) is attested in Akkadian. This absence is significant: the root, at its documented origin, is a *tool of salvation*, not a word of anathema. The polemical pole, when it emerges, will therefore be a *late and localized specialization*, not a native datum.

## 3. Hebrew biblical stratum (12th-3rd centuries BCE) {#s3}

### 3.1 The material gesture remains

Genesis 6:14 takes up Akkadian without rupture: *"kafarta otah ba-kofer."* The bitumen saves the ark.

### 3.2 The central ritual gesture

The great Hebrew innovation is to make *kpr* the central operator of the priestly sacrificial system. Leviticus 1-7, 16, 17 articulate the entire expiatory liturgy around the verb *kipper*. The blood poured on the altar or sprinkled on the *kapporet* (Lev 16:14) accomplishes the *kpr*. The system presupposes a *physics of the sacred*: sin leaves a material trace contaminating the sanctuary; a layer (the blood, the gold lid) is required that *interposes* between the trace and the divine gaze.

### 3.3 The sense of ransom

Exodus 21:30; 30:12; Numbers 35:31-32 — *kofer* is the ransom, what one pays so that a life is not claimed. Juridical *kpr* shares the same grammar: interposing a substitute.

### 3.4 The near-total absence of the sense "to deny"

In biblical Hebrew, the "deny" pole is marginal. The rare occurrences (Job 31:28; Proverbs 30:9?) are philologically contested. **The root in biblical Hebrew is *quasi-univocally positive***: it speaks of the saving, expiatory, juridical, technical gesture. This is essential: the great Semitic matrix of *kpr* is, at its biblical origin, *a word of salvation*.

### 3.5 The agrarian gesture

Although the Hebrew verb *kpr* is not used for sowing, the agrarian gesture of *covering the seed* is semantically akin and culturally central: Gen 26:12 (Isaac the blessed sower), Ps 126 ("those who sow in tears"), Hos 10:12. It prepares the gospel of the sower and the *kāfir*-peasant of Q 57:20.

## 4. Hebrew rabbinic stratum (1st-10th centuries CE) {#s4}

### 4.1 The centrality of Yom Kippur

After the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE, the material expiatory system disappears, but the root *kpr* becomes *more* central, not less. The tractate *Mishnah Yoma* (c. 200 CE) devotes five of its eight chapters to the ritual of *Yom ha-Kippurim*. The strong tannaitic idea: *"death and Yom Kippur atone (mekapprim) with repentance"* (Mishnah Yoma 8:8). *Kipper* becomes the central operator of Jewish soteriology.

### 4.2 *Sifra Aḥarei Mot*

This tannaitic halakhic commentary on Leviticus 16 explicitly articulates the triad *teshuvah / yom ha-kippurim / yissurim* (repentance / Yom Kippur / sufferings) as the structure of post-Temple *kpr*. Rabbinic soteriology posits *kpr* as a tripartite device: effacement of the fault requires human repentance, the gift of a liturgical time, and trial.

### 4.3 Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 35b-87b

The Amoraic development on *kpr* is immense. The aphorism of R. Yehudah ha-Nasi (Yoma 85b) is emblematic: *"Yom Kippur atones [mekapper] for offences between a person and God, but not for those between a person and his fellow, until he appeases him."* **The *kpr* is limited by horizontal justice; it is never a magical seal.** This limitation, fundamental, is one of the most powerful ethical safeguards of the rabbinic tradition: no rite effaces a fault committed against another human without restitution to that human.

### 4.4 The late emergence of the "deny" sense

Sanhedrin 99a introduces the category of *kofer ba-ʿiqqar* (*"one who denies the principle"*) — this is the pejorative sense of the root, but it appears late and remains marginal in the halakhah. It does not designate a Muslim or a Christian (the term applies first to heretical Jews) and it founds no extensive procedure of anathema: the *kofer ba-ʿiqqar* loses his standing as a *talmudic guarantor*, but remains a human being to be respected in his integrity. Rabbinic Hebrew thus preserves an essential distinction: *kofer* may designate a problematic theological position, without thereby legitimating violence against the person.

### 4.5 Midrashic readings

*Pirqei de Rabbi Eliezer* 23 (Geonic recension, 9th century) makes the midrashic reading of Noah's *kofer* explicit as a prefiguration of Israel's redemption. The bitumen becomes a figure of the Torah that *protects* the community from submersion.

*Bereshit Rabba* 31:8 proposes a paradoxical reading: *"Why bitumen? Because the generation of Noah had to be washed as one washes pitch in water."* The *kofer* is here read as partition between inside and outside: it *protects* the ark in order to allow the *destruction* outside. Recovering as sorting.

### 4.6 The word and the letter

Speculation on the letter *kaf* (כ) — open palm, hand that *covers* — appears in the *Otiyot of Rabbi Akiva* and in the *Zohar* (II, 152a). The *kaf* is the letter of the *kapporet* and of prayer (palms open upward). The priestly blessing gesture (*Birkat Kohanim*) is itself a *kpr* operation.

---

## 5. New Testament and Patristic stratum (1st-5th centuries CE) {#s5}

### 5.1 The decisive Pauline translation

Romans 3:25: *"whom God put forward as a propitiation (*hilastērion*)."* The word ἱλαστήριον is *exactly* the LXX translation of *kapporet*. Paul thus performs a *christological translation* of the *kapporet* onto the body of Christ. The blood of Christ becomes what the blood of the bull and the goat were doing on the *kapporet*: the central *kpr* gesture, but now accomplished once and for all.

Hebrews 9:5-12 redoubles this translation: the heavenly sanctuary, the offered blood, the accomplished expiation. *Hilastērion* recurs explicitly (9:5).

### 5.2 Synoptics and Johannine

As article 1 has established, the agrarian gesture of recovering-for-rising is christologized in John 12:24; the sower of the synoptic parable takes up Gen 26:12; Joseph of Arimathea performs the *kpr* of the tomb-furrow. Johannine soteriology makes Christ *the seed* that must be buried in order to bear fruit.

### 5.3 Greek Fathers

Irenaeus (*Adversus Haereses* III, 18-19), Athanasius (*De Incarnatione*), Cyril of Alexandria (*Glaphyra in Levit.*) develop the typological reading: *the *kapporet* prefigures Christ-as-propitiatory*. The Latin tradition (Tertullian, *Adv. Marc.* IV; Augustine, *De civ. Dei* X) takes up the motif. Origen, in his *Homilies on Leviticus* IX, reads Lev 16 as *"hidden sacrament of salvation"* (*sacramentum reconditum*) — the Hebrew *kpr* as a *"veil"* under which Christ may be glimpsed. A typological procedure rigorously rabbinic in its structure.

### 5.4 Ephrem the Syrian

Ephrem (4th century), in his *Memre* on Easter and his *Hymns on the Crucifixion*, is a key mediator between Jewish and Christian readings of *kpr*. He works in Syriac, where the root *kpr* preserves both poles *"to atone"* (*kappar*) and *"to deny"* (*kpar*). Syriac is, moreover, the probable language of transmission toward late antique Arabia (Witztum 2011, *Syriac Milieu*).

## 6. Pre-Islamic Arabic stratum (6th century CE) {#s6}

Article 1 has already established the dominant neutral-positive sense of the root *k-f-r* in pre-Quranic Arabic. The essentials are recalled:

- *al-kāfir* = the night (which covers the day) — Labīd, *Muʿallaqa*; Imruʾ al-Qays, *Muʿallaqa*;
- *al-kāfir* = the dark cloud (bearer of rain) — Tarafa, *Muʿallaqa*;
- *al-kāfir* = the sea (which covers everything) — al-Aṣmaʿī, *Aṣmaʿiyyāt*;
- *al-kāfir* = the cuirass, the sheath — ʿAntara, *Muʿallaqa*;
- *al-kāfir* = the sower (who buries the seed) — confirmed by classical lexicography and Q 57:20.

**Philological conclusion.** Pre-Islamic Arab society massively knew the root *k-f-r* in descriptive and technical uses. The pejorative religious sense (*"unbeliever"*) is *not* the primary sense: it is a *specialization* operating in the Qur'anic preaching, against the polemical background of the Quraysh idolaters. Before the Qur'an, to speak of *kufr* to an Arab was first to speak of the night that covers the sky.

## 7. Qur'anic stratum (610-650 CE) {#s7}

### 7.1 Early Mecca: the *shukr / kufr* couple

In early Meccan revelation, the root retains its original polarity. The decisive pair is *shukr / kufr* (Q 14:7; 27:40; 76:3). The *kāfir* is the *ungrateful* one, the one who *covers* the gift received. This is still close to pre-Islamic usage: *kufr* is an anthropological flaw, not an ontological category.

### 7.2 Middle and late Mecca: widening of the field

*Kufr* extends to *kufr bi-l-āyāt* (covering the signs, Q 6:27; 6:33), *kufr bi-l-ākhira* (denying the hereafter), *kufr bi-l-rabb* (denying the Lord). The constellation *khatm / ghishāwa / labbasa / katama* crystallizes (Q 2:7; 2:42).

### 7.3 Medina: the political polemic

The *kuffār* become the opposing Meccans, the Jews and Christians who refuse the prophecy, the hypocrites (*munāfiqūn*). Here usage becomes massive and polemical. But — crucial fact — *even at Medina, the expiatory pole is preserved and amplified*: Q 5:65; 8:29; 64:9; 4:31 use *yukaffir* to say that *God* covers sins.

### 7.4 The philological survival: Q 57:20

Right in the middle of a late Medinan sura (al-Ḥadīd), the Qur'an uses *al-kuffār* in its archaic agricultural sense. Tabarī, Zamakhsharī, Rāzī are unanimous: here *al-kuffār* = *al-zurrāʿ*. No classical reader was mistaken on this. This occurrence attests that, within the Qur'anic conceptual framework itself, the word can still mean the peasant-coverer.

### 7.5 Conclusion on the Qur'anic stratum

The Qur'an *operates* the polemical polarization but does not complete it. It preserves the Semitic dialectic: negative human *kufr*, positive divine *takfīr*, archaic agricultural *kāfir*. The word remains *threefold* in the canon. **It is *post-Quranic* usage that will reduce this threefold to a single pole.**

---

## 8. Classical *kalām* stratum (8th-12th centuries) {#s8}

The theological status of the *kāfir* becomes the central stake of the first Islamic controversies. Four main positions take shape.

### 8.1 Khārijites (from the *fitna* of 656)

Extremist position: any Muslim who commits a major sin (*kabīra*) is *kāfir* and therefore to be excluded from the community. *Takfīr* is *easy and extensive*. Khārijites practise inter-Muslim anathema and civil war. **A numerically minoritarian position but the matrix of all later returns to extensive *takfīr*.**

### 8.2 Murjiʾa (end of the first hijri century)

The opposite position: only God judges; one *suspends* (*irjāʾ*) judgement on Muslim sinners; as long as one professes the *shahāda*, one is *muʾmin*. *Takfīr* is *quasi-impossible*. A dominant position among the early *fuqahāʾ* of Kufa and Medina, inherited by the Hanafi school. **The Murjiʾa are the great guardians of theological restraint.**

### 8.3 Muʿtazila (9th century, Baghdad and Basra)

A median position: the great sinner is *fāsiq* (transgressor) — neither *muʾmin* nor *kāfir* — in a *manzila bayna al-manzilatayn* (*"position between the two positions"*). *Takfīr* is strictly delimited. *Reason* (*ʿaql*) prevails in establishing status. A determining influence on the Imāmī and Zaydī Shīʿī schools.

### 8.4 Ahl al-Sunna and Ashʿariyya

From al-Ashʿarī (d. 935), a synthesizing position: faith is principally *taṣdīq* (inner assent) and *iqrār* (verbal confession); works are its consequence but not strict condition. *Kufr* is the *absence of taṣdīq*, not mere ethical failure. **Hence *takfīr* is difficult to establish and must be avoided.** This becomes the majority position in classical Sunnism.

Al-Ghazālī (d. 1111), in his *Faysal al-tafriqa bayna al-īmān wa-l-zandaqa*, writes the great methodological defence:

> *"One must abstain to the maximum from takfīr, for declaring a Muslim kāfir exposes one to becoming oneself kāfir if the accusation is false."*

This rule of restraint, articulated from a prophetic hadith (*"Whoever declares a Muslim kāfir, and the accusation is false, the accusation falls back upon him"*), is one of the structuring principles of classical Sunnism. It makes the accusation of *kufr* against a Muslim a *theological risk for the accuser himself*.

### 8.5 Conclusion on *kalām*

Classical Sunni and Shīʿī theology are *quasi-unanimous* on the difficulty of *takfīr*. Khārijite maximalism is explicitly rejected. The dominant attitude is *reluctance*: *kufr* is a real category but *reserved* for extreme and well-established cases. **Extensive *takfīr* is the exception; restraint is the rule.**

## 9. *Falsafa* stratum (9th-12th centuries) {#s9}

### 9.1 Avicenna

Ibn Sīnā (d. 1037), in his *Shifāʾ* and *Najāt*, distinguishes three levels of discourse on God: demonstrative (philosophy), dialectical (theology), rhetorical-imaginative (prophecy for the masses). *Kufr* becomes a category *relative* to a level of discourse: what is *kufr* in one register may be *truth* in another. A delicate position, which feeds the accusation of *"taʾwīl"* (infinite allegorical interpretation).

### 9.2 Averroes

Ibn Rushd (d. 1198), in his *Faṣl al-Maqāl*, defends explicitly that *the philosopher who reads the Qur'an allegorically is not *kāfir**, because he conforms to *another* regime of truth, equally Islamic. The accusation of *kufr* against philosophy (launched by Ghazālī in the *Tahāfut al-falāsifa*) is rejected. Averroes is himself accused of *kufr* at the end of his life and exiled. His *Tahāfut al-tahāfut* is a systematic defence: *the philosopher is muʾmin according to a deeper grammar of taṣdīq*.

### 9.3 Maimonides

Mūsā ibn Maymūn (d. 1204), in his *Guide for the Perplexed* I, 50 and III, 27-28, performs for Judaism the same operation Averroes does for Islam: *the philosopher who reads the Torah allegorically is not *kofer**. He distinguishes *true opinions* (philosophically demonstrated) and *necessary opinions* (politically useful for the masses).

### 9.4 What this shows

*Falsafa* elaborates a *plural* reading of the root. It refuses to absolutize *takfīr*. For the philosophers, raw theological *kufr* is a *popular* category that does not say the depth of faith. The position is delicate, sometimes itself accused of *kufr*, but it persists as a minoritarian yet influential current in the medieval Islamic world.

---

## 10. Sufi stratum (9th-18th centuries) {#s10}

### 10.1 The mystical inversion

Classical Sufism performs a remarkable displacement: it takes seriously the Semitic dialectic of recovering and puts it *at the service of faith*, not against it.

### 10.2 Junayd al-Baghdādī

Junayd (d. 910) distinguishes the theological *al-kāfir* (who covers the truth through ignorance) and the mystical *kāfir* (who *covers* the ego in order to let God shine through). In some *Rasāʾil*, the Sufi is paradoxically *kāfir* in the eyes of the world because he has *covered* his own individuality. A mystical inversion of meaning.

### 10.3 al-Ḥallāj

Ḥusayn ibn Manṣūr al-Ḥallāj (d. 922) is famous for his *"anā al-Ḥaqq"* (*"I am the Truth"*), a proclamation for which he was executed for *kufr*. But his own writings (*Kitāb al-Ṭawāsīn*) perform a vertiginous reading in which *Iblīs* (Satan) is paradoxically the greatest *muwaḥḥid* (unitarian) because he refuses to prostrate before Adam (Q 2:34) — a refusal he interprets as fidelity to God's absolute oneness. A reading that turns *kufr* into radical *īmān*. Massignon, *La Passion de Hallāj* (1922, repr. 1975), remains the reference.

### 10.4 Ibn ʿArabī

Muḥyī al-Dīn Ibn ʿArabī (d. 1240), in his *Futūḥāt Makkiyya* II, 65 and his *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam*, elaborates the doctrine of *taʿdād al-ʿuqūd*:

> *"God manifests himself in all beliefs, and one who restricts his belief to a single form rejects the other forms."*

The total *kāfir*, in this grammar, is the one who *reduces* God to a single form — a gesture that *covers* divine transcendence. **An inversion: extensive *takfīr* is itself a *kufr*.**

### 10.5 Rūmī

Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī (d. 1273), in his *Mathnawī* III, 4719-4724, offers a famous passage on the shepherd and Moses: Moses rebukes a shepherd who speaks to God in naive terms (*"You are my feet, my hands, I will wash your clothes"*); God reproves Moses: *"you have separated one of my servants from me."* The diversity of paths is not *kufr*; *true kufr* is the sectarianism that claims to possess God.

### 10.6 Ibn Taymiyya — the inverse turn

Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) marks the turn that will be analysed in detail in article 3. It is he who reintroduces with vigour extensive *takfīr*, against Ibn ʿArabī and speculative Sufism, and who lays the doctrinal bases that 18th-century Wahhabism will take up. **The rupture is internal to the Islamic tradition itself.** The present article stops at its threshold.

## 11. Classical *fiqh* stratum (8th-15th centuries) {#s11}

While polemical theology has sometimes drifted toward polemical *kufr*, *fiqh* has carefully preserved the expiatory *kpr*. The classical manuals (al-Shāfiʿī, *Umm*; al-Sarakhsī, *Mabsūṭ*; Ibn Qudāma, *Mughnī*; al-Ḥillī for Shīʿism) devote whole chapters to *kaffārāt* (expiations):

- *kaffārat al-yamīn* (Q 5:89): expiation for a broken oath — feed 10 poor persons, or clothe them, or free a slave, or fast three days;
- *kaffārat al-ẓihār* (Q 58:3-4): expiation of pre-Islamic repudiation oath;
- *kaffārat al-qatl* (Q 4:92): expiation of involuntary murder — free a slave and pay blood-money;
- *kaffārat al-ramaḍān*: expiation for voluntary breaking of the fast;
- *kaffārat al-iḥrām*: expiation for faults committed during pilgrimage.

All these *kaffārāt* function on the model of Hebrew *kpr*: *interposing an act (gift, fasting, manumission) that covers the fault*. **The positive pole of the root is therefore preserved by law, where theology has sometimes forgotten it.** Every practising Muslim is, in practice, in relation with this positive pole: the word *takfīr*, in his prayer and daily jurisprudence, means *"to have the fault covered by God,"* not anathema. This datum is massively underestimated in contemporary discussions.

## 12. Diachronic balance: what the root carried before modernity {#s12}

At the end of these strata, the root *k-p-r / k-f-r* presents itself, on the eve of modernity (18th century), as a *semantic operator of rare richness*:

| Pole | Main tradition | Key lexemes |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Akkadian, Hebrew | *kupru*, *kofer* (bitumen) |
| Cultic-expiatory (human → God) | Priestly Hebrew, Pauline Christianity | *kapporet*, *kipper*, *hilastērion* |
| Juridical-ransom | Hebrew, Islamic law | *kofer*, *kaffāra* |
| Expiatory (God → human) | Qur'an, Sufism | divine *takfīr*, *yukaffir* |
| Agrarian | Pre-Islamic, Q 57:20, synoptic parable | *al-kuffār* (peasants), *ho speirōn* |
| Paradoxical mystical | Classical Sufism | *kufr* of the self (Hallāj, Ibn ʿArabī) |
| Theological-polemical | Medinan Qur'an, *kalām* | *kāfir* against *muʾmin* |
| Inter-Muslim anathema | Khārijites (minoritarian), Ibn Taymiyya | *takfīr al-muslim* |
| Sapiential-ethical | Meccan Qur'an | *kufr* against *shukr* |

**What is crucial.** No pre-modern tradition has ever reduced the root to *one only* of these poles. Classical Sunni *kalām* practises restraint; the *fuqahāʾ* maintain the expiatory pole; the Sufis dialectically invert; the philosophers relativize; rabbis, Christian Fathers, and Qur'anic commentators hold several registers together.

**The great question.** Has this plurality been preserved to the present day? It has not been — at least not in dominant public usage. It is the object of article 3 of the dossier to establish the turn that has transformed, from the 18th-20th centuries, this plurivocal operator into a univocal weapon.
