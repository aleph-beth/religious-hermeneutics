## 1. The Iberian paradox {#s1}

In the 11th century, Spain and Portugal are the poorest and most backward countries of Western Europe — feudal kingdoms in permanent war against Andalusian Islam. Three centuries later, they are masters of the world, dividing the planet between them at the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494).

How do two small kingdoms on Europe's periphery become the first global empires?

**Answer**: because they are the first to digest Andalusian knowledge. The Reconquista is not merely a military conquest — it is an intellectual plunder that transforms the victors.

## 2. The geographic advantage: proximity to Al-Andalus {#s2}

| Country | Position | Advantage |
|---------|----------|----------|
| **Portugal** | Western frontier of Al-Andalus, Atlantic facade | First access to Andalusian knowledge + oceanic opening |
| **Castile/Aragon** | Northern frontier of Al-Andalus | Direct access to Toledo (reconquered 1085), Cordoba (1236), Granada (1492) |
| **France, England, Germany** | Further north | Receive Andalusian knowledge *second-hand*, via Montpellier and the universities |

Portugal and Spain have an advantage of **two to three generations** over the rest of Europe in assimilating Andalusian knowledge. It is this temporal advantage that propels them to the forefront of maritime expansion.

## 3. What the Reconquista captures {#s3}

### Toledo (1085) — the intellectual treasure

The capture of Toledo by Alfonso VI is the founding moment. The city preserved an immense Arabic library. The School of Toledo translated massively from Arabic to Latin:
- Gerard of Cremona: 87 works translated
- Michael Scot: Aristotle, astronomy
- Dominicus Gundissalinus: philosophy

This knowledge spread first in Iberia, then to Paris and the rest of Europe. The Iberians had primary access.

### Cordoba (1236) — manuscripts and instruments

The capture of Cordoba by Ferdinand III delivered manuscripts, astronomical instruments, and — above all — the irrigation, agricultural, and craft techniques that enriched Castile.

### Granada (1492) — the final lock

The fall of Granada released the last Andalusian resources and, crucially, freed Castile's financial and military resources for overseas expansion.

## 4. The five decisive transfers {#s4}

### The astrolabe -> deep-sea navigation

**Al-Zarqali** (Cordoba, 11th c.) perfected the astrolabe and compiled the Toledan Tables. This instrument passed to Iberian astronomers, then to **Abraham Zacuto** (Salamanca), who transformed it into a copper marine astrolabe for the King of Portugal.

Without the Andalusian astrolabe, the Portuguese could not determine their latitude on the high seas. No latitude -> no deep-sea navigation -> no route to India -> no Portuguese empire.

### The lateen sail -> the caravel

The triangular lateen sail, borrowed from the **Arab dhows** of the Indian Ocean, allowed sailing into the wind. The Portuguese integrated it into the **caravel** — the exploration vessel that sailed down the African coasts. The word *caravel* probably derives from the Arabic *qarib*.

Without the Arab lateen sail, the Portuguese could not sail against headwinds along Africa. No lateen sail -> no caravel -> no Cape of Good Hope -> no route to India.

### Astronomical tables -> position calculations

The chain is direct:
```
Al-Khwarizmi (Baghdad) -> Al-Zarqali (Cordoba) -> Toledan Tables
    -> Alfonsine Tables (Toledo) -> Majorcan school -> Zacuto (Salamanca)
        -> Vasco da Gama (Lisbon) -> India
```

Columbus used al-Khwarizmi's calculations on the Earth's circumference (misapplying the unit of measurement). The error itself is an Arab inheritance.

### Judeo-Arab cartography -> portolan charts

The **Catalan Atlas** (1375), a cartographic masterpiece attributed to **Abraham Cresques** (Majorcan Jew), integrates Arabic geographical data into charts usable by Christian navigators. Majorca — reconquered in 1229 — was a center of cartography directly inherited from the Andalusian tradition.

The charts used by Portuguese and Spanish navigators carried data accumulated by Arab geographers: al-Idrisi, Ibn Jubayr, Ibn Battuta.

### Gunpowder -> military superiority

Portuguese and Spanish naval artillery — which gave them superiority over the fleets of the Indian Ocean (Indian, Arab, Malay) — rested on gunpowder transmitted via the Islamic world. Cannons mounted on caravels and carracks allowed them to dominate the seas without numerical superiority.

| Date | Event | Andalusian knowledge deployed |

| 1419 | Madeira discovered | Caravel with lateen sail |

**Every line in this table is an inheritance from Cordoba.**

## 6. Comparison with non-Iberian powers {#s6}

| Power | Entry into the race | Delay relative to Iberia | Reason |
|-------|---------------------|--------------------------|--------|
| **Portugal** | 1419 (Madeira) | — | Direct access to Andalusian knowledge |
| **Spain** | 1492 (Columbus) | ~70 years | Later Reconquista |
| **Holland** | ~1600 | ~180 years | Receives knowledge second-hand |
| **England** | ~1600 | ~180 years | Same |
| **France** | ~1600 | ~180 years | Same |

Portugal and Spain had **a century and a half head start** — exactly the time it took for Andalusian knowledge to travel from Toledo northward across Europe. By the time Holland and England entered the race, Portugal and Spain had already divided the world between them.

## 7. The thesis in one sentence {#s7}

**Portugal and Spain are not maritime powers that conquered Al-Andalus — they are poor kingdoms that the conquest of Al-Andalus transformed into maritime powers.**

The Reconquista is the founding theft. The knowledge of Cordoba is the spoils. And those spoils built the first two global empires in history.
