## 1. Making a truth heard without accusing {#s1}

There are times when speech exposes the one who utters it. Criticising the powerful, challenging an authority or announcing another order of the world can then become dangerous. Story offers a possibility: to make a truth heard without presenting it straight away as an accusation. A lion speaks, a blind man recovers his sight, a tree withers. The story holds the attention; its deeper meaning waits to be recognised.

It is from this function of story that La Fontaine's fables and certain gospel miracles can be brought together. The comparison is a reading hypothesis: it invites us to look at how a story can pass on a teaching while veiling it.

---

## 2. La Fontaine and the fall of Fouquet {#s2}

Jean de La Fontaine knew at first hand how fragile patronage was and how violent royal decisions could be. Born in 1621, he became the protégé of Nicolas Fouquet, Louis XIV's superintendent of finances and a patron of the arts. When Fouquet was arrested in 1661, the poet remained loyal to his benefactor. In the *Élégie aux nymphes de Vaux*, then in the *Ode au roi*, he begged the sovereign for clemency. This experience gave him a concrete knowledge of a world in which favour could turn into disgrace (Château de Versailles, “Jean de La Fontaine”).

---

## 3. Animals as masks of power {#s3}

La Fontaine did not, however, invent talking animals to answer this situation alone: he took up an ancient tradition, notably that of Aesop and Phaedrus. But that tradition gave him a precious freedom. The lion can embody sovereign power, the fox flattery, the donkey the vulnerability of someone who has no protection. Fiction lets the reader recognise human behaviour without each character being named. In his preface, La Fontaine explicitly links the fable to moral instruction (Preface to the *Fables*).

In *The Animals Sick of the Plague*, the lion's crimes find defenders, while the donkey's trifling fault brings about his condemnation. Through this animal assembly a justice subject to the balance of power comes into view. The detour makes the criticism shareable and memorable. It can also provide a protective distance, even though the closing moral states the injustice plainly.

## 4. Jesus and the first disciples under threat {#s4}

The context of Jesus and the first disciples makes it possible to imagine a comparable function for indirect speech. Jesus taught in a society under Roman rule, shot through with political and religious tensions. His crucifixion under Pontius Pilate gives this history a horizon of violence. After his death, his disciples also faced repression: Paul mentions his imprisonments and the beatings he received; Acts tells of the execution of James, brother of John, and the arrest of Peter. These testimonies refer to different situations rather than to a uniform and permanent persecution (Tacitus, *Annals* XV.44; 2 Corinthians 11; Acts 12).

In such an environment, one may suppose that symbolic narrative sometimes made it possible to convey risky claims about authority, deliverance or the legitimacy of institutions. Yet the life of Jesus, the transmission of his traditions and the writing of the gospels must be kept apart: the authors may give the actions they narrate a significance that also answers the concerns of their communities.

---

## 5. Teaching stated, teaching staged {#s5}

On the hypothesis of a midrashic reading, the Scriptures then form a reservoir of figures, situations and words from which meaning can be built. The reader recognises an old story within a new one. The loaves distributed to the crowd thus recall Elisha feeding a hundred people with too little food, of which some was nonetheless left over. Through this echo, the gospel narrative can teach something about Jesus' identity (2 Kings 4:42-44).

One can therefore propose a distinction between **teaching that is stated and teaching that is staged**. Some sayings set out a demand directly; some narrated actions give that demand a symbolic form. The distinction remains flexible, since Jesus also speaks in parables. Mark even presents the parables as a place where meaning is revealed to some while remaining obscure to others. The veil thus belongs to the way the story works. Its function may be to protect a word, but also to prompt a search and to put understanding to the test (Mark 4:10-13).

---

## 6. The withered fig tree {#s6}

The withered fig tree offers a particularly suggestive example. In Mark 11, Jesus looks for fruit on a tree covered with leaves. He finds none, although the narrator points out that it is not the season for figs. Jesus speaks a word against the tree; the next day, the disciples find it withered.

The arrangement of the passage invites us to interpret this action: between the word addressed to the fig tree and the discovery that it has withered, Mark places Jesus' intervention in the Temple. The tree can therefore be read as the image of a religious authority whose appearance promises fruit it does not produce. The leaves would evoke visibility and prestige; the lack of fruit, a failure of its mission. This reading targets an institution and its leaders within the conflict being narrated, and gives no licence to condemn Judaism or the Jewish people as a whole. The passage also develops a teaching on faith, prayer and forgiveness (Mark 11:12-25).

From this perspective, the fig tree can play a role comparable to that of the lion in La Fontaine: a concrete figure carries a judgement on the exercise of power. The tree makes visible a barrenness that the story asks the reader to recognise elsewhere.

---

## 7. The blind man and Legion {#s7}

Other miracles allow a similar approach. In John 9, the healing of the blind man becomes a question about those who see and those who refuse to understand. In Mark 5, the name “Legion” given to the demons can raise a military echo and open a political reading of deliverance, without being enough to establish a precise code in which every detail would point to Rome.

---

## 8. Conclusion: going beyond the surface of the story {#s8}

The hypothesis can thus be put as follows: **some gospel miracles may be teachings in narrative form, whose deeper meaning becomes accessible through the Scriptures, symbols and the context of their first readers.** Threat might explain part of this veiled expression; pedagogy and theology might explain other dimensions of it.

In La Fontaine as in this reading of the gospels, understanding therefore means going beyond the surface of the story. The reader meets an animal, a healing or a tree; through them, they are invited to recognise a truth that concerns their way of living, believing and obeying.
