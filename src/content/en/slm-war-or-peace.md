> **Author's note on the word "religion".** I use the word "religion" in this article — including in the title — purely for convenience of translation; in the body I set it in *italics* to mark each use. Yet the word does not exist in the ancient Semitic world: no Semitic language designates a confessional institution separate from life. What exists is the path, the way — Hebrew *dérekh* (דֶּרֶךְ), Arabic *ṭarīq* (طَرِيق) — and above all *dîn* (Arabic دِين), the path of justice and of debt owed to God, which embraces at once worship, law and way of living. Qur'an 109:6 says it in so many words: "to you your *dîn*, to me my *dîn*" — not "your religion" but "your path". When I say "religion of war" or "religion of peace," I therefore name two possible directions of a single path — the S-L-M path, that of peace (*salām*) — never an essence. The modern category of "religion" is a late import, via Latin *religio*.

## 1. Introduction {#s1}

A word that names a *religion* carries within it the whole question asked of it. If "islām" reads as "peace" (*salām*), a *religion* of war seems contradicted by its name. Yet the root S-L-M is not univocal: attested also in an opposite use — down to the chaos carried within names like Salome or Absalom —, it forms a "dual" word, a "dual use" term. The quarrel among interpreters is sterile as long as it looks in the word for a single answer the word does not contain. This article develops the war argument in its strongest form, the peace argument in its strongest form, and then confronts both with the decisive test: the etymological document. The root S-L-M does not decide between war and peace; it names something other than both, and that is precisely why both arguments are simultaneously sustainable.

Plan: (1) the structural argument for war, (2) the eschatological argument for peace, (3) the semantic core of the root, (4) the biblical attestation through the bearers of the name, (5) conclusion.

## 2. The "religion of war" argument {#s2}

The serious argument is not "there is violence in the texts": there is some in every founding religious corpus, and this observation distinguishes nothing. The serious argument is structural: war there holds the status of a legal norm, not a historical accident.

Three observables ground this reading:

1. Jihad has a legal status — conditions for opening it, rules of conduct, distribution of booty, truce regimes. No other founding corpus elevates war to an institution of positive law with that precision; the comparison with the war books of the Hebrew Bible is instructive: those narrate wars, this one codifies them.
2. The community defines itself territorially: *dār al-islām*, the abode where *islām* (the pacified whole) reigns, against *dār al-ḥarb*, the abode of war. The border is not confessional but political: the outside world is not a world to be converted but a world awaiting imposed peace.
3. Expansion is constitutive and not missionary in the Christian sense: the peoples of the Book are not required to convert; they are required to political *islām* — allegiance, the *jizya*, *dhimmi* status. The aim is order, not intimate adherence.

Mechanism: war is not an excess of this *religion*; it is an organ of its legal order.

**Concrete examples.**

- The distribution of booty is quantified in the text itself: Qur'an 8:41 assigns "one fifth" of any gain to God, the Messenger, his kindred, orphans, the poor and the traveller.
- The status of the peoples of the Book is set by Qur'an 9:29: those fought among them pay the *jizya* "from their own hand, in a state of humiliation," in exchange for protection and exemption from military service; concretely, in seventh-century Syria the tax was fixed at one gold dinar per adult free man per year (the tradition of the pact of ʿUmar).
- The conquest of Jerusalem in 638 illustrates the mechanism: Caliph ʿUmar concluded a pact with the patriarch Sophronius leaving Christians their churches and their worship in return for the *jizya*.
- The truce (*hudna*) is a negotiated institution: the treaty of al-Ḥudaybiyya (628) fixed a ten-year truce, with pilgrimage conditions and the exchange of fugitives.
- The division of the world into *dār al-islām*, *dār al-ḥarb* and *dār al-ṣulḥ* (abode of truce) was systematized by classical jurists, for example al-Māwardī in *al-Aḥkām al-suṭāniyya* (eleventh century).

## 3. The "religion of peace" argument {#s3}

The symmetrical argument is not sentimental — "Islam is a *religion* of love" — it is theological and eschatological:

1. *Al-Salām* is a divine name (Qur'an 59:23). Peace there is not a state between nations, the result of balances of power: it is an attribute of God himself.
2. The obligatory greeting between believers is *salām*. Every social interaction repeats peace as the underlying norm; the community greets one another by naming what God is.
3. "No coercion in *religion*" (Qur'an 2:256 — where the Arabic word is *dîn*, the path); the treaty of *al-Ḥudaybiyya*, accepted from a position of weakness and apparently to disadvantage, becomes a "clear victory" in the text; eschatology is a state in which war ceases. Peace is the end, war the regulated exception.

Mechanism: peace is the end there, war the bounded means — which is the very definition of a regulated *religion* of peace.

**Concrete examples.**

- Qur'an 59:23 lists *As-Salām* — "The Peace" — among the names of God, in sūrat al-Ḥashr.
- The daily greeting "*as-salāmu ʿalaykum*" ("peace be upon you") is built on the same root and repeated at every meeting between believers.
- Qur'an 8:61 prescribes the opposite of escalation: "if the enemy inclines to peace, incline to peace and trust in God."
- Qur'an 2:256 applies concretely: the Christian and Jewish communities of the Levant, brought under Muslim rule after 636-642, were not compelled to convert and kept their worship.
- The treaty of al-Ḥudaybiyya (628), signed from a position of weakness, is called a "clear victory" by the text (Qur'an 48:1).
- Qur'anic eschatology culminates in the "reassured soul" (*an-nafs al-muṭma'inna*) of Qur'an 89:27-28, which "returns to its Lord, well-pleased and pleasing": peace as the final state.

The two arguments are therefore internal, coherent, and each supported by the sources. That is the sign that the question is badly put.

## 4. The decisive argument: the semantic core of the root S-L-M {#s4}

The Semitic root S-L-M means neither "peace" nor "war." It means totality, integrity, the state of what is whole. Two major derivatives proceed from it:

- *shalom* / *salām*: the state of totality attained — the completion, whence the ordinary sense of "peace";
- *islām*: the act by which one enters this state — *aslama*, "to hand over peace," to restore *salām*; the root does not mean "submission" in the sense of capitulation.

Peace and war are therefore not two opposite meanings of the root, but two ways toward the same referent. Totality is restored in two ways: by adherence, when the whole accepts entering into the whole — that is *salām*; by reduction, when the outside must be forcibly brought back into the whole — that is *islām* in the military sense carried by the classical lexicons. The root names the criterion — the integrity of the whole — and leaves the means open.

**The word is dual.** The same S-L-M is attested in its two opposite uses: *salām* designates peace, and yet bearers of the name produce chaos — Absalom raises the tribes against his father, Salome demands a head on a platter. Today one would say, in military terms, "dual use": the same root serves the state of peace and its opposite, conquered peace. This is not a contradiction of the language but its structure: the root names totality, and totality can be achieved by welcome or by reduction.

This mechanism explains why the two preceding arguments are simultaneously valid: each describes one hand of the root. The war argument describes the hand that seizes; the peace argument describes the hand that welcomes. Neither can be denied without mutilating the other.

**Concrete examples of the root.**

- In the Amarna letters (fourteenth century BCE), written in Akkadian, the greeting formula is *šulmu ša šarri* — "peace to the king": the root S-L-M already designates there the state of wholeness wished for.
- In biblical Hebrew, *Shalem* is the city of King Melchizedek (Genesis 14:18), and *Yerushalayim* (Jerusalem) contains the same root.
- In Arabic, the same root produces *salām* (the greeting of peace), *aslama* (to hand over peace), *islām* (the act of restoring peace), *muslim* (the one who restores peace).

## 5. The bearers of the name: the biblical attestation {#s5}

The Bible provides the control corpus: figures bear the root, and their destinies distribute exactly the two branches.

| Bearer | Etymology | Destiny |
|---|---|---|
| Solomon (Shelomo) | "his peace," a derivative of *shalom* | Positive apex: Temple, wisdom, peace — but forced labor and schism already contained |
| Melchizedek, king of Shalem | *shalem* = peace | Positive apex: bread and wine offered to Abram, blessing (Genesis 14:18) |
| Absalom (Avishalom) | "my father is peace" | Negative apex: civil war against his own father David |
| Salome | from *shalom* | Demands the head of John the Baptist |

An anomaly not to be smoothed over — it is the heart of the attestation: the bearers of the name of peace are distributed between the summit of blessing (Solomon, the Temple, wisdom) and the summit of chaos (Absalom, civil war against his own father; Salome, the head demanded on a platter). Salome and Absalom carry chaos within them under a name of peace. If "peace" were the full and only meaning of the name, this distribution would be improbable; it becomes necessary as soon as one reads the word as "dual" ("dual use"): to bear S-L-M is to bear totality — and totality is realized as much in building the Temple as in raising the tribes against one's father or in demanding a head. The name guarantees the stake, never the means.

This biblical distribution is the strongest external attestation of the etymological thesis: it shows the root at work, outside any Qur'anic debate, producing opposite destinies under one and the same name.

## 6. Conclusion {#s6}

The question "*religion* of war or of peace?" assumes that the root contains the answer. It contains the question. S-L-M defines the state aimed at — the whole order of the world under God — and designates simultaneously the two operations that lead to it: peace when totality is accepted, imposed *islām* when it must be forcibly brought back. To say "islām" is therefore neither to bless nor to curse: it is to name the demand for totality, of which war and peace are the two hands.

This result opens onto a structural parallel with the K-F-R series: that root named exclusion and carried redemption within it (*kafir* / *kippur*); S-L-M names total inclusion and carries its own end within it. Two roots, one and the same mechanism: the word does not decide, it measures.
