**Type**: Strategic analysis — cultural transfer
**Thesis**: Courtly love, the lute, lyric song — the foundations of refined European culture — were born in Cordoba. Ziryab (9th century) transformed the material civilization of Al-Andalus, and Andalusian poetic forms (*muwashshah*, *zajal*) migrated to Provence, becoming the poetry of the troubadours. The culture that Europe considers its essence — the art of love, music, refinement — is an Arab inheritance it has forgotten.

---

## 1. Ziryab: the man who civilized Cordoba (789-857) {#s1}

### The arrival

Abu l-Hasan Ali ibn Nafi', nicknamed **Ziryab** ("blackbird," for his voice and complexion), arrived in Cordoba in **822**, driven from Baghdad by the jealousy of his master, the musician Ishaq al-Mawsili. Emir Abd al-Rahman II welcomed him with a salary of 200 gold dinars per month.

### What he transformed

| Domain | Ziryab's innovation | European legacy |
|--------|---------------------|-----------------|
| **Music** | Added a 5th string to the oud (4 strings -> 5), enabling new tonalities | The 5-string oud became the **lute** (*al-ʿūd* -> *laúd* -> *luth* -> *lute*) — central instrument of European music until the 18th c. |
| **Fashion** | Introduced seasonal clothing: white in summer, bright colors in spring, furs in winter | First seasonal fashion system in Europe |
| **Gastronomy** | Three-course service (soup -> main course -> dessert), white tablecloth, crystal glassware | French-style service descends from Ziryab's protocol |
| **Hygiene** | Toothpaste, deodorant, shampoo, regular baths | "European" personal hygiene is an Andalusian import |
| **Hairdressing** | Short hair with bangs, clean shaving | Break with long hair and untrimmed beards |
| **Conservatory** | Created the first structured music school in Western Europe, in Cordoba | Pedagogical model later adopted |

Ziryab did not merely transform music — he transformed **the art of living**. He made Cordoba the capital of Western refinement, rivaling Baghdad and Constantinople.

## 2. The oud -> the lute: a lexical and musical lineage {#s2}

```
Oud ('ud, "wood") — Baghdad/Cordoba
    -> Laud (Spanish)
        -> Liuto (Italian)
            -> Luth (French)
                -> Lute (English)
                    -> Laute (German)
```

The **lute** dominated European music from the 13th to the 18th century. Bach composed for the lute. The instrument of the Renaissance and Baroque is a transformed Andalusian oud.

Other instruments of Andalusian/Arab origin:
- **Guitar**: from *qithara* (itself from Greek *kithara*, but the modern guitar descends from the Moorish guitar)
- **Rebec** -> violin: from *rabab* (Arab bowed instrument)
- **Drum**: from *tunbur*
- **Naker** -> kettledrums: from *naqqara*

## 3. From the muwashshah to the troubadours: the poetic lineage thesis {#s3}

### Andalusian poetic forms

The **muwashshah** (invented in Cordoba in the 9th-10th century) is a strophic form with a refrain, cross-rhymes, and a *kharja* (final envoi) often in dialect or Mozarabic Romance. The **zajal** (popularized by Ibn Quzman, Cordoba, 12th century) is its popular version in dialectal Arabic.

These forms break radically with classical Arabic poetry (the monolithic *qasida* in long verses): they introduce the strophe, the refrain, the mixing of registers, and above all the **theme of impossible love for an inaccessible lady**.

### The Ribera-Nykl-Menocal thesis

| Scholar | Thesis | Date |
|---------|--------|------|
| **Julian Ribera** | The music of the Provencal troubadours derives directly from Andalusian melodies | 1922 |
| **A.R. Nykl** | The concept of courtly *amor* is a calque of *ishq 'udhri* (Arab platonic love, named after the Banu 'Udhra tribe) | 1946 |
| **Maria Rosa Menocal** | The first troubadour, William IX of Aquitaine, had direct contacts with Muslim Spain (his father had Muslim prisoners) | 1987 |
| **Samuel Stern** | The Mozarabic *kharjas* (11th c.) are the oldest lyric texts in a Romance language | 1948 |

### The troubling convergences

| Element | Andalusian poetry | Provencal troubadours |
|---------|-------------------|----------------------|
| **Structure** | Strophes with refrain (muwashshah) | Strophes with refrain (*canso*) |
| **Central theme** | Platonic love for an inaccessible lady (*ishq 'udhri*) | Courtly love for an inaccessible lady (*fin'amor*) |
| **Poet's stance** | Servant of the beloved, suffering elevated to art | Servant of the lady, sublimated suffering |
| **First location** | Cordoba/Seville (9th-11th c.) | Aquitaine/Provence (late 11th c.) |
| **Geographic proximity** | Al-Andalus | Just north of the Pyrenees |
| **Chronology** | The muwashshah precedes the troubadour by at least 150 years | William IX of Aquitaine composes around 1100 |

### The counter-thesis

Medievalists (Dronke, Boase) argue that courtly love may have independent sources (Ovid, Latin poetry, feudal culture). The debate remains open, but the chronological precedence and geographic proximity weigh heavily.

## 4. From courtly love to the European novel {#s4}

If the lineage holds, the consequences are dizzying:

```
Ishq 'udhri (Arab poetry)
    -> Muwashshah/Zajal (Cordoba, 9th-12th c.)
        -> Provencal troubadours (11th-13th c.)
            -> Courtly romance (Chretien de Troyes, 12th c.)
                -> Dante (Vita nuova, Divine Comedy)
                    -> Petrarch (Canzoniere)
                        -> Ronsard, Shakespeare (sonnets)
                            -> Modern novel
```

Love as the central literary subject of Europe — from Tristan and Isolde to Romeo and Juliet — would be an Arabo-Andalusian inheritance.

## 5. The colonial irony {#s5}

When the French colonized the Maghreb, they brought a "superior culture" whose musical foundations (the lute), poetic foundations (courtly love), and gastronomic foundations (table service) were inheritances from the very world they were colonizing. The "civilizing mission" carried, unknowingly, a civilization that Ziryab had invented a thousand years earlier in Cordoba.
