## 1. Introduction {#s1}

"Where does what I believe come from?" This simple question is rarely asked of religions that present themselves as revealed. It is taken for granted that the Qur'an came down from heaven, that the hadiths — those tens of thousands of reports attributed to the Prophet Muhammad — transmit his words and deeds, and that the whole forms a coherent edifice. But what happens when these traditions are examined not as historical archives but as literary texts, comparable to other texts of late antiquity? What does one discover when the hadith is placed back within the great current of learning — Greek, Jewish, Christian, pre-Islamic Arab — that ran through the Near East at the time of its formation?

This article, the first part of a dossier devoted to the literary origins of the hadith, presents the results of an inquiry conducted on sixteen concrete cases, chosen for their capacity to reveal the mechanisms by which the tradition was made. The point is not to pronounce "true" or "false," but to understand how a corpus of texts is built, how it rearranges earlier materials, and how it invents an original mode of authorization that belongs properly neither to the archive nor to forgery.

The plan is as follows. We begin by setting out the central hypothesis and the methodological framework. We then present the sixteen cases, grouped into three families according to the type of operation they illustrate: borrowing and adaptation, *drash* in the strong sense, and the limit-cases that reveal the rule. A synthesis draws the lessons of the dossier, before a conclusion that opens onto the questions these results pose to contemporary scholarship.

## 2. The framework of the inquiry {#s2}

### The hypothesis

The hadith corpus is neither a historical archive nor a forgery. It is the product of an interpretive gesture — a rearrangement of ancient knowledge and literary materials (Greek, Jewish, Christian, pre-Islamic Arab) placed under a new authority. This gesture is analogous to the midrashic gesture practised by the Jewish rabbis, but within the economy of Islam it occupies the structural position that the Mishnah occupies in rabbinic Judaism — not that of the midrash.

To put it differently: in Judaism, the midrash comments on a verse of the Torah; in Islam, it is the *tafsīr* (Qur'anic commentary) that plays this role. The hadith, for its part, does not comment on a verse: it states norms, narratives and doctrines organized by legal topic — exactly as the Mishnah organizes Jewish law without following the order of the biblical verses. The hadith is mishnaic, not midrashic.

### The structural table

| Function | Rabbinic Judaism | Islam |
|---|---|---|
| Lemmatic organization (follows the verse) | Midrash (*Mekhilta*, *Sifra*, *Rabbah*) | *Tafsīr bi-l-maʾthūr* |
| Thematic organization (follows the chapters of the law) | Mishnah, Tosefta | *Muṣannaf* (Bukhārī, Muslim, Mālik) |
| Layer of commentary | Gemara | *Sharḥ* (Ibn Ḥajar, al-Nawawī) |
| Guarantor of transmission | "Rabbi X said in the name of Rabbi Y" | *Isnād* ("so-and-so reported to us, on the authority of so-and-so…") |

The methodological consequence is decisive: the hadith has no lemma. Where the midrash is anchored in a verse, the hadith is anchored in a chain of transmitters. It is this substitution — the lemma replaced by the chain — that constitutes the proper object of the inquiry.

An important corollary: when a hadith recovers a scriptural anchor (when it harmonizes a received material with a Qur'anic verse), we are dealing with *drash* in the strong sense, no longer with mere mishnaic transmission. Cases B, H, I and L below are of this type — they are the most probative in the dossier.

### The analytical grid

Each case is examined according to the same six-point grid:

1. **The hadith** — references, text, canonical status.
2. **The prior material** — identified source, reference, degree of certainty of the filiation.
3. **The type of operation** — *borrowing* (diffusion of a topos, no interpretive gesture), *adaptation* (Islamization of the setting, function unchanged), *drash* (scriptural anchor + formal operation + shift of meaning), or *borrowing of form* (the mould is received, the content is proper).
4. **The formal marks** — narrative amplification, families of variants, play on the root, numerical transformation, intertextual harmonization.
5. **The substituted anchor** — which guarantor replaces which guarantor.
6. **The function** in the present of the redaction.

### State of research and point of originality

The late date of the hadith corpus and its juridico-political function have been established results of Orientalist scholarship since the end of the nineteenth century. Ignác Goldziher (*Muhammedanische Studien*, 1890) showed that many hadiths reflect the theological and political debates of the second and third centuries of the Hijra rather than the actual words of the Prophet. Joseph Schacht (*Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence*, 1950) demonstrated the backward growth of the chains of transmission: the further back one goes in time, the longer and more systematic the chains become, which suggests that they were fabricated retrospectively. G. H. A. Juynboll (*Muslim Tradition*, 1983) refined the "common link" method for identifying the branching points of the chains. Schacht's dating has been contested by Harald Motzki, who holds that certain traditions may be older than Schacht allowed.

This paradigm is judicial: it speaks of authenticity and falsification. Goldziher himself noted the kinship between hadith and *haggadah* (the Jewish homiletic narrative) and catalogued rabbinic sayings that passed into Bukhārī's collection. But he did so within a framework of "Jewish influence" — that is, in terms of borrowed content, not in terms of literary genre.

Structural comparison has been carried out on the side of narrative: Uri Rubin (*Between Bible and Qurʾān*, 1999), Shari Lowin (*The Making of a Forefather*, 2006) and Roberto Tottoli (on the stories of the prophets) have compared Islamic and Jewish narrative traditions. But these works bear on the exegetical narrative corpus, not on the canonical hadith as a form.

The point of originality of the present inquiry is this: to treat the hadith neither as borrowed content nor as a fake, but as a *regime of authorization* — and to pose the question of the migration of the guarantor of truth (from the biblical verse to the chain of transmitters, then to the imam) as an object in its own right.

## 3. The cases: borrowings, adaptations and drash {#s3}

### Case A — The fly: medical knowledge turned prophetic saying

**The hadith.** "If a fly falls into the drink of one of you, let him dip it in entirely and then take it out, for in one of its wings there is disease and in the other a cure." Reported by Bukhārī (in the book of the *Beginning of Creation*), Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Māja. Status: fully canonical (*ṣaḥīḥ*) — which is what makes it interesting.

**The prior material.** Oded Zinger, in an article published in 2016 in the journal *Arabica*, proposed the cantharid — the famous "Spanish fly" of Greco-Roman medical literature. Pliny the Elder describes an insect that is at once a violent poison and a therapeutic principle once compounded. Zinger explicitly rules out a late-antique Jewish origin. The structure is that of the *pharmakon*: the remedy is drawn from the venom, as in theriac.

**The operation.** Adaptation, not *drash*. No scriptural anchor, no operation on the letter of a text. A piece of Greek medical knowledge becomes a prophetic saying by a simple change of guarantor: the authority of the physician is replaced by the chain of transmission. Nothing of the original guarantor survives.

**The formal marks.** The binary symmetry (one wing carries the disease, the other the cure) is a mnemonic shaping typical of oral tradition, absent from the Greek medical source. It is the signature of narrative work, not of mere transcription.

**The function.** To found a prophetic competence in matters of nature. This hadith is the stem cell of the genre known as "prophetic medicine" (*al-ṭibb al-nabawī*), developed by authors such as Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya or al-Dhahabī, who systematically back-project Galenic humoral medicine into the mouth of the Prophet. Zinger further shows how medieval scholars had to defend this hadith against the Greco-Islamic science of their day: the seam between the borrowed knowledge and the new guarantor remains visible.

**Verdict.** Plausible and documented filiation, but a weak operation. This case proves rearrangement, not *drash*. It is the best pedagogical entry point for understanding the method, and the worst theoretical argument for demonstrating the thesis. To be used as such.

### Case B — The five keys of the unseen: a complete drash

**The hadith.** "The keys of the unseen are five": the final Hour; the descent of the rain; what is in the wombs; what each will earn tomorrow; in what land he will die. Reported by Bukhārī.

**The prior material.** The Babylonian Talmud (tractate *Taʿanit* 2a): Rabbi Yoḥanan teaches that there are three keys God has entrusted to no messenger — the key of rain, the key of the woman in childbirth, and the key of the resurrection of the dead.

**The operation.** *Drash* in the strong sense. Three operations are superimposed:

1. *Anchoring*: the hadith does not simply take over the rabbinic list. It reads it against Qur'an 31:34, which enumerates precisely five objects reserved to divine knowledge, and borrows its title from Qur'an 6:59: "With Him are the keys of the unseen." The Qur'anic lemma is indeed there.
2. *Numerical transformation*: the list goes from three to five, under the constraint of the verse. This is a classic midrashic procedure: the received list is readjusted to the number in the sacred text.
3. *Shift of meaning*: for Rabbi Yoḥanan, the keys express the inalienability of a power — God has delegated these keys to no one. In the hadith, they express the inaccessibility of a knowledge — man cannot know these things. One passes from the register of power to that of knowledge. This is exactly the theological shift to be expected in a context where the question of prophetic foreknowledge is disputed.

**The formal marks.** The metaphor of the key is preserved. Two of the three rabbinic items are retained (the rain; the woman in childbirth, become "what is in the wombs"). The third (the resurrection) is dropped, and two properly Qur'anic items are added (tomorrow's earnings, the place of death). This profile — core preserved, periphery recomposed under the constraint of the verse — is the signature of exegetical work, not of simple borrowing.

**The anchor.** Here the anchor is restored: the chain of transmitters leans on the Qur'anic verse. The hadith functions as a commentary (*tafsīr*) disguised as tradition (*sunna*).

**The function.** To close, by way of tradition, a question the Qur'an leaves open: how far does the Prophet's knowledge extend? The answer is restrictive, and serves to limit claims to foreknowledge.

**Verdict.** The central case of the dossier. If the filiation from *Taʿanit* to the hadith is confirmed, we have a complete demonstration: lemma + received material + formal operation + theological shift.

### Case C — The gharqad: narrative amplification

**The hadith.** On the Day of Judgment, the stones and the trees denounce the Jews hiding behind them, except the *gharqad* tree, which is the tree of the Jews. Reported by Muslim; a shorter, earlier version is found in Bukhārī.

**The prior material.** Late-antique apocalyptic stock, probably of Syriac origin.

**The operation.** Adaptation with narrative amplification.

**The formal marks.** It is here that the dossier finds its proof of textual growth: the short version in Bukhārī is narratively enriched in Muslim. The *gharqad* tree appears in the amplified version. The botanical uncertainty about the identity of this tree and the absence of any Qur'anic basis confirm that it is a traditional addition. The text grows between two canonical collections: this is the behaviour of an aggadic tradition (homiletic narrative), not of a verbatim record.

**The function.** Eschatological. This hadith was never treated as a legal norm by the classical commentators — until its literal citation in the Hamas Charter (1988), which performs precisely the illegitimate conversion of an aggadic narrative into a legal norm (*halakha*).

**Verdict.** The value of this case is methodological: it supplies the formal mark of amplification between recensions, which we shall look for in the other cases.

### Case D — The logia of Jesus: annexing a rival authority

**The corpus.** Around three hundred sayings attributed to Jesus in the Islamic literature of asceticism (*zuhd*) and belles-lettres (*adab*), catalogued by Tarif Khalidi (*The Muslim Jesus*, 2001). Status: marginal in the canonical (*ṣaḥīḥ*) collections, central in ascetic and Sufi literature.

**The prior material.** Rewritten Gospel logia; apophthegms of the Desert Fathers; Syriac monastic wisdom.

**The operation.** Adaptation predominates, with cases of ascetic *drash*: the Gospel saying is bent toward a doctrine of detachment proper to *zuhd*.

**The anchor.** A remarkable case: the guarantor remains Jesus. This is therefore not a substitution but an annexation. The Islamic corpus appropriates a rival authority without erasing it. This is an intermediate position between anonymous borrowing (case A) and prophetic attribution.

**The function.** To give nascent Muslim asceticism a genealogy, at a moment when Christian monasticism remains the available model of renunciation.

**Verdict.** The richest case quantitatively, the least advanced analytically. A worksite in its own right.

### Case E — "I was a hidden treasure": the avowed drash

**The hadith.** "I was a hidden treasure; I loved to be known; therefore I created creation in order to be known." This *hadith qudsī* (a reported divine utterance) is absent from every canonical collection. Al-Sakhāwī and al-ʿAjlūnī note that it has no chain of transmission. It is nevertheless universally cited by the Sufis.

**The prior material.** The Neoplatonic and Gnostic stock of emanation: the One diffuses itself out of love of knowledge.

**The operation.** Pure *drash*, and avowed as such. Ibn ʿArabī maintains that traditions can be authenticated by unveiling (*kashf*) rather than by the chain of transmitters. The drashic criterion enters into open competition with the philological criterion.

**The anchor.** Neither lemma nor chain: the experience of the interpreter. This is a fourth position of the guarantor.

**The function.** To found a cosmology of love and knowledge that has no direct Qur'anic support.

**Verdict.** A limit-case that reveals the rule. The system itself produces traditions that everyone knows are not "authentic" and that are nevertheless held to be true. The category of authenticity therefore does not account for what the corpus does.

### Case F — The meta-clue: the system's internal admission

This is not a case but a fact of doctrine, and it is perhaps the strongest argument in the dossier.

The classical rule — attributed to Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Mahdī, codified by Ibn Ḥajar — holds that a weak hadith (*ḍaʿīf*) is admissible for moral exhortation (the merits of deeds, encouragement and deterrence), but inadmissible in law.

In other words: the tradition has a threshold of veracity that varies with the function of the text. The strict regime applies to the legal norm (*halakha*), a loose regime to the homiletic narrative (*aggadah*). This is the explicit recognition, by the system itself, that part of its corpus does not operate in the mode of historical attestation.

One must add the category of the *isrāʾīliyyāt*: the tradition names and classifies the Jewish and Christian materials it integrates. It never believed it was creating *ex nihilo*.

### Case G — ʿĀshūrāʾ: the tradition that narrates its own borrowing

**The hadith.** "When the Prophet arrived in Medina, he saw that the Jews observed the tenth day of Muḥarram and fasted on that day. He said: We have more right to this day than you, and he ordered that it be fasted." (Bukhārī 3942.)

**The prior material.** The fast of the tenth of Tishri — Yom Kippur. Leviticus 16:29 and 23:27 prescribe: "on the tenth day of the month."

**The operation.** *Drash* in the strong sense, and in its rarest mode: it is the etymology that carries the operation. *ʿĀshūrāʾ* is an Aramaic word — Syriac *ʿasorā* means "tenth" — from the same root as Hebrew *ʿasor*. The Arabic name preserves the Jewish date in its very form. This is not a concealed borrowing: the word says where it comes from.

**The formal marks.** Two, equally telling:

1. *The reversal of the claim*: *naḥnu aḥaqqu*, "we have more right." The formula that authorizes the borrowing is the very one that delegitimizes it as a borrowing. This is a supersessionist gesture in its chemically pure state — and an explicit one.
2. *The calendrical decoupling*: the shift to a purely lunar calendar detaches ʿĀshūrāʾ from Tishri. The fast becomes supererogatory after the break with the Jewish tribes of Medina. The tradition preserves the trace of both moments: adoption and loosening.

**The anchor.** No Qur'anic lemma. But the authority invoked is not the Prophet alone: it is Moses, laid claim to. The guarantor is not replaced; it is disputed.

**The function.** To found a practice by claiming it from its holders rather than by denying it.

**Verdict.** The most eloquent case in the dossier for a non-specialist audience: the tradition itself stages the appropriation — the date, the source, the argument. There is nothing to demonstrate, only to read.

### Case H — Fifty prayers brought down to five: a norm in narrative

**The hadith.** During the night ascension (*miʿrāj*), fifty prayers are prescribed. Moses sends the Prophet back to God: "Return to your Lord, your community will not be able to bear it." The number decreases by steps — fifty, twenty-five, twelve, six — down to five. Final divine answer: "They are five prayers, and they are worth fifty; my word does not change." (Bukhārī.)

**The prior material.** Two sources:

1. The negotiating Moses of the rabbinic *aggadah*, who argues with God and makes him reverse his decision. In the Talmud (*Berakhot* 32a), Moses "seized the Holy One, blessed be He, by his garment" and refused to let go.
2. The topos of tenfold reward, anchored here in Qur'an 6:160: "whoever comes with a good deed shall have ten like it."

**The operation.** *Drash* in the strong sense. The narrative resolves a legal contradiction: how can an obligation be both fixed by God and modified? Answer: it is not. Five times ten is fifty. The word does not change. Arithmetic saves divine immutability.

**The formal marks.** Numerical transformation under the constraint of a verse — the same signature as in case B. And a borrowed character-function: Moses is the one who knows from experience that a community does not keep its obligations. The allusion to the weight of the Mosaic Law is transparent.

**The anchor.** Restored: verse 6:160 holds the calculation together.

**The function.** An aetiology of the number five — which the Qur'an nowhere prescribes. This is the canonical example of a norm without a lemma, later endowed with a founding narrative: exactly what the *aggadah* does for the *halakha*.

**Verdict.** The second strong piece in the dossier, and the most demonstrative as to function: here the narrative does not decorate the norm, it manufactures it.

### Case I — "God created Adam in his image": a biblical lemma grafted onto the Qur'anic lexicon

**The hadith.** "God created Adam in his image (*ṣūra*), his height being sixty cubits." (Bukhārī 6227.)

**The prior material.** Genesis 1:26-27: "in our image, after our likeness." The passage through the Arabic root Ṣ-W-R is decisive: Arabic *ṣūra* answers to Hebrew *tsurah* (form), and the Qur'an knows this root perfectly well — "He formed you and made your forms beautiful" (Qur'an 64:3) — as well as the divine name *al-Muṣawwir*, "the Fashioner." The hadith plugs the Genesis verse into the Qur'anic lexicon of formation.

**The operation.** *Drash* in the strong sense, with a heavy theological consequence: the Qur'an affirms divine non-resemblance ("there is nothing like unto Him," Qur'an 42:11). The hadith introduces into the corpus a proposition the Qur'an excludes.

**The formal marks.** Exceptional: the variant betrays the work. Two recensions of the same hadith, in the same compiler, Bukhārī:

| Reference | Text | Clause "in his image" |
|---|---|---|
| Bukhārī 6227 | "in his image (*ṣūratihi*), his height being sixty cubits" | present |
| Bukhārī 3326 | "his height being sixty cubits" | absent |

The same utterance, transmitted by the same Companion (Abū Hurayra), circulates with and without the theologically dangerous proposition. This is the best material proof in the dossier that a clause is added or removed as needed. The centuries of commentary on the antecedent of the pronoun — "his" image: God's? Adam's? — are the fossil trace of this embarrassment.

**The anchor.** Biblical lemma, prophetic guarantor, Qur'anic lexicon: the three planes are superimposed in a single sentence.

**Verdict.** The best case in the dossier after B. It brings together an identifiable lemma, a shared Semitic root, a lexical operation, a theological shift, and material proof by variants.

### Case J — Adam of sixty cubits: the primordial giant

**The hadith.** "Adam measures sixty cubits; men have not ceased to diminish in stature since." (Bukhārī 3326 and 6227.)

**The prior material.** Rabbinic traditions on Adam's cosmic stature, reduced after the fall. The Talmud (*Ḥagigah* 12a): "Adam extended from the earth to the firmament; God laid his hand upon him and diminished him." *Bereshit Rabbah* 8 and 12; Talmud *Sanhedrin* 38b.

**The operation.** Adaptation, with numerical fixation. The *aggadah* gives a giant with no stable figure; the hadith fixes sixty cubits — a sexagesimal number, of Mesopotamian ancestry.

**The formal marks.** The motif preserved is not the height but the continuous decrease — this is the most specific trait of the source, and it is the one that passes across.

**The function.** To inscribe humanity in a degradation from the origin. It is also the implicit argument of every authority founded on antiquity: the Companions are worth more than the Successors, and so on.

**Verdict.** A reinforcing case, whose value lies in its solidarity with case I: it is the same hadith. A single utterance carries both an aggadic borrowing (the height) and a lemmatic *drash* (the image). The composition is stratified.

### Case K — Seventy-three sects: the arithmetic of one-upmanship

**The hadith.** "The Jews divided into seventy-one sects, the Christians into seventy-two, and my community will divide into seventy-three — all in hell save one." (Tirmidhī, Ibn Māja, Abū Dāwūd.) Its authenticity is contested within the tradition itself: Ibn Ḥazm rejects it; Ibn al-Wazīr notes its absence from the two major canonical collections.

**The prior material.** The topos of seventy: the seventy nations of Genesis 10 (seventy-two in the Septuagint), seventy elders, seventy languages, seventy-two translators. Throughout the Semitic world, 70 or 72 is the number of the totality of peoples.

**The operation.** *Drash* in the mode of ordinal one-upmanship: 71, then 72, then 73. Each tradition receives one notch more. The most recent is the most divided — which, in a logic of decadence, is as much an admission as a claim.

**The formal marks.** The series makes sense only relationally: none of the three numbers means anything in isolation; it is the gap of one that carries everything. The signature of a construction, not of an observation.

*Note: the filiation to the topos of the seventy nations is a proposed reading, not a result established by scholarship. It must be investigated before any public use.*

**The function.** To produce orthodoxy by subtraction — to define the saved community as a remainder (*al-firqa al-nājiya*, "the saved group").

**Verdict.** High polemical yield — it is the matrix of all later exclusivisms — and low philological security. To be handled with reserve.

### Case L — The pen: the pre-existent instrument

**The hadith.** "The first thing God created was the pen. He said to it: Write. It said: Lord, what shall I write? He said: Write the decree of all things until the Hour comes." (Tirmidhī.)

**The prior material.** Two distinct strata:

1. *Bereshit Rabbah* 1:1: the pre-existent Torah declares "I was the working tool of the Holy One, blessed be He"; God looks into the Torah and creates the world. The world is created by a writing that precedes it.
2. The heavenly tablets of Enochic literature and the book of *Jubilees*, where the whole decree is inscribed in advance.

**The operation.** *Drash* in the strong sense. The hadith has two Qur'anic lemmas at its disposal: "by the pen and what they inscribe" (Qur'an 68:1) and "the preserved tablet" (Qur'an 85:22). The Qur'an gives the pen and the tablet; the hadith gives them a chronology — the pen first — and a cosmological function that the Qur'an does not formulate.

**The formal marks.** Substitution of instrument: where the *aggadah* places the Torah as the blueprint of the work, the hadith places the writing tool. A remarkable shift: one passes from the text to the instrument, which is consistent with a theology in which the uncreated text is the Qur'an itself and cannot be duplicated.

**The anchor.** Restored, and double (Qur'an 68:1; 85:22).

**The function.** To found *qadar* (the divine decree): the decree is written before the world. This is the cosmological piece of the debate on predestination in the second and third centuries of the Hijra.

**Verdict.** A strong case, and the most interesting theologically: it shows a systemic constraint at work. The received material is adopted, but the element that would compete with the uncreated Qur'an (the pre-existent book) is replaced by a compatible element (the tool). *Drash* is not only transformation: it is transformation under doctrinal constraint.

### Case M — The God of the last third of the night

**The hadith.** "Our Lord descends every night to the nearest heaven, when the last third of the night remains, and says: Who calls upon Me, that I may answer him? Who asks of Me, that I may give him? Who seeks My forgiveness, that I may forgive him?" (Bukhārī, Muslim.)

**The prior material.** The Talmud (*Berakhot* 3a): the night is divided into three watches; at each, God sits and roars like a lion, saying: "Woe to the children, on account of whose sins I destroyed my house…" There follows (3b) the motif of David's harp waking at midnight and of the last watch of the night as the privileged time of prayer.

**The operation.** *Drash*, without a Qur'anic lemma. Three elements pass together and are found nowhere else in this form: the tripartite division of the night, the last watch as the privileged moment, and a God who speaks aloud in the night.

**The formal marks.** The inversion of the content of the nocturnal speech is the signature: among the rabbis, God laments the destruction of the Temple; in the hadith, he offers — answer, gift, forgiveness. The frame is preserved in its entirety; the content is turned from mourning to grace. This is exactly the type of transformation one expects from a tradition that inherits a liturgical structure without inheriting the mourning that motivated it.

**The function.** To found the supererogatory night prayer (*tahajjud*) by giving it a divine respondent.

**Verdict.** A major case, and the most consequential. This hadith is one of the principal battlefields of Islamic theology: the question of the divine descent (*nuzūl*) occupied Muʿtazilites, Ashʿarites and Ḥanbalites for centuries, and still occupies contemporary Salafis. Yet the structure comes from a Jewish liturgical vigil. The anthropomorphism that has cost so much ink is not a datum of revelation: it is an inheritance of form.

### Case N — "Deeds are only by intentions"

**The hadith.** "Deeds are only by intentions, and each has only what he intended." This hadith is the first in Bukhārī's collection, deliberately placed there — as a thesis on law set as an epigraph to the book.

**The prior material.** The rabbinic doctrine of *kavvanah* (directed intention): the Mishnah (*Menaḥot* 13:11) — "whether one offers much or offers little, provided he directs his heart toward Heaven"; the Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 106b) — "the Merciful One demands the heart."

**The operation.** Undecidable — and that is the interest of the case. Three readings are possible: independent convergence (the principle is too general to prove a filiation); a late-antique ethical koine (a principle shared across the whole Near East); *drash*. The second is the most probable hypothesis.

**The function.** To set out the principle that governs the whole of ritual law: the act without intention is void.

**Verdict.** A case that serves as a safeguard. It looks like a borrowing and perhaps is not one. It is kept in the dossier precisely for that reason: a dossier containing only conclusive cases would be suspect. It is here that the distinction between borrowing, koine and *drash* must be defended in practice.

### Case O — The hadith of Gabriel: borrowing the mould

**The hadith.** A stranger sits down before the Prophet and questions him in turn: what is *islām*? what is *īmān* (faith)? what is *iḥsān* (excellence)? when will the Hour come? At each answer the stranger approves — "you have spoken truly." He departs; the Prophet says: "That was Gabriel, come to teach you your religion." (Bukhārī, Muslim.)

**The prior material.** Not a content but a genre: the *erotapokriseis*, the late-antique Christian literature of questions and answers, the catechetical form par excellence. More broadly, the didactic dialogue in which the questioner already knows the answer and asks in order to instruct the audience.

**The operation.** Borrowing of form. Nothing of the content is foreign to Islam; everything of the architecture is. This is a category the initial grid did not anticipate.

**The formal marks.** Three traits of the catechetical genre come together: the questioner who validates the answers, and therefore knows them; the graded progression from the visible to the inward (practice, then faith, then excellence); and the final revelation of the master's identity, which retroactively transforms the dialogue into authorized teaching.

**The function.** To produce a catechism: this hadith is, to this day, the standard definition of religion in three degrees.

**Verdict.** A decisive case for the typology. It forces the addition of a category: what is transmitted is not only material, it is also a format. And a transmitted format is harder to spot than a content — hence probably more frequent than it appears.

### Case P — Circumcision: an institution without Qur'anic support

**The corpus.** Two interlocking traditions:

1. "The *fiṭra* (the native disposition) is five — circumcision, shaving the pubic hair, trimming the moustache, cutting the nails, plucking the armpits." (Bukhārī.)
2. "Abraham circumcised himself when past eighty, and he circumcised himself with an adze (*qadūm*)." (Bukhārī.)

**The decisive fact.** Circumcision is absent from the Qur'an. Not one occurrence, not one allusion. The most identity-defining institution of Muslim practice rests entirely on the hadith.

**The prior material.** Genesis 17: the Abrahamic covenant. The hadith does not contest the origin: it claims it, as in case G, by way of Abraham rather than Moses.

**The formal marks.** Two anomalies, both telling:

1. *The age.* Genesis 17:24 says that Abraham was circumcised at ninety-nine. The hadith says eighty. The gap is not an approximation: it is a different number, in a tradition where the ages of the patriarchs are scriptural data. What is this eighty doing here? An open question.
2. *The qadūm.* The classical commentators hesitate: is *qadūm* the tool (an adze, a detail of brutal realism) or a toponym (a place in Syria)? The ambiguity is carried by the vocalization. This is exactly the kind of philological knot that *drash* exploits rather than resolves.

A third trait: the inscription in a list of five, which absorbs a covenant into a hygiene. The passage from the sign of the covenant to an item of *fiṭra* is a major theological shift, effected by the simple act of listing.

**The function.** To found a constitutive practice without scriptural support — and to naturalize it (*fiṭra*: the native disposition) rather than contractualize it (*berit*: the covenant).

**Verdict.** The most demonstrative case in the entire dossier, and the hardest to contest: there is no need to establish a fine textual filiation; it suffices to note an absence. If the hadith were an ornament of revelation, removing the hadith would leave Islam intact. Yet removing the hadith makes circumcision disappear, along with the number of five prayers (case H), ʿĀshūrāʾ (case G) and the night prayer (case M). The corpus is not a supplement: it is load-bearing.

---

## 4. Control note: the Qur'an performs the same gesture {#s4}

The Qur'an itself openly practises the rearrangement of earlier materials, which forbids treating this as an invention of the hadith. Qur'an 5:32 — "for this reason We prescribed to the Children of Israel that whoever kills a soul… it is as if he had killed all mankind" — cites Mishnah *Sanhedrin* 4:5, explicitly presenting it as an earlier prescription addressed to Israel.

Two consequences, despite the restriction of the corpus to the hadith:

1. The hadith does not invent rearrangement; it changes its *regime of avowal*. The Qur'an cites by naming its source; the hadith cites by substituting its guarantor. This is precisely the difference between the lemma and the chain.
2. Any critique that reproached the hadith for borrowing would strike the Qur'an as well. The argument is therefore not "the hadith borrows," but "the hadith designates differently what it borrows."

### What the dossier establishes

The corpus behaves like a traditionist literature with variable thresholds of veracity, which rearranges ancient materials under a substituted guarantor. The structural position is mishnaic; the operation is, depending on the case, borrowing, adaptation or genuine *drash*.

### Six results that emerged in the course of the investigation

**1. Stratification (cases I + J).** A single hadith — Adam in his image, sixty cubits tall — carries simultaneously an aggadic borrowing and a lemmatic *drash*. The unit of analysis is therefore not the hadith but the utterance: a single tradition can be composite, each clause having its own history. This requires finer cutting than the initial grid anticipated.

**2. The disputed guarantor (case G).** ʿĀshūrāʾ adds a fifth position to the typology of anchors: neither lemma, nor chain, nor *kashf*, nor imam — but the claim to a rival guarantor ("we have more right to Moses than you"). Authority is not substituted; it is disputed with its holder.

**3. The regime of avowal.** What distinguishes the hadith from the Qur'an is not that it borrows, but that it names its source differently. The theoretical note must bear on the regimes of designating origin, not on origin itself.

**4. Borrowing of form (case O).** What is transmitted is not only material: it is also a format — here, catechesis by question and answer. A transmitted format is harder to spot than a content, hence probably more frequent than it appears. The grid must gain a fourth category, alongside borrowing, adaptation and *drash*.

**5. Transformation under doctrinal constraint (case L).** The received material is not freely reworked: the element that would compete with an established dogma is replaced by a compatible equivalent. The pre-existent book becomes the pre-existent tool, because the place of the uncreated book is already held by the Qur'an. *Drash* works under systemic pressure.

**6. The corpus is load-bearing, not ornamental (case P).** Removing the hadith makes circumcision, the number of five prayers, ʿĀshūrāʾ and the night prayer disappear. It is not a supplement to revelation; it is part of the structure. The question "is the hadith authentic?" is therefore badly posed: a negative answer would leave nothing standing, which is precisely the sign that we are not dealing with a documentary genre.

### What the dossier does not establish

That every hadith is a midrash. The distinction of three degrees (borrowing, adaptation, *drash*) must remain operative, on pain of pan-midrashism: if every reuse is *drash*, the concept no longer discriminates anything.

## 6. Toward a theory: the displacement of the guarantor of truth {#s6}

The inquiry suggests a single history, traversed by one and the same problem: how to authorize a new utterance in a world where only the old carries authority? Five solutions have been identified, which form as many positions of the guarantor of truth:

1. **The verse** (the midrashic solution: the new legitimates itself by commentary on the old).
2. **The chain of transmitters** (the mishnaic solution: the new legitimates itself by tracing back to the origin).
3. **The disputed guarantor** (the supersessionist solution: the new legitimates itself by claiming the rival's guarantor — "we have more right to Moses than you").
4. **The experience of the interpreter** (the Sufi solution: the new legitimates itself by unveiling, *kashf*).
5. **The living person** (the Shīʿī solution: the imam is the living guarantor of the continuing word — cf. Amir-Moezzi, *Le Coran silencieux et le Coran parlant*).

The Islamic doubling of the gesture is clear: the *tafsīr* keeps the lemma, the hadith keeps the chain. The hadith is mishnaic; the *tafsīr* is midrashic. The cases where a hadith recovers its lemma (cases B, H, I, L) are the points of contact between the two regimes, and they are the most instructive.

Graded veracity is a feature of the genre. The tradition distinguishes the strict hadith (admissible in law) from the weak hadith (admissible in moral exhortation). It names the *isrāʾīliyyāt*. It accepts hadiths without a chain that are held to be true. The corpus never operated on a binary theory of true and false. It is modern readings — apologetic and polemical — that impose one on it.

The critical consequence is direct: contemporary literalism, by treating every canonical hadith as a legal norm, commits an error of genre. It reads *aggadah* as *halakha*. The *gharqad* hadith in the Hamas Charter is the most visible demonstration of this.

---

## 7. Conclusion {#s7}

This inquiry proposes a change of perspective on the hadith. No longer: *is it authentic?* — a question that presupposes the corpus to be an archive and that does not know what to do with the answer, whatever it may be. But rather: *what does the corpus do?* How does it manufacture authority? What does it rearrange, and under what constraints?

The answer is that the hadith occupies in Islam the position the Mishnah occupies in Judaism: it organizes law and narrative not by following the order of the verses, but by following the order of the chapters of life. It has no lemma: it has a chain. And when, exceptionally, it recovers a lemma — when *aggadah* becomes *drash* — that is where the mechanism reveals itself most clearly.

What this inquiry opens, beyond the hadith, is a general theory of the migration of the guarantor of truth in religious traditions. The verse, the chain, the disputed guarantor, the unveiling, the living person: five answers to the same problem, five ways of saying that a new utterance has the right to be uttered. The history of religions is perhaps, in part, the history of these substitutions.

## 9. Threads left open {#s9}

The results of this inquiry call for several research questions, which the following parts of the dossier will have to take up:

- If the structure of one of the greatest theological debates in Islam (the divine descent, case M) is a Jewish liturgical inheritance, what becomes of the debate once it is resituated in its inter-religious context of formation? Must the history of Islamic theology be rewritten to take account of the archaeology of its forms?
- If the hadith is load-bearing and not ornamental (case P), what theory of religious authority accounts for a system in which practical norms rest on a corpus known to rearrange foreign materials? Must a theory of authority distinguish origin from function?
- If the unit of analysis is the utterance and not the hadith (cases I + J), what methods of material philology (collation of variants, stemmatics) can be applied to the hadith corpus in order to map the internal stratification of the traditions?
- If *drash* works under doctrinal constraint (case L), should the history of religions adopt a model of "transformation under constraint" that would account for the selection of transmitted elements according to the structure that receives them?
