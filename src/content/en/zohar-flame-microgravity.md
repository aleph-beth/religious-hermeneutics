## 1. Prologue — Midrash as a dialogue between tradition and modernity {#s1}

The Jewish tradition has always used concrete images to explore the most abstract questions. Among these images, the flame holds a central place, both in rabbinic literature and in mystical texts such as the Zohar. This article proposes a modern rereading of midrash, crossing traditional perspectives with contemporary scientific findings on the physics of flames, microgravity, and cosmology.

Our point of departure is an apocryphal scene, imagined as a dialogue between two sages discussing the nature of a candle flame. This fictional dialogue will serve as our guiding thread for exploring fundamental questions: how does the tradition understand the relationship between matter and spirit? Between origin and becoming? Between gravity and light?

This article is addressed both to specialists in Jewish studies and to the educated general reader. We have therefore taken care to explain every technical concept, whether it comes from modern physics or from Semitic philology, while maintaining scientific and philosophical rigor.

## 2. The imagined midrash: two sages, a lamp, and a fruitful error {#s2}

### The apocryphal dialogue

Here is a scene that exists in no book, but that could have appeared in one.

Rabbi Yose and his companion were walking by night when they entered a house in which a lamp was burning. The companion said: "Look how the flame has the shape of a tongue, tapering upward." Rabbi Yose answered: "It has that shape because it flees the earth. Fire cannot bear the place below: it climbs back toward its sphere, and all its beauty comes from tearing itself away." The companion asked: "Then the earth gives it nothing?" Rabbi Yose concluded: "The earth gives it the wick and the oil, and nothing more. The rest is flight."

They fell silent, and the lamp burned until morning.

### The confrontation with modern science

Seven centuries later, an experiment conducted aboard the International Space Station (ISS) photographed a flame in a microgravity environment, roughly four hundred kilometers above the ground. Contrary to what Rabbi Yose had observed, this flame did not have the shape of a tongue. It was spherical. Blue. Almost invisible. It rose nowhere. And after a few minutes, it went out in its own combustion products.

At first glance, Rabbi Yose was wrong. But as we shall see, he was wrong *exactly where it mattered*. His very error opens a door onto a deeper understanding of the relationships between matter, origin, and light.

### Why this apocryphal midrash?

Midrash, as a hermeneutic method, does not merely explain sacred texts. It *makes them speak* by placing them in new contexts, confronting them with new questions. In imagining this dialogue between Rabbi Yose and his companion, we are only following the midrashic tradition: creating a space of dialogue in which modern questions can meet ancient answers.

This apocryphal scene works as a *maaseh*, an exemplary story, which allows us to approach complex questions concretely. It invites us to ask: what does modern physics teach us about the nature of the flame? And in return, what can the Jewish tradition teach us about how to understand these scientific discoveries?

## 3. The Zohar and the double light: a physics of inversion {#s3}

### A cosmological and philosophical passage

In the Zohar, section *Bereshit* (In the Beginning), there is a passage that speaks as much about the origin of the universe as about philosophy. The flame of a lamp is broken down into two superimposed lights:

1. **The white light above** (*Ohr Elyon*): it does not vary and consumes nothing. It is pure, eternal, unchanging.
2. **The blue-black light below** (*Ohr Tahor*, or *aḥida bi-ftila*, "gripped in the wick"): it devours the wick and the oil. It is active, consuming, temporal.

The white light rests upon the black light. It subsists only as long as the other destroys on its behalf. This text, at once cosmological and metaphysical, thus describes a paradoxical relationship of dependence: the purest light depends on the most impure light in order to exist. What concerns us here is this dynamic of the flame, in which purity can only come about through the consumption of matter, in which radiance presupposes imperfection.

### The Aristotelian physics of the Zohar

The Zohar, composed in thirteenth-century Castile, belongs to an intellectual context shaped by Aristotelianism, transmitted by Arab commentators such as Averroes. In that physics, fire rises because it returns to its *natural place*, the sphere of fire located beneath the moon. The flame does not rise *because of* the earth but *by fleeing* it. Its entire dynamic is a flight toward its own element.

Here lies our deliberate inversion. Modern physics teaches us that the flame does not rise because it flees the earth, but because the earth provides it with an *orientation*. Without the gravitational field, the flame cannot create the draft it needs to feed itself with oxygen.

### Midrash as a rule-governed procedure

It is crucial to understand that we are not doing exegesis here in the classical sense. We are not seeking to discover the original meaning of the Zohar. We are instead practicing midrash, that is, a *rule-governed procedure that makes the text say what it did not say*, on condition that we declare by which rule we make it say so.

Fundamentalism operates in reverse: it presents its own reading as the original, timeless, and unchanging meaning. Our approach assumes its anachronism as a hermeneutic instrument, not as a discovery. We use microgravity as a lever to interrogate the Zohar, knowing full well that the Zohar knew nothing of microgravity.

### Why is this inversion legitimate?

Because the Zohar itself practices a form of inversion. It takes the physical categories of its own time (the ascent of fire toward its natural place) and uses them to speak of metaphysical realities (the relationship between the upper and lower worlds). We do the same, but with the physical categories of our own time.

This is the strength of midrash: it allows sacred texts to *speak* to each age, using the language and concepts of that age. The text does not change, but our understanding of what it can say changes.

## 4. Gravity as orientation: when the earth gives nothing but orients everything {#s4}

### First fact: microgravity is free fall, not the absence of gravity

A common misconception holds that aboard the International Space Station there is *no gravity*. This is inaccurate. Earth's gravity at the altitude of the ISS (about 400 km) is still **90% of its value at ground level**. What is missing is not gravitational attraction but *apparent weight*.

The station and its contents are in **permanent free fall** around the Earth. We therefore speak of *microgravity*, not of *weightlessness*. This nuance is crucial to understanding what happens to a flame in orbit.

### Second fact: gravity gives the flame nothing

Contrary to what one might think, gravity provides the flame with **neither fuel, nor oxidizer, nor heat**. It provides only an **asymmetry**: an up and a down.

It is the flame itself that then produces its own draft:

1. Its hot gases are less dense than the surrounding air.
2. The gravitational field sorts these gases by density.
3. A low-pressure zone forms at the base of the flame.
4. Fresh air is drawn in laterally, through the base, at the level of the wick.

The flame thus converts its own heat into a kind of *pump* that allows it to feed on oxygen. But it can do so only in a space that has a *direction*.

### In weightlessness: suffocation by excess of self

In a microgravity environment, the flame is deprived of orientation. Without this sorting of gases by density, the combustion products (water vapor, carbon dioxide, soot) accumulate around it and cut off its access to oxygen.

It is not a shortage of resources that extinguishes the flame, but **suffocation by excess of self**. The flame *steeps in what it has produced*.

### First proposition: origin as symmetry breaking

From these observations we can draw a first philosophical proposition:

> **Origin does not provide substance; it breaks isotropy.**

Origin does not feed; it orients. This is an **anti-substantialist** position: it grants origin no essence, no privileged content, nothing but a function of *symmetry breaking*.

This idea has the advantage of being difficult for essentialist discourses to co-opt. If origin is only a function of orientation, then it cannot be turned into a sacred substance, an immutable principle.

### Three clarifications to lock the figure in place

To keep this proposition from drifting toward a eulogy of roots or of pure origin, three clarifications are needed:

1. **Buoyancy and the necessary gap.** The buoyant force that allows the flame to rise is proportional to the *difference in density* between the hot gases and the surrounding air. A flame at the temperature of the ambient air would not rise. It is therefore not enough to be attached to one's origin: one must *differ* from it. Perfect conformity to the environment and weightlessness produce the same result: **nothing circulates**. What makes a flame shine is the gap within an oriented field.

2. **Renewal comes from the ground.** New air does not arrive from above. It enters laterally, through the base, at the level of the wax. Renewal comes *from the ground*, not from the sky.

3. **Ritual as a substitute for natural orientation.** Aboard the space station, what saves flames from suffocation is the *fans*. An *instituted* circulation replaces the absent field. This is a functional definition of ritual: **to produce an orientation where nature no longer provides one**. The ambivalence remains intact, since it is precisely this forced ventilation that makes fire more unpredictable and more dangerous in orbit than on the ground.

**Philosophical conclusion**: for human beings, gravity can be seen as their origin or their possibilities. That gravity allows the flame to shine reveals a deeper truth: the same holds for human history.

**Cosmological conclusion**: human beings are nothing but organized mud. Likewise, the universe needs physical laws, but also an attractor to make it dynamic and alive.

## 5. K-B-D: the root that binds glory and weight {#s5}

### A Semitic root with many meanings

The Hebrew language (and Semitic languages more broadly) offers a fascinating perspective on the relationship between gravity and glory. A single root, **K-B-D (כבד)**, holds together meanings that in Indo-European languages are generally kept apart:

| Language | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Akkadian | *kabātu* / *kabtu* | to be heavy; important, considerable |
| Akkadian | *kabattu* | the liver, seat of mood |
| Ugaritic | *kbd* | liver; to honor |
| Hebrew | כָּבֵד (*kaved*) | heavy; the liver |
| Hebrew | כָּבוֹד (*kavod*) | glory, the weight of a being |
| Hebrew | כִּבֵּד (*kibbed*) | to honor, to make heavy |
| Hebrew | לֵב כָּבֵד (*lev kaved*) | the heavy heart (of Pharaoh) |
| Arabic | كبد (*kabid*) | the liver |
| Arabic | كبد (*kabad*) | toil, hardship |
| Arabic (Qur'an 90:4) | فِي كَبَدٍ (*fī kabad*) | "We created man in hardship" |

### *Kavod*: glory as weight

*Kavod* (כָּבוֹד) is usually translated as "glory." But its literal meaning is "weight," "heaviness." The glory of God, in the Hebrew Bible, is not a radiance added to the divine being: it is its very *density*.

What weighs is what shines, and Semitic needed no second word to say it. Glory and gravity are two faces of a single reality.

### The commandment of honor

The Decalogue's commandment concerning parents (Exodus 20:12) illustrates this connection perfectly:

> כַּבֵּד אֶת-אָבִיךָ וְאֶת-אִמֶּךָ<br>
> *Kabbed et avikha ve-et immekha*<br>
> "Honor your father and your mother"

The verb used here, *kabbed* (כַּבֵּד), literally means "make heavy," "give weight to." To honor one's parents is to give them weight, to make them heavy in the sense of making them more important, more worthy of respect.

The reward promised for this commandment is significant:

> לְמַעַן יַאֲרִיכוּן יָמֶיךָ עַל הָאֲדָמָה<br>
> *Lema'an ya'arikhun yamekha al ha-adamah*<br>
> "so that your days may be long upon the ground"

The relationship to origin (the parents) is described in the exact lexicon of *weight*, and its counterpart is a *rooting* in the ground (*adamah*).

### The ambivalence of the root K-B-D

But the same root also says the opposite. Pharaoh's heart *grows heavy* (*vayikhbad lev Par'o*, וַיִּכְבַּד לֵב פַּרְעֹה), and this heaviness is a *petrification*. In the Exodus narrative, the hardening of Pharaoh's heart is what prevents him from letting the Hebrews go. The weight that glorifies and the weight that hardens are not two different words: they are one and the same word.

This is a safeguard the language imposes on any sentimental reading of origin: **what binds you to the ground can also nail you to it**. The difference lies not in the quantity of weight, but in whether or not it *circulates*.

- **Weight that circulates**: glory, honor, transmission.
- **Weight that stagnates**: hardening, petrification, death.

### Philosophical implications

This linguistic ambivalence invites us to rethink our relationship to origin. Origin is neither good nor bad in itself. Everything depends on how we relate to it:

- If we let it *circulate* through us, it enriches and orients us.
- If we let it *stagnate* within us, it petrifies and smothers us.

This is a valuable lesson for the debate between traditionalists and modernists. Traditionalists are right to stress the importance of origin (tradition, sacred texts, ancestors). But modernists are right to stress that this origin must not become a burden that keeps us from breathing.

## 6. Q-D-M and Q-B-L: the roots of orientation toward the origin {#s6}

### The root Q-D-M: the east and the origin

Another Semitic root sheds light on how orientation is constructed. The root **Q-D-M (קדמ)** is particularly rich:

| Language | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew | קֶדֶם (*qedem*) | the east, the front, the ancient |
| Hebrew | מִקֶּדֶם (*miqedem*) | from the origin, in the east |
| Arabic | قَدِيم (*qadīm*) | the ancient |
| Arabic | قَدَم (*qadam*) | the foot |
| Arabic | تَقَدُّم (*taqaddum*) | advance, progress |

In Genesis 2:8, the garden of Eden is planted *be-Eden miqedem* (בְּעֵדֶן מִקֶּדֶם), which can equally be read as:

- "in the east" (a spatial direction);
- "from the origin" (a temporal dimension).

The whole Hebrew system of orientation follows from this:

- One stands facing the sunrise (*qedem*).
- Thereupon *yamin* (יָמִין), the right hand, gives the south.
- *Semol* (שְׂמֹאל), the left hand, gives the north.
- *Aḥor* (אֲחֹר), the back, gives the west.

No direction can be thought without first facing the origin. **The future is called *aḥarit ha-yamim* (אַחֲרִית הַיָּמִים), "the back of the days"**: it lies behind, at the back of the one who looks toward the point where the day rises.

### The root Q-B-L: to receive and to face

Another root, **Q-B-L (קבל)**, completes this perspective:

| Language | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew | קַבָּלָה (*qabbalah*) | reception, tradition |
| Hebrew | קִבֵּל (*qibbel*) | to receive, to accept |
| Arabic | قَبِلَ (*qabila*) | to receive, to accept |
| Arabic | قِبْلَة (*qibla*) | the direction one faces in prayer |

- **Qabbalah** (קַבָּלָה): reception, but also the Jewish mystical tradition, handed down from master to disciple.
- **Qibla** (قِبْلَة): the direction Muslims face in prayer (the Kaaba, in Mecca).

**To receive, in Semitic, is first of all to be turned toward.**

### Application to the flame

A flame that no longer receives suffocates. To burn, it must:

1. be turned toward its origin (the wick, the wax);
2. be open to what comes from outside (the oxygen in the air).

In weightlessness, the flame loses this capacity to receive in an oriented way. The combustion products accumulate around it, and it suffocates in what it has itself produced.

The very name of the corpus we are reading, the *Qabbalah*, designates the operation we are describing: **to receive, to be turned toward, to face the origin.**

## 7. The four terms of the apparatus: wax, wick, field, and air {#s7}

### A misconception to correct

A common image in the Jewish tradition holds that the wick (*petilah*, פְּתִילָה) is the Torah because it is consumed. This image is mistaken, and correcting it makes the symbol far more powerful.

**The wick does not burn.** What burns is the vaporized wax. The wick is a capillary device that draws the liquid wax up to the zone where it passes into the gaseous phase. It carbonizes slowly, and in a well-made candle it curls out of the flame and trims itself.

### The respective functions

To understand the dynamics of the candle, four terms must be distinguished:

| Term | Physical function | Philosophical correlate | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The wax** | Inert reserve, not combustible as it stands | Accumulated heritage, transmitted matter | The wax is the potential fuel, but it does not burn as it is. |
| **The wick** | Phase transducer, capillary, independent of the gravitational field | The Torah: what makes the heritage combustible | The wick draws the liquid wax up toward the combustion zone. |
| **The gravitational field** | Isotropy breaking, sorting of products and reactants | Origin, the past, historical context | The field makes the flame's draft possible. |
| **Ignition** | Input of energy from outside the apparatus | Unresolved term (see section 10) | A spark is needed to start combustion. |

### Implications for understanding the Torah

This table lets us rethink the role of the Torah in the Jewish tradition:

1. **The Torah is neither what burns nor what feeds.** It does not replace the heritage (the wax). It does not itself supply the fuel.

2. **The Torah is what makes the heritage combustible.** Without it, the heritage remains inert (like a block of wax). With it, the heritage can be transformed into light and heat.

3. **The Torah does not survive without the heritage.** A tradition without transmitted matter makes a flash (a flame without wax). Matter without tradition makes a block (wax without a wick).

### Capillarity: a force independent of gravity

A point that is often overlooked: **capillarity does not depend on the gravitational field**. The wax rises by surface tension, *against* gravity, and it would rise just as well in orbit.

There are therefore two independent chains in the candle:

1. **What rises despite the field**: the inherited substance (the wax).
2. **What circulates thanks to the field**: the relationship to the environment (the air, the oxygen).

**Origin is not one; it is double**, and the two debts are not of the same nature.

## 8. Adam and the conduit: the soul as passage {#s8}

### Genesis 2:7: the creation of man

The account of the creation of man in Genesis 2:7 is often misunderstood:

> וַיִּיצֶר יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים אֶת-הָאָדָם עָפָר מִן-הָאֲדָמָה וַיִּפַּח בְּאַפָּיו נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים<br>
> *Vayyitzer Adonai Elohim et-ha'adam afar min-ha'adamah vayipaḥ be'apav nishmat ḥayyim*<br>
> "The Lord God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life."

This verse does not describe the addition of a substance (a soul) to a material body, but rather **the opening of a passage**:

1. **Dust from the ground** (*afar min-ha-adamah*): the raw material;
2. **Breath blown into the nostrils** (*vayipaḥ be'apav nishmat ḥayyim*): the opening of a conduit.

### The Semitic lexicon of the soul

The entire Semitic lexicon of the soul is **respiratory** and **anatomical**:

| Term | Language | Primary meaning | Derived meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Nefesh* (נֶפֶשׁ) | Hebrew | the throat | life, the soul |
| *Napištu* | Akkadian | the throat | life |
| *Nafs* (نفس) | Arabic | breath | the soul |
| *Nafas* (نفس) | Arabic | breath, breathing | — |
| *Ruaḥ* (רוּחַ) | Hebrew | wind, breath | spirit |
| *Rīḥ* (ريح) | Arabic | wind | — |
| *Rūḥ* (روح) | Arabic | breath | spirit |

**The soul is not an added substance but a conduit.** This is precisely what the flame in weightlessness lacks: not matter, but a path by which the new enters and the spent leaves.

### The *golem*: man without a conduit

Psalm 139:16 uses a revealing term to describe man before his birth:

> גָּלְמִי רָאוּ עֵינֶיךָ<br>
> *Golmi ra'u einekha*<br>
> "Your eyes saw my *golem*."

*Golmi* (גָּלְמִי) means "my unformed mass," "my unfinished self": it is man before the breath has been blown into him, before the conduit has been opened.

In the Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 38b), Adam's first hours are described as those of a *golem* (גֹּלֶם), a lifeless mass of clay without consciousness. Only when God breathes into him the *nishmat ḥayyim* (נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים), the "breath of life," does this mass become a living being.

### The rituals of creation: letters as wicks

In the Jewish mystical tradition, notably in Eleazar of Worms (thirteenth century), what activates the mass of clay is a set of **letter permutations** carried out according to the *Sefer Yetsira* (The Book of Creation).

This ancient text (perhaps from the third or fourth century) describes God's creation of the universe through the **32 paths of wisdom**: the 10 *sefirot* (numbers) and the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet.

In this context, **the letter occupies the place of the wick**:

- it does not supply the matter (the wax);
- it does not supply the energy (the breath);
- **it makes matter capable of receiving energy.**

This is a unique conception of creation: the world is activated by *letters*, which function as wicks, conduits, transducers between matter and spirit.

## 9. Imperfection as condition: why purity is sterile {#s9}

### Modern cosmology and the importance of imperfections

Modern cosmology offers a surprising perspective on the question of origin. According to the Big Bang model, the universe began in an extremely hot and dense state. As it expanded and cooled, structures (galaxies, stars, planets) formed.

But for these structures to emerge, one condition was necessary: **the universe must not have been perfectly homogeneous.**

**The anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background.** The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is the fossil radiation left over from the Big Bang. It gives us an image of the universe as it was roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang.

Observations of the CMB (notably by the COBE, WMAP, and Planck satellites) revealed the existence of **anisotropies**: slight variations in temperature (on the order of **1 part in 100,000**) in this otherwise extremely uniform radiation.

These anisotropies are crucial: without them, the universe would have remained homogeneous indefinitely. **No structure could have formed.**

- **Without density differences**: no gravitational collapse.
- **Without gravitational collapse**: no stars, no galaxies.
- **Without stars and galaxies**: no heavy elements (carbon, oxygen, and so on), and therefore no life.

**The matter-antimatter asymmetry.** Another flaw of origin is even more dizzying. Our material existence rests on a **slight excess of matter over antimatter**, on the order of **1 part in a billion**.

- Had matter and antimatter been exactly symmetrical, they would have annihilated each other.
- The result would have been a universe of photons, **luminous and rigorously empty**.

### Central proposition: the wick of the universe is a flaw

From these cosmological observations we can draw a central philosophical proposition:

> **The wick of the universe is not a substance; it is a flaw. An imperfection of origin.**

Perfect purity (exact homogeneity, exact symmetry) is **rigorously sterile**. Nothing comes out of it, by construction.

This corroborates a thesis that philology had already established: **the fundamentalist search for a pure origin is not only historically false; it is structurally contradictory.**

- Had the origin been pure (perfectly homogeneous, perfectly symmetrical), **nothing would have come to pass**.
- What fundamentalism calls "corruption" (the gap, the variant, the flaw in transmission, polysemy) is **the very condition of what it claims to preserve**.

### Applications to textual tradition

This cosmological perspective illuminates how sacred texts are transmitted and interpreted:

1. **There is no text without variants.** Every text transmitted orally or in writing undergoes modifications, additions, omissions. These variants are not corruptions but **traces of the text's life**.

2. **There is no tradition without adaptation.** Each generation interprets the sacred texts in the light of its own questions and its own context. These adaptations are not betrayals but **manifestations of the tradition's vitality**.

3. **There is no galaxy without fluctuation.** Just as a perfectly homogeneous universe would produce no structure, a perfectly frozen tradition would produce no new meaning.

### Midrash as a necessary flaw

Midrash itself can be understood as a **necessary flaw**:

- It introduces questions, interpretations, contradictions where the text seems uniform.
- It breaks the isotropy of the text, giving it a new orientation.
- Without this flaw, without this symmetry breaking, the text would remain mute, inert.

## 10. The enigma of ignition: the burning bush and the flame without transaction {#s10}

### The fifth term: ignition

We have identified four terms in the apparatus of the candle:

1. The wax (inert reserve).
2. The wick (phase transducer).
3. The gravitational field (isotropy breaking).
4. The air (oxidizer).

But a fifth is missing: **ignition**, the input of energy from outside the system.

Without an initial spark, the candle does not light. All the elements may be in place (wax, wick, air, gravity), but an **external impulse** is needed to start combustion.

### The theist vs. Spinozist debate

This fifth term raises a fundamental philosophical question:

- **Theist position**: ignition is an input from outside the system. In the Genesis account, it is God's breath that animates man (Genesis 2:7): God lights the flame of life.
- **Spinozist position**: ignition is internal to the system. In Spinoza's philosophy, God is not a transcendent being but *natura naturans*, nature naturing. Ignition is then internal, without an external agent: a fluctuation that needs no one in order to occur.

This text does not decide, and it would be dishonest to pretend that physics decides in its place. What physics teaches us is that ignition is punctual (a spark suffices), that it is not permanent (once lit, the candle sustains itself), and that the apparatus, once lit, sustains itself as long as it receives and as long as it evacuates.

### The burning bush: a flame without transaction

There is, however, a limit case described in the Bible: the burning bush of Exodus 3.

> וְהִנֵּה הַסְּנֶה בֹּעֵר בָּאֵשׁ וְהַסְּנֶה אֵינֶנּוּ אֻכָּל<br>
> *Ve-hineh ha-sneh bo'er ba'esh ve-ha-sneh einennu ukkal*<br>
> "And behold, the bush was burning with fire, and the bush was not consumed." (Exodus 3:2)

This scene defies all the laws of physics we have described: no more wax spent, no more wick carbonized, no more debt toward destroyed matter.

**A flame without transaction.**

The Hebrew text takes care to specify that the bush "is not consumed" (*einennu ukkal*), as if it knew that it is precisely the impossible that signals.

### Miracle as a flame without payment

This is perhaps the only usable definition of **miracle** within a naturalistic framework:

> **Not an intervention in the course of things, but a flame that would pay nothing.**

In the case of the candle, the flame pays for its existence by consuming wax and oxygen. In the case of the burning bush, it burns without consuming.

### The open question: the text as burning bush

The question that remains open is this: should Exodus 3 be read as

1. **an exception to the regime of the candle**: the burning bush is a miracle, a divine intervention that suspends natural laws;
2. **the exact description of what a text does**: to burn indefinitely without being consumed, because what it spends (meaning) is given back to it by reading?

**A text, like the burning bush, burns without being consumed.**

- Each reading consumes the text (by giving it a particular meaning);
- but each reading also feeds the text (by allowing it to go on signifying).

In this perspective, the text is a flame that pays nothing, because what it spends is always returned to it by those who read it.

## 11. Epilogue — Toward a theology of imperfection {#s11}

### Synthesis of the propositions

At the end of this exploration, we can summarize our central propositions:

1. **Origin does not provide substance; it breaks isotropy.** Gravity gives the flame nothing; it gives it an orientation. Origin is not an essence but a function of symmetry breaking.

2. **The wick is not what burns, but what makes burning possible.** The Torah is not the fuel, but the transducer that makes the heritage combustible. Without it, the heritage remains inert. Without the heritage, it has nothing to transmute.

3. **Glory is a weight.** The root K-B-D binds together mass, honor, and hardening. What binds us to the ground can nail us to it or root us in it.

4. **To orient oneself is to face the origin.** The roots Q-D-M and Q-B-L teach us that to receive is to be turned toward. The flame, like the human being, needs an orientation in order to live.

5. **The soul is a conduit, not a substance.** The Semitic lexicon of the soul is respiratory and anatomical. The human being is a mass of clay "pierced" by breath.

6. **Purity is sterile.** The imperfections of origin (anisotropies, matter-antimatter asymmetry) are the condition of all structure. Variants, adaptations, and flaws in transmission are the condition of a tradition's vitality.

7. **The text is a burning bush.** It burns without being consumed, because each reading gives back to it what it takes.

### Opening

This modern rereading of midrash invites us to rethink our relationship to tradition, to origin, and to perfection.

- **Against fundamentalism**: the quest for a pure and immutable origin is vain. Purity is sterile; it is imperfection, the flaw, and the variant that allow a tradition to live and renew itself.
- **Against relativism**: not everything is equivalent for all that. Tradition is not merely one deposit of meaning among others, but an **apparatus** that orients, that transmits, and that makes possible the combustion of inherited matter.
- **For a theology of imperfection**: what if God himself were not perfect? What if creation were not an act of perfection but an act of *opening*? An act that introduces into the world not order, but the possibility of order through disorder?

Midrash, in the end, is not only a method of interpretation. It is a **metaphysics of imperfection**, a way of saying that it is precisely because the world is imperfect, because texts are ambiguous, and because traditions are alive, that something can emerge from them: light, meaning, life.

And perhaps this is the greatest teaching of the flame: **what weighs and what shines are not two different things, but two faces of a single reality**, that of a world in which origin is not a substance but an orientation; in which tradition is not a block but a conduit; in which perfection is not the end, but the beginning of sterility.

> *"The flame is a letter that the wax writes to the air, and that the air reads by burning."*

- Observe a candle flame on Earth and compare it with videos of flames in microgravity (available on NASA's website).
- Study the textual variants in the manuscripts of the Zohar (for instance, between the Mantua and Vilna editions).

**Questions for reflection**

- How can the notion of a pure origin be reconciled with the necessity of imperfections for anything to come to pass?
- In what way does the Jewish tradition, with its insistence on study and debate, embody a "physics of imperfection"?
- Can one imagine a theology in which God is not perfect, but *open*?

**Research directions**

- Explore parallels between the physics of flames and other natural phenomena (such as vortices or chemical reactions) to illuminate philosophical concepts.
- Study other Hebrew roots to see whether they offer similar perspectives on the relationship between matter and spirit.
- Compare Jewish, Christian, and Muslim approaches to textual interpretation to see how each handles the question of the imperfection of sacred texts.
