## 1. Purpose {#s1}

Midrash Academy offers comparative readings of the Jewish, Christian and Islamic traditions, built on one thesis: the Semitic languages share a common substrate and common ways of thinking; midrash is the mechanism of history within them; and understanding the world, like understanding philosophy, runs through the *sod*, the deepest level of reading. A text is then read as a structure, and a myth as a mechanism.

**What the site does:**

- it brings together texts, roots and motifs from neighbouring traditions to reveal their shared structures;
- it makes its reasoning verifiable: every claim indicates what kind of statement it is (see section 2);
- it releases all its content under a CC0 licence, in French and in English.

**What the site does not do:**

- it does not rank the traditions or try to prove the superiority of any one of them;
- it does not replace confessional exegesis or the critical editing of texts;
- it does not present a midrashic reading as an established historical fact.

Essays are signed collectively "Midrash Academy". The site's authority rests on the verifiability of its sources, not on the identity of its authors.

## 2. Three regimes of statement {#s2}

Every claim on the site belongs to one of three regimes, which the reader must be able to tell apart without effort.

| Regime | What it asserts | What grounds it | How it is signalled |
|---|---|---|---|
| Established | A textual, linguistic or historical fact accepted in scholarship | An edited primary source, dictionary or cited study | Reference in a note; indicative wording |
| Comparative hypothesis | A plausible but undemonstrated connection (shared root, borrowing, structural parallel) | Converging evidence, but no proof of descent | "one may propose", "it is plausible"; refutation criteria stated |
| Midrashic reading | An openly assumed interpretation that makes sense within a tradition of reading | The coherence of the text and the logic of midrash, not historical proof | Explicit mention "midrashic reading"; never presented as a fact |

**Rules of application:**

- an essay may combine the three regimes, but never confuses them within a single sentence;
- a midrashic reading is not false because it cannot be demonstrated: it is a genre, with its own rules;
- a parallel is never presented as proof of descent between two texts.

## 3. Corpus and sources {#s3}

The site works on primary texts in their original language whenever possible, and cites the edition used.

| Tradition | Main corpus | Reference edition to cite |
|---|---|---|
| Jewish | Hebrew Bible, Mishnah, Talmud, midrashim, Zohar | Masoretic Text (BHS or Leningrad), Vilna edition of the Talmud, critical editions of the midrashim |
| Christian | New Testament, Septuagint, patristic writings | Nestle-Aland, Rahlfs-Hanhart, Patrologia |
| Islamic | Qur'an, hadith, sira, falsafa | Cairo edition of the Qur'an, canonical hadith collections with numbering, critical editions |
| Mesopotamian and ancient | Epic of Gilgamesh, Akkadian texts, Greco-Roman sources | Scholarly editions and standard translations |

**Principles:**

- a primary source outweighs a secondary source, and a secondary source outweighs popularisation;
- every translation is identified (translator, year); a translation made for the site is flagged as such;
- a hadith is always cited with its collection, its number and, if the point depends on it, its chain of transmission;
- digital sources (Sefaria, Corpus Coranicum, manuscript databases) are cited with their address and the date accessed.

## 4. Philological and etymological method {#s4}

A root comparison is presented as solid only if it passes five checks; otherwise it is classed as a hypothesis or as a midrashic reading.

1. **Regular phonetic correspondences.** The move from one root to another follows the known correspondences between Semitic languages (for example K-P-R in Hebrew and K-F-R in Arabic), not a surface resemblance.
2. **Attestation.** Every meaning put forward is attested in a corpus, with a reference. A merely reconstructed meaning is flagged as such.
3. **Reference dictionaries.** Roots are checked in works such as HALOT, Brown-Driver-Briggs, Lane's Lexicon or the *Lisān al-ʿArab*, and disagreement between dictionaries is mentioned.
4. **Etymology versus paronomasia.** A play on sounds or letters (such as reading a word backwards) belongs to midrashic reading, not to historical linguistics. The site names the two separately.
5. **Refutation test.** Every hypothesis states what would disprove it: an unattested meaning, an irregular phonetic correspondence, an incompatible dating.

Reading backwards, the numerical value of letters and play on graphic forms are classic midrashic devices. The site uses them openly as such, without passing them off as linguistic proof.

## 5. Structural and comparative reading {#s5}

Comparison concerns structures (narratives, motifs, interpretive procedures), not supposed influences between traditions.

- **A common substrate, with midrash as mechanism.** The Semitic languages share a common substrate and common ways of thinking; midrash, the rewriting of and commentary on an earlier text, is the mechanism by which history is made and traditions borrow from one another.
- **A text with several meanings, read down to the *sod*.** A text is read as a structure and a myth as a mechanism; their different meanings coexist peacefully, and the *sod*, the deepest level of reading, opens up the understanding of the world as of philosophy.
- **Comparison without hierarchy.** Two traditions are compared on the same axes (ontology of the divine, relation to reason, mediation, mode of interpretation), without placing one above the other.
- **Context first.** A parallel is situated: where, when, in what milieu, with what real possibility of contact between the texts. Failing that, it is presented as a structural analogy and not as a borrowing.
- **Midrash as method.** Rewriting, rearranging and commenting on an earlier text is treated as a literary procedure in its own right, including in the Gospels and in hadith literature.

## 6. Citations, references and translations {#s6}

Every quotation and every reference must let the reader find the passage in under a minute.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Biblical verse | Book, chapter, verse (e.g. Gen 3:22); text version specified |
| Qur'anic verse | Sura and verse (e.g. Q 2:255); edition specified |
| Hadith | Collection, book or chapter, number; degree of authenticity if the point depends on it |
| Rabbinic text | Tractate, folio and page (e.g. Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 5a) |
| Original languages | Consistent transliteration; original text given in quotation marks or in a note if the argument turns on the word |
| Translation | Translator and year; "site translation" if made for the essay |
| Secondary literature | Author, title, year, publisher or DOI |
| Online source | Full address and date accessed |

Each essay ends with a "Sources" section and a "Level of proof" section stating, in one line per thesis, whether it belongs to the established regime, the comparative hypothesis or the midrashic reading.

The French and English versions of an essay are equivalent. Where they differ, the date of last update of each version is displayed.

## 7. Limits, reservations and sensitive subjects {#s7}

The site's readings are proposals open to criticism, not final conclusions.

**Acknowledged limits:**

- traditions borrow from one another; every borrowing put forward must nonetheless rest on evidence of contact (date, milieu, circulation of texts), failing which it remains a hypothesis;
- midrashic reading is a philosophical concept in its own right, a way of thinking about meaning and history, and not merely a method of interpretation or of establishing facts;
- the site is a re-reading of compared texts in which the different meanings coexist peacefully without being adjudicated; it is not a peer-reviewed publication; its essays carry no weight as confessional or legal expertise.

**Sensitive subjects.** For any essay touching on violence, a recent event or the religious identity of living groups:

- the subject is treated by keeping the analysis of words separate from any judgement on persons or communities;
- factual claims about current affairs are sourced or omitted;
- the essay states that a lexical analysis is not a political analysis;
- readers are invited to report any error, which is corrected publicly.

## 8. Licence, versions, corrections and how to cite {#s8}

All content is placed in the public domain (CC0 1.0): it may be copied, adapted and reused without permission, including for commercial purposes.

- **Versions.** The source code and the change history are public on GitHub; each essay displays its date of last update.
- **Corrections.** Every reported error is corrected, and the correction is dated in the essay's history.
- **Anonymity.** Essays are published under the site's name. Criticism addresses arguments and sources, never the identity of their author.
- **Citing an essay.** Midrash Academy, "Title of the essay", page address, version of day/month/year, accessed day/month/year. For a lasting citation, prefer the archived version or a persistent identifier (DOI) where one exists.
