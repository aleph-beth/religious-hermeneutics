## 1. The general mechanism: co-protection rather than descent {#s1}

Unlike Rome, where the Julio-Claudians claimed descent from Venus, the emperors at Ephesus never claim descent from Artemis. The local formula is co-protection: the emperor presents himself as protector and benefactor of the goddess, and the city becomes "neokoros" (temple-warden) of both the Temple of Artemis and the temples of the imperial cult. The two sacralities coexist hierarchically: Artemis remains "the goddess", the emperor is "the lord".

---

## 2. The main emperors associated with Artemis {#s2}

### Augustus

The first emperor established in the goddess's city. He restored civil peace, and Ephesus gradually obtained the neokorate of the imperial cult alongside that of Artemis. The Ephesian imperial cult is born in the shadow of the Artemision.

### Trajan

The monumental fountain that bears his name, on the Curetes Street, displays his statue above the waters; water, an imperial benefaction, echoes the goddess's sacred springs. Ephesian coins systematically pair the emperor with the Artemision.

### Hadrian

The most explicit case: the temple bearing his name on the Curetes Street carries a foundation inscription that traces the city back to the goddess and the Amazons. A philhellene ruler, Hadrian presents himself as friend and continuator of the Greek cults. Under his reign Ephesus becomes "metropolis" of the province of Asia.

### Antoninus Pius

His reign protects the cult of Artemis; Antonine coins pair the emperor with the goddess, the Artemision remaining the emblem of the city.

### Domitian

His temple at Ephesus illustrates the cult of the deified emperor: the myth of the sovereign's divinity in its starkest form.

## 3. The relation to the feminine: sovereignty under maternal guardianship {#s3}

Here the Ephesian case becomes singular:

- At Ephesus, masculine sovereignty (the emperor) can only take root by allying itself with the sacred feminine (the mother goddess). The emperor does not found, he protects; he does not create, he nourishes by echo. His legitimacy runs through coexistence with a feminine power older than himself.
- The contrast with the Roman model is sharp: Venus is an ancestor (the masculine descends from the feminine), Artemis of Ephesus is an ally (the masculine coexists with the feminine). An Anatolian mother goddess could not be the credible ancestor of a Latin emperor, but she could be his ally.
- The neokorate itself is a function of guarding, traditionally feminine in the ancient imagination: guarding the temple, the threshold, life, just as Hecate, associated with Artemis at Ephesus, guards the crossroads.

| Model | Feminine figure | Relation of masculine power |
|---|---|---|
| Rome | Venus | Descent: the masculine descends from the feminine |
| Ephesus | Artemis | Alliance: the masculine coexists with the feminine |

## 4. The Pauline rupture {#s4}

This balance was disturbed by St Paul: to refuse to say "Caesar is lord" is to refuse the very balance of the coexistence. The riot of the silversmiths (Acts 19) is not a mere commercial dispute: it is a people sensing that the source of its life is being torn away, the nourishing goddess, ally of the emperor.
