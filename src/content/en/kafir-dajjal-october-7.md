Some violence cannot be measured by its death toll alone. The violence of October 7, 2023 lies also in the way it was conceived, organised, filmed and claimed. Faced with it, language sometimes fails, or betrays: the words we use to name it can veil it more than they illuminate it. This is why it is worth returning to two Arabic terms, *kāfir* and *dajjāl*, the first of which has been mistranslated and the second of which, paradoxically, names precisely what we saw.

This article proceeds in three steps. It first returns to the word *kāfir*, often wrongly rendered "unbeliever", in order to restore the richness of its root. It then turns to the word *dajjāl*, which in the Muslim tradition designates the figure of the deceiving evil. Finally, it shows why this second word illuminates the violence of October 7 better than any other.

---

## 1. Kāfir: The One Who Covers the Seed {#s1}

The Arabic root K-F-R means "to cover" or "to veil". The ancient language first used it for the farmer, the *kāfir*, the one who covers with earth the seed he has just sown. There is nothing hostile in this gesture: the farmer entrusts the seed to the soil, hides it, and waits for time to bring up the plant that will feed.

The primary sense is therefore one of hope. The earth keeps, then gives. The Qur'an itself uses the word in this agricultural sense, when it compares the life of this world to a rain whose vegetation delights the *kuffār*, the cultivators (Qur'an 57:20): the parable refers to those who gaze in wonder, not to those who would be condemned.

We must nonetheless be precise. The word later took on, in religious usage, the sense of one who "covers" a blessing received or the truth: the ungrateful, the concealer. This semantic shift is attested from the Qur'an onward, and it is not illegitimate in itself. But translating *kāfir* systematically as "unbeliever" reduces a broad field of meaning to one of its later developments, and suggests that hostility is inscribed in the root itself. Yet the root speaks of earth, seed and patience. It names the veil that protects and brings to birth, not the veil that separates and excludes.

---

## 2. Dajjāl: The One Who Covers with Gold {#s2}

The true name of evil is found elsewhere. *Dajjāl* refers to the act of covering with gold leaf, of gilding. In the Muslim tradition, it is the name of the Antichrist, the impostor of the last days, whose coming is announced as the supreme trial: he will not come as an obvious monster, but as a dazzling seduction.

The two words are thus opposed term for term, though they share the same logic of covering. The *kāfir* covers so that something true may be born. The *dajjāl* covers so that something false may appear beautiful. The first hides in order to give life; the second hides in order to deceive. In this grammar, evil is not first of all naked violence: it is violence dressed up, violence that takes on the appearance of glory.

The reach of this intuition deserves emphasis. The tradition does not say that evil is recognised by its ugliness; it says that evil is recognised by its gilding. The criterion of evil is not the absence of beauty but false beauty: splendour plated over abomination. This distinction is searingly relevant today.

---

## 3. October 7, or the Gilding of Violence {#s3}

Here the word becomes a key to interpretation. Hamas did not merely kill on October 7: it magnified death, cruelty and injustice. It laid a gilding of the sacred over murder and torture. The massacre was filmed by the assailants themselves, claimed and exalted: the crime was presented as an offering, and blood as glory. The staging was not a peripheral excess of the violence; it was its heart, its properly *dajjālic* dimension.

The scandal goes further. Among the victims were residents of kibbutzim that were among the most left-leaning in Israel, long-time activists for coexistence and for Palestinian rights, some of whom regularly drove sick people from Gaza to Israeli hospitals. The gilding was applied even to the friends of the very cause invoked. This is the gesture of the *dajjāl* in its clearest form: to make what is abominable shine, and to destroy precisely those who could have testified that another path existed.

Naming things this way has a real use. What is said under a right word does not remain under the veil of a bad translation. Conversely, as long as the vocabulary itself is corrupted (*kāfir* hardened into an insult, jihad reduced to its martial sense, martyrdom diverted into the apotheosis of murder), violence has a linguistic garment ready-made, and the work of mourning and judgement is hindered.

## 4. Conclusion: Questions That Remain Open {#s4}

Recovering the meaning of words brings no one back. But it protects against gilding. Philological fidelity is not a scholar's luxury: it is a hygiene of judgement, especially necessary when war is also waged on the terrain of language. Two questions extend this reflection.

### How is religious vocabulary being diverted today to sacralise violence, in every camp and every tradition?

Take an emblematic example: the word *shahīd*. Its root Sh-H-D means "to witness". The *shahīd* is first of all the witness, the one who gives account of the truth before people and before God; this is the sense in which the Qur'an uses the term, and it is also why the same root gives the *shahāda*, the profession of faith. Martyrdom, in its primary sense, is not even necessarily death: it is witness carried to the end, of which death may become the extreme form when it is suffered, not inflicted.

The contemporary shift is therefore twofold. On the one hand, the word has come to designate almost exclusively one who dies weapon in hand, then one who dies while killing: the suicide bomber is called *shahīd*, and the murder of civilians thus becomes a superior form of witness. On the other hand, this diversion is doubly profitable: it sacralises the act in the eyes of sympathisers, and it supplies the adversary with a vocabulary for describing Islam as a whole as a religion of death.

The same mechanism appears elsewhere: the "just war", developed by Saint Augustine and then by the Christian tradition as a moral limit imposed on violence (strict conditions, last resort, proportionality), has become a nearly automatic formula of legitimation, invoked by almost every modern belligerent to baptise its own operations. And it appears, more deeply still, in a reflex that precedes all vocabularies: the conviction of being systematically on God's side, while the enemy is systematically on the side of evil. The sacred texts themselves tell the opposite: Jonah angry that God forgives the Ninevites, Job refusing to let his suffering prove him right against God, the prophets contesting the chosen people's election. To read Scripture is normally to lose the assurance of being in the right camp; to read it in order to regain that assurance is already gilding.

### How can we learn to recognise violence that adorns itself with glory, whatever its form, including when it is familiar to us?

The difficulty is not spotting violence in the adversary: it is spotting it in oneself, precisely where it presents itself in the colours of the good. Yet there are fairly constant signs, which belong less to morality than to grammar.

**The disappearance of the victim from the discourse.** When a narrative speaks only of the glory of the cause, the courage of the fighters or the justice of the punishment, and the bodies on the other side appear only as figures, abstract or deserved, the gilding is at work.

**The runaway of language.** Euphemisms on one's own side ("collateral damage", "targeted strike", "defensive operation" for any offensive) and, in mirror image, hyperbole applied to the enemy: violence adorns itself with glory on one side and absolute horror on the other, and the symmetry of this make-up is itself a clue.

**The rush to the sacred.** As soon as an event is immediately inscribed in a cosmic narrative (the struggle of good against the axis of evil, civilisation against barbarism, martyrdom or providence), discussing the facts becomes treason. This shift from the political to the untouchable is the sign that the violence has just been baptised.

**The test of detail.** This is the most demanding sign. Gilded violence always appears in wide shot (the cause, the nation, God) and cannot bear the close-up on a child's face, a name, a singular story.

Learning to recognise violence that adorns itself with glory is therefore learning to change scale: to refuse the wide shot of the sacred and return to the tight shot of the victims, and to apply this gesture first to the violence that our own camp dresses up. The most effective gilding is never the enemy's: it is the one we see without seeing it, because it wears our colours.
