## 1. Introduction {#s1}

A text never carries only its words. With them, it carries the mode of reading called for by its language, its culture and its literary genres. The Gospels, from their very origin, lived a double life: composed, on a serious hypothesis, in a Semitic world where texts were read as midrash, they were canonized in Greek and then read as chronicles. The same text was thus successively understood as an intellectual and literary construction, a representation of the Messiah in echo with the Scriptures, and then as a historical witness, a record of facts — and therefore of miracles.

The philosophical problem is this: the shift was not a choice but an invisible effect of translation and canonization. This article sets out the mechanism of that shift, then illustrates it with examples in which the two readings, midrashic and classical, diverge radically.

## 2. The mechanism: how a translation changes a regime of truth {#s2}

### Midrash, a genre recognized from within

Midrash is a practice of reading proper to the Jewish world: rewriting an episode of the Scriptures by replaying it in a new narrative, through verbal echoes, plays on words, alliterations and implicit quotations. To say that a narrative is "midrashic" is to say that it does not report a fact but actualizes an earlier text: it is replayed, therefore it signifies.

This genre presupposes an equipped reader: one who hears the echo, recognizes the name that recalls another, perceives the alliteration that links two verses. A Semitic audience possesses this code; it reads the narrative as one reads a variation on a theme.

### Translation carries the content, not the genre

When a midrashic text is translated into Greek, each word passes, but the instructions for use do not. The alliteration disappears, the play on words becomes inaudible, the implicit quotation becomes an ordinary sentence. The translated text is exact, and yet it has changed in nature: it no longer signals that it is a rewriting. The Greek reader has no chance of knowing that they are reading a midrash, because the target language does not mark non-literality.

The so-called "midrashic" hypothesis advances precisely this: the earliest Christian texts are not documents reporting historical facts, but texts of a midrashic nature, written in Hebrew before being translated into their present Greek ([Le champ du Midrash, "Allitérations et Rétroversion"](https://www.lechampdumidrash.net/category/lecture-midrashique-des-evangiles/)).

### Two regimes of truth

- Semitic regime: the truth of a narrative is its fidelity to the corpus, its coherence of echo with the Torah and the prophets. True = meaningful.
- Greek, then historical regime: truth is correspondence to an event. True = happened.

The same verse, depending on the regime, is a figure or a statement. The miracle appears at the exact point where the criterion changes: what was a literary actualization becomes an extraordinary event to be believed or verified.

### Canonization closes the trap

The canon fixes the language, the language fixes the regime of reading, and the regime becomes indistinguishable from the content. The Hellenophone generation can no longer go back to the midrashic intention: the reading code is dead, the text remains. It then closes upon the only mode of reading available, the literal one. Theology is built on this literalized text, and miracles become articles of faith. The evolution is historical and proceeds in three stages:

| Phase | Language | Mode of reading | Status of the "miracle" |
|---|---|---|---|
| Origin | Hebrew / Aramaic | Midrash: rewriting, representation of the Messiah | Literary figure, scriptural echo |
| Translation | Greek | The midrash is translated without its instructions for use | The sign becomes a statement |
| Canonization | Greek fixed | Literal, historical | Supernatural event to be believed |

### Methodological clarification: historical persons, midrashic narratives

A clarification is needed, because the midrashic hypothesis is often misunderstood. To say that the Gospel narratives are midrashic constructions is not to deny the historicity of persons: Jesus, Paul, Simon, Judas are historical persons. What is midrashic is the narrative: the narrative function of the characters, the episodes attributed to them, the arrangement of their story. These episodes connect to the interpretation of the Torah and to the eschatological expectations of Second Temple Judaism, not to chronicle.

The clearest example is the temptation on the pinnacle of the temple (Matt 4:5). If the narrative places Jesus on the temple, it is not because an event of this kind took place, but because the Jewish tradition expected, at the end of time, the manifestation of the Messiah on the roof of the temple, from where he would call the believers to redemption. Placing Jesus on the temple is to affirm, in midrashic language: this one is the awaited Messiah. Likewise, walking on the waters says, in the Semitic code, eschatological sovereignty more than a physical feat. The narrative is a theological confession formulated in the only idiom that makes it audible to a Semitic reader: scriptural rewriting.

The methodological consequence is double: the midrashic hypothesis is neither mythicist (everything would be invented) nor literalist apologetics (everything would have happened). It distinguishes the person, which belongs to history, from the narrative, which belongs to interpretation.

## 3. Three examples: two readings, two worlds {#s3}

### The raising of Lazarus (John 11)

Classical reading: a miraculous event, the greatest sign of Jesus' power, proof of his divinity, the occasion of the chief priests' decision to put him to death. The historical problem is then insoluble: why does no chronicler of the time mention a dead man of four days returned to life?

Midrashic reading: the pericope is an elaboration on the death of Aaron's sons, in Leviticus. Lazarus (*Eleazar*) and Martha recall, through their sounds, the two spared sons of Aaron, Eleazar and Itamar, of whom Lev 10:6 says they shall not "die." The midrash Leviticus Rabbah (10:4) already contains a scene of "resurrection" linked to this episode: a sage "brings back" a dying man by showing him that his master, a former pagan, has risen (that is, has renounced idolatry). The midrash works on the equation: healing = conversion = resurrection. To raise again is to make someone come back, in the religious sense of the term ([Le champ du Midrash, "The Raising of Lazarus"](https://www.lechampdumidrash.net/lecture-midrashique-des-evangiles/la-resurrection-de-lazare/)).

Discriminating observation: in the midrashic reading, there is nothing to believe in the sense of the supernatural; there is a text to understand. In the classical reading, there is a fact to admit. The two readings are not even discussing the same object.

### Judas, the seller (Matt 26-27, Acts 1)

Classical reading: Judas is a historical character, a traitor through greed or political disappointment; his betrayal is a fact that triggers the Passion; theology even makes him a necessary instrument of salvation, which raises the classic problem of his guilt.

Midrashic reading: Judas is a reprise of Judah son of Jacob, seller of Joseph. All the material of the narrative comes from Gen 37: the brothers sit down to eat while Joseph is in the pit (*bor*, which evokes death); hence, in the Gospels, Judas appearing on the occasion of meals, and Jesus seized with great anguish like Joseph. The motive is the same: jealousy toward the one who announces his lordship (the Kingdom, *malkhut*). The surname Iscariot combines *sakhar* (reward, retribution, not pejorative in Hebrew) and *ot* (sign): Judah has been linked to the "sign" ever since the episode of Tamar, who produces signs that Judah recognizes; Judas, for his part, gives signs of recognition, the kiss. Even the dispute "which of you is the greatest" (Luke 22:24), which arises for no reason in the middle of the Passion, fulfils Gen 49:10: the sceptre shall not depart from Judah until the tribute (*shilo*) comes to him ([Le champ du Midrash, "A seller named Judas"](https://www.lechampdumidrash.net/lecture-midrashique-des-evangiles/vendeurjudas/)).

Discriminating observation: in the classical reading, Judas' act is a crime, and theology must explain his guilt; in the midrashic reading, the person of Judas is historical, but his function in the narrative is to be the new Judah, seller of the new Joseph, and the episode fulfils Genesis. The classical moral question shifts: it concerns the narrative, not the man.

### Simon the magus (Acts 8) and the adulterous woman (John 8)

Classical reading: Simon the Magician is a historical figure whom tradition has made the father of all heresies, even of Gnosis; the adulterous woman is a real sinner whom they try to stone.

Midrashic reading: Simon is indeed a historical person, but the narrative the Acts make of him is a midrashic artefact, a constructed caricature whose narrative function is scriptural ([Le champ du Midrash, "Simon the Magus"](https://www.lechampdumidrash.net/category/lecture-midrashique-des-evangiles/)). As for the adulterous woman, the forgetting of the midrashic tradition leads exegetes into embarrassing contortions: the case is comparable to that of Rahab, the prostitute who lies and deceives her king, and whom the Epistle to the Hebrews nonetheless makes the paragon of faith; the midrash resolves the tension through a rereading that escapes the literal entirely ([Le champ du Midrash, "The Adulterous Woman"](https://www.lechampdumidrash.net/category/lecture-midrashique-des-evangiles/)).

Discriminating observation: the classical reading manufactures history (and therefore heresies, trials, debates) out of figures whose function is first of all scriptural.

## 4. Philosophical consequences {#s4}

### The supernatural as an artefact of reception

The miracle is not a primitive belief that criticism later contested; it is the late effect of a change in the regime of reading. The Christian supernatural is, in part, a product of the disappearance of the Semitic reader: when the reading code dies, the text closes upon the literal, and the literal reading of a midrash is an impossible event, hence miraculous.

### A category absent from the Semitic world

The very category of "miracle", a suspension of the laws of nature, presupposes a nomology, hence a physics. The Semitic world of origin had no such category: healing, conversion and resurrection were there reprises of a single verb (to make come back), and the "signs" were readings, not violations of the order of the world. The theology of miracle was constructed retrospectively, in Greek, with Greek concepts.

### The asymmetry of proofs

The midrashic hypothesis is testable: it rests on verifiable indices within the text (sounds, plays on words, Hebrew retroversion, midrashic parallels), such as the NS alliterations in the temptation in the wilderness (Matt 4:1) linking *nasa* (the Spirit carries away) and *menasse* (the tempter) ([Le champ du Midrash, "Temptations in the Wilderness"](https://www.lechampdumidrash.net/category/lecture-midrashique-des-evangiles/)). The historical hypothesis, for its part, can be neither proved nor refuted: the debate freezes into faith against reason. It is precisely this asymmetry that allowed the problem to last two millennia without dissolving.

## 5. Conclusion {#s5}

The passage from the Semitic world to the Greek world did not only translate words: it changed the regime of truth of an entire corpus. A text written to be reread in echo with the Scriptures was read as a testimony, and its truth of meaning became a truth of fact. Miracles, from this perspective, are not contested events of the past: they are points of no return of a translation that became invisible, midrashic figures frozen into facts by a canon that had forgotten their instructions for use.

This is not about deciding between the two readings, but about noting that the second was built in ignorance of the first, and that this ignorance was not a fault but a structural consequence of translation. The reception history of the Gospels is, in this sense, a remarkable case study of a more general phenomenon: any text translated outside its culture of reading becomes more literal, and therefore more fragile.

Opening: if this mechanism is correct, it extends far beyond the case of the Gospels. Every canonized translation, every corpus carried beyond its hermeneutic horizon, reproduces the same slide, from sign to fact, from interpretation to dogma. The question then becomes general: can a lost regime of reading be restored after the fact, or is fixation in a foreign language always irreversible? And if it can be restored, what then remains of the miracle?
