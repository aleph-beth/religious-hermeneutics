## 1. Artemis of Ephesus: the virgin who nourishes {#s1}

The Ephesian Artemis is not the huntress of the classical Greek pantheon. Probably heir to an Anatolian mother goddess (close to Cybele), she bears on her statue protuberances (breasts, bull's testicles or bees' ovaries, depending on the reading), symbols of an all-powerful fertility.

The founding paradox: the goddess is *parthenos* (virgin) in her Greek name, yet in fact a mother of fertility. The Ephesian feminine is not defined in relation to man, neither wife nor seductress, but by itself: purity and fertility combined. The Ephesians called themselves the goddess's "nurslings"; during the siege by Croesus, a rope was tied from the city wall to the temple, literally placing the city in the lap of its mother.

## 2. The Amazons: armed virginity {#s2}

According to Pausanias, Callimachus and local legends, the Amazons founded Ephesus or established the first cult of Artemis there, dancing an armed dance around her primitive image (the *xoanon*, a wooden statue "fallen from the sky").

- The Amazons embody virginity in the Greek sense of autonomy: they need no man, neither to found, nor to fight, nor to worship. They are not sterile, tradition gives them children conceived on their own initiative, but free.
- They link the wild world and the city: warriors and mothers, they embody the very vocation of the Ephesian goddess, hunt and fertility.
- The founding of Ephesus by women is an exception in the Greek imagination, almost always masculine: the city tells itself as born of women around a goddess.

## 3. Mary: the virginity that bears God {#s3}

The continuity is striking: tradition places Mary at Ephesus, on the flank of Coressos, because the city was already, in the imagination, the city of the Mother.

- The Council of Ephesus (431), held in the church of the Mother of God, proclaims Mary *Theotokos*, "she who bears God", against Nestorius, who wished to call her only the mother of the human Christ. The crowd greeted the decision with torches and songs in the streets, echoing the old processions of Artemis.
- The dogma takes up the function of the mother goddess: bringing the divine into the world. The city that cried "Great is Artemis" now acclaims "Holy Mary, Mother of God": same enthusiasm, same function.
- The House of the Virgin (*Meryem Ana*), revealed through the visions of Anne Catherine Emmerich, has become a shared pilgrimage: Muslim women still tie ribbons there as vows for fertility and healing, as their ancestors did for the goddess.

## 4. Synthesis: three figures, one structure {#s4}

| Figure | Virginity | Relation to fertility | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Artemis of Ephesus | *Parthenos*, autonomous | Nourishes the entire city | Source of civic life |
| Amazons | Armed autonomy | Mothers on their own initiative | Founders |
| Mary | Virgin-mother | Bears God himself | *Theotokos*, matrix of the divine |

The shared structure: the Ephesian feminine is never subordinate to the masculine; it founds (Amazons), nourishes (Artemis), bears the god (Mary). This feminine survives every cultural transition (Greek, Roman, Christian, Muslim) while divine masculinities pass through in turn. The masculine changes; the feminine remains.
