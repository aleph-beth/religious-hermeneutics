## 1. Introduction: a feast born of anger {#s1}

Yom Kippur, *Yom ha-Kippurim*, *"the Day of Coverings,"* is the summit of the Jewish calendar. It is inscribed in Leviticus (Lev 16; 23:26-32) and in Numbers (Num 29:7-11) as a precise ritual device: a fast, offerings, sprinklings of blood on the *kapporet*, the sending of the scapegoat to Azazel. But the priestly code presents the rite *without recounting its origin*. It prescribes; it does not narrate.

Rabbinic memory has filled this silence. For it, Yom Kippur is not first a ritual: it is a *narrative*. More precisely, it is the anniversary of a founding narrative event told in Exodus 32-34: the story of Israel's revolt at the foot of Sinai, of divine anger, of Moses's intercession, and of God's *first forgiveness* — a forgiveness sealed by the giving of the second Tablets and by the revelation of the thirteen attributes of mercy.

The rabbinic hypothesis, which this article will unfold, is rigorous: the Day of Atonement is not an abstract cultic creation but the *annual commemoration* of the day on which God, having repented of His anger, gave back the Tablets. According to *Seder Olam Rabba*, that day is the 10th of Tishri. It is this coincidence of date — and the reading underlying it — that transforms a priestly rite into a *midrashic feast*: a feast that, every year, *replays* the narrative that founded it.

This article follows that birth, step by step: from the theophanic Sinai to the *kapporet* of the Holy of Holies, by way of the Golden Calf rupture and the patient reconstruction of the divine face of forgiveness. In doing so, it deploys narratively what article 1 of the dossier deployed structurally: the cultic-expiatory pole of the root *kpr* takes, in Yom Kippur, its densest narrative configuration.

## 2. The first theophany: Exodus 19-20 and the giving of the Tablets {#s2}

The Sinai of Exodus 19-20 is the horizon of all that follows. Israel, having come out of Egypt, reaches the foot of the mountain in the third month (Ex 19:1). YHWH descends in fire, thunder, and dense cloud. The people tremble and stand at a distance; Moses alone draws near. The theophany sets the conditions of the covenant: *"you shall be for me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation"* (Ex 19:6).

The Ten Words (*ʿaseret ha-dibrot*) are then proclaimed (Ex 20:1-17). They are not yet materially handed over at this stage. The text specifies that YHWH writes them *on two tablets of stone, inscribed by the finger of God* (Ex 31:18) — a gesture given as the conclusion of a long account of prescriptions (Ex 25-31). Moses ascends to the summit to receive these tablets and remains there *forty days and forty nights* (Ex 24:18).

During this time, the people wait. They wait too long. *"When the people saw that Moses delayed to come down from the mountain…"* (Ex 32:1). It is on this waiting that the first covenant breaks.

## 3. The revolt: the Golden Calf (Exodus 32) {#s3}

### 3.1 Absence as ordeal

> When the people saw that Moses delayed to come down from the mountain, the people gathered around Aaron and said to him: "Up, make us gods who shall go before us; for as for this Moses, the man who brought us up out of the land of Egypt, **we do not know what has become of him**." (Ex 32:1)

The trial is the trial of waiting. Israel cannot bear the absence of the mediator. The rupture begins with an apparently rational question (*"we do not know"*) which, in the narrative, is in fact a *decision*: to decide that the absent one will not return is already to fashion oneself a substitute.

### 3.2 Aaron and the fashioning

Aaron, brother of Moses and designated high priest, does not resist. He requests the gold rings from the women (Ex 32:2), melts them down, and fashions a metal calf. The people exclaim: *"These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt!"* (Ex 32:4). The next day, Aaron proclaims a feast: *"tomorrow shall be a feast to YHWH"* (Ex 32:5).

The detail is philologically decisive: Aaron *does not deny YHWH*. He claims to offer an *image* of YHWH. That is, he transforms the imageless Sinai into an idol. The transgression bears not on the addressee of the cult but on its form. The grammar of the covenant is broken — not by rejection, but by confusion.

### 3.3 The divine speech to Moses

> YHWH said to Moses: "Go down; for **your people**, whom you brought up out of the land of Egypt, have corrupted themselves." (Ex 32:7)

The possessive is striking: *"your people… whom you brought up."* God, in His anger, *returns* the people to Moses. *"My people"* has become *"your people."* The rupture is rhetorically consummated: God withdraws from possession. It is on this withdrawal that the great negotiation of Ex 32:11-14 will turn.

## 4. Divine anger and Moses's intercession {#s4}

### 4.1 The threat of extermination

> YHWH said to Moses: "I have seen this people, and behold, it is a stiff-necked people. Now therefore **let me alone**, that my wrath may burn hot against them and I may consume them; and I will make of you a great nation." (Ex 32:9-10)

The threat is total: destruction of the people, and the promise to Moses to become himself the stock of a new nation. It is to offer Moses to become *a second Abraham*. But the text is philologically remarkable on one point: God says *"let me alone"* (*hannīḥah lī*). This is an *anomalous* formula. Why would God ask to be left alone, unless an intercession is *expected* and *possible*?

The rabbis seized on the detail. *Babylonian Talmud Berakhot* 32a: *"This shows that Moses held God as one holds a man by his garment, and said to Him: I will not let go until you forgive."* The divine *"let me alone"* is the opening of a struggle — that of a God who *wants* to be held back.

### 4.2 Moses's intercession

> But Moses implored the face of YHWH his God, and said: "Why, O YHWH, should your wrath burn hot against **your people**, whom you brought out of the land of Egypt with great power and with a mighty hand?" (Ex 32:11)

The reversal is philologically exact: Moses gives back to God what God had returned to him. To the divine *"your people"* (v. 7), Moses replies *"your people"* (v. 11). He refuses the cession. He places the people back under the divine possessive. This is the first act of forgiveness: restoring the possessive relation.

Moses's argument has three planes: (a) not to humiliate God before the Egyptians — *"will they say you brought them out to exterminate them?"* (Ex 32:12); (b) to remember the covenant of the fathers — *"remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel"* (Ex 32:13); (c) the promise of posterity.

### 4.3 *Vayinaḥem YHWH*: God repents of the evil

> And YHWH repented of the evil he had said he would do to his people. (Ex 32:14)

This verse is one of the most densely discussed of rabbinic and patristic exegesis. The verb *nāḥam* here means *"to repent, to relent, to allow oneself to be moved."* The divine movement is explicit: God *changes*. For the priestly code and the majoritarian rabbinic tradition, this movement is not inconstancy — it is the *truth* of mercy. God is the God who can repent.

And this verse is a *founding narrative verse* of Yom Kippur. It posits, in the order of event, what Lev 16 will posit in the order of rite: that there exists an operation by which the fault is *covered* and the relation restored. The narrative *engenders* the liturgy. The liturgy *commemorates* the narrative.

## 5. The breaking of the first Tablets {#s5}

### 5.1 Moses's gesture

> And as soon as he came near the camp and saw the calf and the dancing, Moses' anger burned hot, and **he threw the tablets out of his hands and broke them at the foot of the mountain**. (Ex 32:19)

Moses, having descended from Sinai with the tablets engraved by the hand of God, sees the scene and shatters the stone. The narrative, taken at face value, seems to present an act of fury — an analogous anger redoubling that of God. But the rabbinic reading effects here a remarkable reversal.

### 5.2 The midrashic reading: breaking to cover

*Midrash Tanḥuma Ki-Tissa* 26 and Rashi *ad locum* (on Ex 32:19) propose an inverse reading:

> *"If Moses had not broken the Tablets, how could Israel have lived — they who would have been bound by the written covenant and had already transgressed it? Without the Tablets, no formal commitment; without formal commitment, the transgression is not counted as breaking of a signed covenant."*

The breaking becomes an *act of protection*. Moses, by shattering the Tablets before they are given, *prevents the fault from being recognized as breaking of a signed covenant*. The grammar is exactly that of *kpr*: interposing an object (here, the shard) between the fault and the judgement. To break the Tablets is to *cover* the verdict before it takes effect.

### 5.3 The shard as first *kofer*

This reading is philologically vertiginous. Moses's gesture, superficially seen as a rupture, is read midrashically as **the first expiatory act in the history of Israel**. Before the *kapporet* of the Holy of Holies, before the sprinkled blood, before the scapegoat, there are *the shards of the Tablets at the foot of the mountain*. These shards do not say the end of the covenant; they say the *protection* of the covenant.

And the detail is rabbinically precious: according to *Bava Batra* 14b, the shards of the first Tablets were *gathered and placed in the Ark*, alongside the intact second Tablets. *The shards travel with the people*. Forgiveness, in Israel's memory, does not forget the rupture — it *keeps* it within itself.

## 6. The forty days and the second Tablets {#s6}

### 6.1 The new ascent

After the breaking and the partial chastisement (Ex 32:25-29), Moses ascends Sinai a second time. The narrative opens with the divine command:

> Cut for yourself two tablets of stone like the first, and I will write on the tablets the words that were on the first tablets, which you broke. (Ex 34:1)

A decisive philological difference: the *first* Tablets were *engraved by the finger of God* (Ex 31:18); the *second* are *cut by Moses* before God writes upon them. Forgiveness does not erase the rupture: *it inscribes the trace of human cooperation upon it*. The human now has a part in the stone.

### 6.2 The revelation of the thirteen attributes (Exodus 34:6-7)

On the morning of the new forty days, YHWH descends in the cloud and passes before Moses, proclaiming:

> YHWH passed before him and proclaimed: "**YHWH, YHWH, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness, keeping steadfast love for thousands, *bearing* iniquity and transgression and sin**." (Ex 34:6-7)

These verses — the *"thirteen attributes of mercy"* (*shelosh-ʿesreh middot*) — constitute the heart of the Jewish liturgy of forgiveness. They are recited at Yom Kippur, at Selihot, at Rosh Hashanah, in all penitential supplications. They *define* the divine face of forgiveness.

### 6.3 *Nośeʾ ʿavon* — bearing the fault

The central verb is *nośeʾ ʿavon*. *Nasaʾ* means *"to bear, to lift, to carry away."* God does not *make the fault disappear* by fiat; He *carries it*. He takes it upon Himself so that it may no longer be a crushing weight upon the human.

The philological articulation with *kpr* is here precise. *Nośeʾ ʿavon* is the strict verbal synonym of *kipper ʿal*. Both describe the gesture by which the higher instance interposes its own weight between fault and sanction. Both belong to *good recovering* (article 1 of the dossier, § 7): covering to make live, not to make silent.

It is this verb — *nośeʾ* — that will resound throughout the prophecy of Israel (Isa 53:4: *"he has borne our infirmities"*) and that will be translated in patristic Greek by *baptazein, airein* — verbs the Gospels will take up regarding Christ. The language of forgiveness is, from Sinai onward, *the language of bearing*.

## 7. The 10th of Tishri: the midrashic date {#s7}

### 7.1 The rabbinic chronology

The rabbinic tradition has calculated with precision the chronology of the Exodus 19-34 narrative. *Seder Olam Rabba* 6, *Pirqei de Rabbi Eliezer* 46, *Tanḥuma Ki-Tissa* 31, and the *Babylonian Talmud* (*Taʿanit* 30b; *Bava Batra* 121a) converge on the following calculation:

- 6 Sivan: giving of the first covenant;
- 7 Sivan: Moses ascends for the first time;
- 17 Tammuz: Moses descends, breaks the Tablets (a minor fast day, Tisha be-Tammuz);
- 19 Tammuz: Moses ascends again to intercede (forty days, until 29 Av);
- 1 Elul: Moses ascends a third time for the *second* Tablets (forty days, until 10 Tishri);
- **10 Tishri**: Moses descends with the second Tablets and the radiant face — *"Know that I have forgiven"* (according to the midrash).

### 7.2 Why 10 Tishri becomes Yom Kippur

This chronological construction is not a mere scholarly curiosity. It posits 10 Tishri as **the anniversary day of God's first forgiveness**. And it is *that* day, and not another, that Lev 16:29 and Lev 23:27 prescribe as a day of fasting and atonement:

> On the tenth of this seventh month is the Day of Coverings (*Yom ha-Kippurim*). (Lev 23:27)

The coincidence is not, for the rabbinic reading, a coincidence: it is the liturgical inscription of a narrative date. Each 10 Tishri replays the descent of the second Tablets. Each Yom Kippur is the memory of a God who once forgave. The rite, prescribed by Leviticus, *commemorates* the narrative told by Exodus. The two books are knotted by this date.

### 7.3 The feast as annual return

This articulation transforms Yom Kippur into a *midrashic feast* in the strong sense: a feast that is not a natural or agricultural cycle but an *annual return upon a narrative event*. Just as Pesah commemorates the Exodus from Egypt and Shavuot the giving of the Torah, Yom Kippur commemorates God's forgiveness after the Golden Calf. The feast is memory; and memory operates as renewal of the effacement.

## 8. Leviticus 16 and the great Yom Kippur liturgy {#s8}

### 8.1 The High Priest's ritual

Leviticus 16 describes the most elaborate expiatory liturgy of the entire priestly code. The High Priest — and he alone — enters the Holy of Holies, *once a year*, on 10 Tishri. He puts on linen garments (and not the ordinary golden vestments), he immerses several times in the *miqveh*, he offers a bull for his own sin, then two goats: one for YHWH, the other for Azazel.

*Mishnah Yoma* 3, 4-7 details the changes of garments and the ablutions. The rite is conceived as a progressive descent toward the Holy, then a re-ascent. The liturgical year culminates in this single day, and this single day culminates in the moment when the High Priest crosses the veil.

### 8.2 The seven sprinklings on the *kapporet*

> He shall take some of the blood of the bull and sprinkle it with his finger on the front of the kapporet on the east side; and before the kapporet he shall sprinkle the blood with his finger seven times. (Lev 16:14)

The *seven* sprinklings on the *kapporet* are at the heart of the rite. The blood is not poured to purify the human; it is sprinkled to *purify the sanctuary itself*, contaminated by the people's faults (Lev 16:16). The *physics of the sacred* established in article 1 of the dossier — the fault leaves a material trace upon the holy — culminates here in its most solemn rite.

### 8.3 The two goats: YHWH and Azazel

> And Aaron shall cast lots upon the two goats, one lot for YHWH and the other lot for Azazel. (Lev 16:8)

The double economy is striking. The goat *"for YHWH"* is offered upon the altar; its blood serves for the sprinkling. The goat *"for Azazel"* is symbolically loaded with the people's faults — the High Priest places his two hands upon its head and confesses — then it is led into the wilderness and cast down from a cliff. The Mesopotamian rite of elimination by transfer (article 2 of the dossier, § 2) reaches here its most articulated version: a goat for the verticality of the holy, a goat for the horizontality of the wilderness. Forgiveness mobilizes both directions.

### 8.4 The invocation of the Name and the prostration

*Mishnah Yoma* 6:2 reports that, on this day, the High Priest pronounces the Tetragrammaton ten times — the *only* day of the year when the unspoken Name is uttered openly, in the Holy of Holies. When the people, standing in the courtyard, hear the Name, they prostrate face-down and say: *"Blessed be the Name of the glory of His kingdom forever."* (*Barukh shem kavod malkhuto le-ʿolam vaʿed*).

It is the only moment in the liturgical year when the entire community says this formula aloud (the rest of the year, it is whispered in the *Shemaʿ*). Yom Kippur is the day on which the Name *manifests* and the people *prostrate* — a gesture that exactly inverts the people's posture before the Golden Calf, where they bowed before an image. The rite *redresses* the grammar of cult that was broken at Sinai.

## 9. The midrashim of forgiveness {#s9}

### 9.1 *Teshuvah* precedes creation

> *"Seven things were created before the creation of the world: the Torah, the Throne, the Temple, the Patriarchs, Israel, the Messiah, and teshuvah."* (*Bereshit Rabba* 1:4; cf. *Pesahim* 54a)

*Teshuvah* (turning, return) is, in this list, *prior* to creation. The reading is vertiginous: God created the *possibility* of forgiveness before creating the world. That is, creation has been possible only on condition that forgiveness was already there. Without this precondition, the gap between divine holiness and the human would be uncrossable. **Creation presupposes forgiveness.**

This primordial inscription of forgiveness within the order of things makes Yom Kippur, read in this grammar, *the feast of origin*. Before Pesah, before Sinai, before Adam — there is *teshuvah*. Yom Kippur annually replays this priority.

### 9.2 Adam, the first *baʿal teshuvah*

> *"Adam performed teshuvah"* — *Bereshit Rabba* 21:6; *Pirqei de Rabbi Eliezer* 20.

The midrash makes Adam, after the transgression of the garden, the first *baʿal teshuvah* (*"master of return"*). He fasts for seven weeks, he prays, and God accepts him. The Garden narrative thus becomes a *prelude* to Yom Kippur: the first fault calls forth the first forgiveness; each year, Israel replays this original drama. Forgiveness is not a late rabbinic innovation — it is inscribed from the narrative of origins.

### 9.3 *Yoma* 86b and the *teshuvah* that transforms

> *"Great is teshuvah, which transforms voluntary faults into merits."* (*Yoma* 86b)

The Amoraic aphorism pushes the grammar to its peak. *Teshuvah* not only *covers* the fault (a *kpr* gesture): it *transforms* it. The voluntary fault (*zadon*) becomes, through the dynamic of return, a *merit* (*zekhut*). The rejected stone becomes the cornerstone; the wound becomes a place of strength. This idea — that the fault, *covered by teshuvah*, becomes a resource — is one of the most singular Jewish contributions to the grammar of forgiveness.

### 9.4 *Teshuvah* rises to the Throne

> *"Great is teshuvah, for it rises to the Throne of glory."* (*Yoma* 86a, citing Hos 14:2)

*Teshuvah* is a *bidirectional* operation. The human turns back toward God; God lets Himself be *reached* by this turning. Forgiveness is not a unilateral gesture; it is an *encounter*. This is what *Berakhot* 32a was already saying when reading Ex 32:14: Moses holds God by His garment, and God, *because He wants to be held*, accepts to be moved.

### 9.5 *Teshuvah* and *kipper*: two faces of the same movement

Philologically, *teshuvah* and *kipper* operate the same structure in two directions:

- *Teshuvah*: *the human* turns back, exposes the fault, asks to be covered.
- *Kipper*: *God* covers, bears the fault (*nośeʾ ʿavon*), effaces.

The Yom Kippur rite rigorously articulates the two. There is no *kpr* without prior *teshuvah* (*Yoma* 85b: *"Yom Kippur atones for the offences between a person and God, but not for those between a person and his fellow, until he appeases him"*). But there is also no *teshuvah* without the grace of a God who wants to be reached. Forgiveness is this *coincidence* of a human movement and a divine movement. Yom Kippur is the feast where this coincidence is replayed.

## 10. Comparative readings: Yom Kippur, Pauline *kapporet*, Qur'anic *takfīr* {#s10}

### 10.1 Yom Kippur and the Pauline *kapporet*

Article 1 of the dossier (§ 8) established that Romans 3:25 makes Christ a *hilastērion* — the term by which the Septuagint translates *kapporet*. Paul performs a *christologization* of Yom Kippur: the blood of Christ becomes what the blood of the bull was on the *kapporet*, but *"once for all"* (Hebrews 9:12). The Letter to the Hebrews (chapters 9-10) deploys this translation in detail: Christ as *high priest* enters the heavenly sanctuary, and his own blood operates the definitive *kpr*.

For the grammar of the dossier, this translation is a *midrashic exercise* on the rite of Lev 16. It does not deny Yom Kippur; it *recomposes* it. Whether this recomposition is legitimate in the eyes of rabbinic Judaism is a different question. But the *grammar mobilized* — that of priestly *kpr* — is rigorously the same.

### 10.2 The Qur'anic divine *takfīr* as structural parallel

Q 5:65 and Q 8:29 have *God* as their subject and use the verb *kaffara* in the sense of *"to cover, to efface"*:

> If only the People of the Book had believed and feared God, **We would have covered** (*lakaffarnā*) their evil deeds. (Q 5:65)

The verb *lakaffarnā* (*"We would have covered"*) is philologically the *strict equivalent* of Hebrew *kipper*. The grammar is the same: God interposes an act (forgiveness) between the fault and the judgement. The expiatory pole of the root *k-f-r* is explicitly preserved in the Qur'anic canon, and explicitly attributed to God. The sura is Medinan, hence late; it preserves the echo of Hebrew *kpr* in its original sense.

### 10.3 ʿĀshūrāʾ: the trace of 10 Tishri in Islamic liturgy

Bukhārī reports (*Ṣaḥīḥ*, *Kitāb al-Ṣawm* 69) that the Prophet, on his arrival in Medina, saw the Jews fasting on the day of ʿĀshūrāʾ — the tenth day of Muḥarram, first month of the Hijri calendar. When asked, they replied that it was *"the day on which God saved Moses and drowned Pharaoh."* The Prophet would have then said: *"We are closer to Moses than they are,"* and he fasted that day, prescribing it to his companions.

Several classical traditions (Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr) identify this *"tenth day"* with 10 Tishri — that is, Yom Kippur. The *fast of ʿĀshūrāʾ*, which remains recommended in Sunni Islam (and obligatory according to certain Shīʿī readings for distinct reasons connected to Karbala), would then be the Islamic *trace* of the Jewish 10 Tishri. If the identification is correct, Islamic liturgy preserves, in its calendar, the echo of Yom Kippur — just as Qur'anic *kaffāra* preserves in its lexicon the echo of Hebrew *kapparah*.

This continuity is consistent with the general thesis of the dossier: the root *kpr* has, even in its latest Arabic extensions, maintained its expiatory-positive pole. It is the modern polemical use of *kāfir* (article 3 of the dossier) that has obliterated this filiation, not the Qur'anic canon itself.

### 10.4 A common grammar of forgiveness as recovering-effacement

Yom Kippur, the Pauline *kapporet*, the Qur'anic divine *takfīr*: three distinct configurations of *one and the same grammar*. In all of them, God *covers* the fault so that the human face may appear again. In all of them, forgiveness is not a mere affective disposition but an *operation* — gesture, rite, sacrifice, word. In all of them, *kpr* is the central operator.

Theologies diverge — on whether the sacrifice is repeated or unique, on the mediation of priest or Christ, on unconditional grace or human cooperation. But the *grammar* is shared. And this shared grammar is exactly what modern polemical usage of the word *kāfir* has lost.

## 11. Conclusion: forgiveness as primary grammar {#s11}

Yom Kippur is the first midrashic feast in the rigorous sense of the term: a feast that *commemorates a narrative* by *operating a rite*. The narrative is Exodus 32-34 — rupture, anger, intercession, forgiveness. The rite is Leviticus 16 — sprinkling, prostration, confession, sending out. The 10 Tishri knots the two. Each year, that day brings back the memory of the first breaking and the possibility of the first forgiveness.

This articulation has a precise philological scope for the dossier. It deploys narratively what article 1 deployed structurally: that the cultic-expiatory pole of the root *kpr* was *narratively* born of a specific drama. Forgiveness, in the Semitic grammar, is not a theological abstraction. It is *that* event, replayed annually: a God who wanted to be held, a mediator who dared to hold, a people who learned that the fault could be *borne*.

And this articulation has a decisive comparative scope. The Pauline *kapporet* — Christ as *hilastērion* — is intelligible in this grammar and only in it. The Qur'anic divine *takfīr* — *"We would have covered their evil deeds"* — preserves the same grammar in its lexicon. The fast of ʿĀshūrāʾ — which may be the Islamic liturgical echo of 10 Tishri — preserves it in its calendar. **Three traditions, one common grammar: to cover the fault to make live.**

The contemporary word *kāfir*, become a word of anathema (article 3 of the dossier), has forgotten that it is, in the depth of its root, *the word of Yom Kippur*. The word of the Great Atonement. The word by which God, in Ex 32:14, *repented of the evil*. To remind it — through narrative, through letter, through liturgy — is to restore to forgiveness its primary grammar: that of a recovering that lets the face appear again. The face of the brother, the face of the people, the face of God.
