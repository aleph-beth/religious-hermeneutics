## 1. Introduction {#s1}

Any community that expands dreams of being eternal. Islam offers a singular case study in this respect: no religious community has conquered, within a century, so vast a territory; and none, perhaps, has known so many internal wars between co-religionists from its very first generation. This article defends a simple thesis: the expansion of Islam was not the remedy for its internal divisions but their engine. To absorb the enemy is to import his conflicts; and the absence of a recognized succession procedure leaves them insoluble except by arms. The phenomenon to be explained is not a destruction — Islam today counts nearly two billion believers — but a persistent fragmentation of politico-religious authority, proportional to the growth of the community.

One methodological clarification must be made from the outset. The sources distinguish what history attests from what tradition reports. The Qur'an, contemporary with the preaching, alludes to a real hostility (the expulsion of believers from their homes, Qur'an 22:39–40 and 2:217; plots against the Prophet, Qur'an 8:30) but offers no circumstantial account of it; the *Sira*, composed more than a century after the events, offers a complete and probably dramatized account. This article draws on both registers while keeping them distinct.

Finally, for the thesis to be discussable, its variables must be defined. By expansion we mean three distinct phenomena: territorial expansion (military conquest), the demographic expansion of converts (a slow process, measured by Bulliet), and integrated diversity (absorbed social structures). By internal war we mean an armed conflict opposing two claimants to power within the community of believers, and we will measure its scale by the number of rival powers present and the size of the forces engaged.

Plan: first, the absorption mechanism; second, the cycle tested against the facts; third, the law of amplification and its counter-examples; fourth, the comparative reach of the model; fifth, its limits; in conclusion, an opening.

## 2. The absorption mechanism: growing by importing the conflicts of others {#s2}

The first fact, attested by tradition and by historical criticism alike, is the conversion of the initial enemies. The *Quraysh* of Mecca, Muhammad's adversaries, were neither exterminated nor marginalized: they were integrated. The community does not define itself by excluding the adversary but by absorbing him. Yesterday's enemy becomes tomorrow's believer.

This mechanism has an immediate structural consequence: to integrate the enemy is to integrate his conflicts. The community grows by absorbing tribal structures, Persian elites, Byzantine aristocracies, Berber confederations, with their hierarchies, their loyalties and their grudges. Conversion does not dissolve old structures: it puts them at the service of new conflicts.

Three illustrations, of growing scope:

**The *Ridda* (632–633).** As soon as Muhammad died, allied tribes seceded, and Abu Bakr reintegrated them by force. This is the purest illustration of the mechanism: the absorbed enemy becomes an internal problem as soon as the question "who commands?" arises — in this case less than a year after the Prophet's death.

**Pre-existing monotheism.** The fact is no longer merely traditional: the Himyarite inscriptions of the 5th–6th centuries attest, epigraphically, an Arab monotheism of the cult of *Rahmanan* before Islam; Jewish tribes at Yathrib, Christians at Najran and in Syria complete the picture. Conquest does not spread monotheist faith to lands that ignore it: it re-codes pre-existing beliefs and unifies them under a new authority. Fred Donner defended the hypothesis of an initially ecumenical community of "Believers", including Jews and Christians, the exclusivization being later. The land to be converted was already, in large part, believing; the war was internal first.

**The *mawali*.** The non-Arab converts, placed in a subordinate position in the Umayyad empire (inferior fiscal and social status, limited access to high commands), constitute the limiting case of the mechanism: an enemy absorbed without being fully integrated. Their frustration was one of the engines of the Abbasid revolution. It is the exact illustration of old cleavages recomposed inside the community: the Persian conquest continues as an Arab-Persian civil war.

## 3. The cycle tested against the facts {#s3}

The mechanism unfolds in four stages:

1. the community binds itself together against an external enemy;
2. the defeated enemy is converted, hence integrated;
3. the common enemy gone, the community splits over the question of power;
4. a new external enemy is sought to glue the body back together, and the cycle begins again.

| Phase | Dates | Event | Type of conflict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preaching | 610–630 | Opposition of the *Quraysh*, capture of Mecca | Internal, Arab against Arab |
| *Ridda* | 632–633 | Secession of the allied tribes, reintegration by Abu Bakr | Internal |
| Conquests I | 633–656 | Persia, Syria, Egypt | External |
| First *fitna* | 656–661 | Ali against Muawiya | Internal |
| Conquests II | 661–680 | North Africa, Central Asia | External |
| Second *fitna* | 680–692 | Karbala, the rival caliphate of Ibn al-Zubayr | Internal |
| Third *fitna* | 744–750 | Abbasid revolution | Internal |
| Fourth *fitna* | 811–813 | Al-Amin against al-Ma'mun, two sons of the same caliph | Internal |
| Fragmentation | 756–1258 | Rival emirs and caliphates, three simultaneous caliphates | Internal, chronicled |
| Empires | 16th–18th c. | Ottomans against Safavids | Internal, at the scale of two empires |

Two observations must be made, one of which serves the thesis and the other corrects it.

**The first.** The exact chronology serves the thesis better than the simplified version. Less than twenty years after the first *fitna*, a second breaks out; the catastrophic succession of al-Amin and al-Ma'mun, two sons of the same caliph, shows that no procedure had been stabilized after a hundred and eighty years of empire. The four *fitnas*, recognized as such by Sunni tradition itself, are central, not marginal.

**The second.** The cycle does not unfold in successive phases but in overlap. One conquers and kills one's own simultaneously; the external fronts freeze during the *fitnas* (conquests stall in 656–661 and in 680–692) and then resume. The model must therefore be reformulated: the alternation is not a sequence but a permanent tension between two contradictory needs — the external enemy that binds and the internal succession that divides — with expansion serving precisely as an anesthetic to the second.

---

## 4. The law of amplification: more expansion, more internal wars {#s4}

If the cycle were simply repetitive, it would condemn the community to stagnation. Yet history shows an amplification: each stage of growth coincides with a larger internal war than the previous one. This is not an accident but a logical consequence, for three reasons.

**The price of power grows with size.** In a restricted community, succession is worth commanding an oasis. At the scale of an empire, it is worth provinces, armies and treasuries. Peter Turchin formalized this mechanism under the name of elite overproduction: the number of qualified claimants grows faster than the positions to be filled, which makes the succession conflict structural rather than conjunctural.

**Absorbed diversity recomposes into factions.** Islam's internal wars almost always take up old cleavages in confessional garb: Persians and *mawali* against the Arab establishment in the Abbasid revolution, tribal cleavages in al-Andalus, the Turko-Persian opposition between Ottomans and Safavids, the persistence of Shiism on the Iranian margin.

**Consensus becomes geographically impossible.** A community of a few thousand people holds together through proximity and face-to-face contact. Ten thousand kilometres away, between Sindh and Cordoba, agreement on "who commands and in whose name" can no longer be maintained by deliberation, but only by force.

This pattern has a precursor that must be named, because he formulated it before us: Ibn Khaldun, whose cycle of *asabiyya* describes the conquering cohesion that dissolves into the luxury of empire. Our thesis is a displacement of his: the factor of dissolution is not only the softness of dynasties but the sheer mass of what has been absorbed. Richard Bulliet provides here an almost quantitative support: his conversion curve (*Conversion to Islam in the Medieval Period*, 1979) shows that mass conversion follows conquest with centuries of delay, deep Islamization reaching the provinces around the 9th–10th century, precisely when political fragmentation generalizes.

The counter-examples remain, and must be faced. There are long phases of relative internal peace: the Ottoman Empire between 1600 and 1800, outside the Safavid front; the Mughal Empire at its apogee; the Umayyad Empire between two *fitnas*. They do not refute the model but specify it: internal war does not erupt continuously, it accumulates. Phases of stability correspond either to a univocal balance of forces (a power strong enough to deter any claim) or to an expansion still under way that supplies the enemy and the positions to be distributed. War returns when either fails. Likewise, contemporary Sunni-Shiite conflicts largely belong to the logics of nation-states, decolonization and external interventions: the conquest-absorption mechanism applies to them only indirectly, and the article does not claim to extend it without caution.

Finally, the complementary hypothesis about tradition must be presented for what it is: a hypothesis. The need, for the *Sira*, to rewrite the origins as a pure confrontation with polytheism may have served to mask the internal conflict, the central problem of the first centuries. This reading is consistent with the model but does not confirm it: it is an interpretive extension of it, to be held as such.

## 5. Comparative reach: the succession procedure, not the religion {#s5}

The model would be weak if it did not isolate the decisive factor. The contrast with rival doctrines brings it out, provided the comparison is conducted honestly.

Rome did not survive its succession wars through "legal fiction": the crisis of the 3rd century saw dozens of emperors in fifty years, almost all products of civil wars. Byzantium, precisely, did not separate throne and altar, and constitutes the most direct counter-example to any explanation by the mere separation of powers. As for Latin Christendom, it did not avoid wars between co-religionists: the Wars of Religion and the Thirty Years' War are comparable in scale to the greatest *fitnas*.

This counter-example, far from ruining the thesis, specifies it: Latin Christendom confirms the model (expansion and internal wars at once) but shows that the empirical separation of throne and altar does not suppress these wars; it displaces them between states, and only after Westphalia (1648), the date from which the principle of politico-religious unity ceased to be the norm in Europe. The explanatory factor emerges rectified: what produces internal war is neither Islam in particular nor the fusion of the religious and the political in general, but the absence of a recognized succession procedure, backed by a claim to total authority. Crone and Hinds (*God's Caliph*, 1986) showed how thoroughly the Umayyad caliphate fused religious and political authority, the caliph proclaiming himself God's own representative: it is this fusion that makes the question "who commands?" theologically undecidable, and therefore militarily soluble. Madelung (*The Succession to Muhammad*, 1997) establishes that this undecidability is constitutive, the succession of the Prophet having been the object of no procedural designation recognized by all parties.

For the general reach of the absorption mechanism, two theoretical references: Carl Schmitt, for whom political unity is constituted by the designation of the enemy, and René Girard, whose theory of the scapegoat describes the cohesion obtained by designating an outside. The model presented here is its inverse: a community that absorbs its enemy instead of designating him renounces its factor of cohesion and imports, with him, the conflict.

## 6. Limits of the model {#s6}

Three limits must be stated, lest the model immunize itself.

**The contemporary period.** From the 19th century on, the internal wars of the Muslim world are explained more and more by colonialism, decolonization, nation-states and external interventions. Extending the law of amplification up to the present would require showing that these modern factors recompose or mask the old mechanism rather than replace it. The article holds this extension to be an analogy, not a demonstration.

**External ruptures.** The fall of Baghdad (1258) and the Mongol irruption interrupt the institutional continuity of the caliphate; later internal wars unfold in a transformed political framework. The model holds for a community whose authority claims universality; it does not apply mechanically to ordinary kingdoms and empires.

**Refutation criteria.** The model would be refuted if one observed, at comparable community size, internal wars rarer or smaller after the great absorptions than before them; or if a recognized succession procedure, at constant politico-religious fusion, durably suppressed the *fitnas*. The Byzantine case, which combines absence of separation with long survival through a stabilized dynastic procedure, already tests the second branch and invites insistence on the single factor that resists: a recognized procedure is lacking precisely where total authority is claimed.

---

## 7. Conclusion {#s7}

The expansion of Islam was a prodigious machine for absorbing enemies, peoples and beliefs; in doing so, it was a machine for importing conflicts, whose resolution, in the absence of a recognized succession procedure, was each time achieved by arms between co-religionists. The law of amplification is verified over the first century with particular clarity (the *Ridda*, four *fitnas*) and extends, with the stated precautions, to the Sunni-Shiite wars of empires. The decisive factor is not a religious essence but a configuration: total authority claimed, succession undecidable, growth through absorption.

## 9. Threads left open {#s9}

**Does rabbinic Judaism offer a counter-model?** A community without territorial expansion after 70, it would hold together through procedure (*halakha*, instituted controversy) rather than through the enemy.

**Does the absorption model apply to universalist secular ideologies?** Such as communism and the Sino-Soviet split, where the internal enemy succeeds the class enemy.

**Can the law of amplification be transposed to modern organizations?** Firms growing through acquisitions, or political coalitions, where one also absorbs the conflicts of those one integrates.
