## 1. Two series, one history {#s1}

Al-Fārābī writes his *Virtuous City* while the caliph of Baghdad ceases to govern; Ibn Rushd dies in disgrace the year the Almohad empire begins to recede; Ibn Khaldūn invents the sociology of history after watching Damascus burn. **Arab thought does not comment on the history of Islam: it takes place inside it.**

This chronology sets the two series face to face — on one side political and institutional events, on the other the thinkers — to make legible the coincidences that textbooks keep apart. It covers fourteen centuries, from the first revelation (610) to the present, divided into seven periods. Each period opens with a short reading note, then aligns its historical landmarks and its intellectual figures.

Six traditions are distinguished by colour: *falsafa* (philosophy of Greek inspiration), *kalām* and theology, Sufism and *ishrāq*, the sciences and translation, Arabic-speaking Jews and Christians, and finally the Nahḍa and contemporary thought. This classification is a reading convenience, not a taxonomy: Ghazālī belongs to kalām and to Sufism, Mullā Ṣadrā to falsafa and to ishrāq, and it is precisely the passage from one column to the other that makes the history.

## 2. Overview: 1,400 years in a single band {#s2}

The band below condenses the whole: each bar is a life, each vertical tick a historical landmark. It makes three densities visible — the century of Baghdad (800–950), the century without a caliph (950–1050), the Nahḍa (1850–2010) — and two troughs, the fourteenth century after the plague and the eighteenth century before Bonaparte, which, as will be seen below, are partly artefacts.

## 3. 610 – 750 · A community before a doctrine {#s3}

The first century has no philosophers: it has jurists, collectors of traditions and civil wars. The questions that will make theology — who is a believer? is man free? is the Qurʾān created? — are born first as political stakes of the fitna, before being formulated as speculative problems in Basra and Damascus.

## 4. 750 – 950 · Baghdad translates, and begins to think in Greek {#s4}

In two generations, almost the whole Greek scientific and philosophical corpus passes into Arabic — carried by paper, state patronage and mostly Christian workshops. Muʿtazilism conquers power and then loses it with the miḥna; al-Kindī and then al-Fārābī invent a philosophy that speaks Greek in Arabic; al-Ashʿarī turns its tools against it. Everything that follows starts here.

## 5. 950 – 1100 · The caliph no longer governs, philosophy culminates {#s5}

The century in which Abbasid power is reduced to a symbol is also the most fertile: Avicenna, al-Bīrūnī, Ibn al-Haytham, ʿAbd al-Jabbār, Ibn Gabirol. Political fragmentation multiplies courts and commissions. Then the Seljuks restore the Sunni order and Niẓām al-Mulk gives it its schools — knowledge gains an institution and loses part of its freedom.

## 6. 1100 – 1258 · The Muslim West, the apogee and the break {#s6}

Al-Ghazālī puts falsafa on trial, and it is from al-Andalus that the reply comes: Ibn Ṭufayl, Ibn Rushd, while Maimonides writes his Guide in Arabic. Ibn ʿArabī and Suhrawardī open another path, metaphysical and visionary. Within half a century, Muslim Spain collapses, Latin Europe translates everything, and Baghdad burns.

## 7. 1258 – 1500 · After the catastrophe: scholasticism, critique, a science of history {#s7}

The narrative of “decline” does not survive scrutiny well. Al-Ṭūsī saves Avicennism under the Mongols and founds Marāgha; Ījī, Taftāzānī and al-Jurjānī build a scholasticism of extreme technicality, taught for four centuries; Ibn Taymiyya conducts the most radical critique of Greek logic; Ibn Khaldūn invents a science of civilisation that no one will take up before the nineteenth century.

## 8. 1500 – 1800 · Three empires, and an Iranian metaphysics {#s8}

Ottomans, Safavids and Mughals share the Muslim world and fix confessional frontiers. Against the idea of an extinguished thought, the school of Isfahan produces with Mullā Ṣadrā one of the most ambitious metaphysical syntheses of the tradition. At the margins, Sirhindī, Shāh Walī Allāh and Ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb each answer differently the same question: what is to be done when the order comes undone?

## 9. 1800 – today · Thinking under the gaze of the other {#s9}

From the Egyptian expedition to the present, Arab thought works within a balance of power: Europe is at once model and occupier. Reform, liberalism, nationalism, Islamism, then historical criticism of the corpus — Arkoun, al-Jābrī, Abū Zayd, Mernissi — and the price sometimes paid for practising it: censorship, trials, exile.

## 10. What the parallel reveals, and what it hides {#s10}

Three regularities leap out once the two series are aligned.

**Thought does not follow power.** The philosophically densest century (950–1050) is the one in which the Abbasid caliphate had ceased to govern, and Ibn Rushd, Maimonides and Ibn ʿArabī come out of a Muslim West in the process of losing Spain.

**The great doctrinal ruptures follow political defeats** rather than precede them: the miḥna, 1258, 1798 and 1967 each reorganise the field of what can be thought.

**Institutional thresholds matter more than the dates of reigns**: paper after Talas, the Niẓāmiyya madrasa, the late printing press, the colonial university.

But a timeline is a crude instrument. It favours the authors who were preserved over the milieus that carried them, aligns lives where manuscripts circulated, and leaves out of the frame the anonymous transmitters, the learned women rarely credited, and the oral traditions.

## 12. Threads left open {#s12}

**Is the “decline” after 1258 a fact or an artefact of our sources?** Recent research on the post-classical period (Ījī, Taftāzānī, Dawānī, the school of Isfahan) shows a continuous philosophical activity that Orientalist historiography long ignored.

**What is an “Arab” philosopher?** Ibn Sīnā, al-Fārābī, al-Ghazālī and Suhrawardī are Persian; Maimonides and Ibn Gabirol are Jewish; Ḥunayn is Christian. It is the *language*, not ethnicity or confession, that makes the intellectual community.

**Did Ghazālī's *Tahāfut* “kill” falsafa?** Renan's thesis is untenable: Ibn Rushd answers him a century later, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī integrates Avicennism into kalām, and Safavid Iran builds a metaphysics of existence.

**How should the Nahḍa be read: renaissance or trauma?** From Ṭahṭāwī to al-Jābrī, modern Arab thought is written facing a West that is at once model and occupier — a condition the timeline makes visible but does not explain.
