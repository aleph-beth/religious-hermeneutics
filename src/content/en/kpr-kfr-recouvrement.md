## 1. Introduction: one root, seven poles {#s1}

The hypothesis of this article is that a single common Semitic root — **k-p-r / כ־פ־ר / ك-ف-ر** — carries a unique gestural core, *to apply a layer, to cover*, which has unfolded into several competing registers across the traditions inheriting the ancient Levantine background. The bitumen that caulks Noah's ark, the gold lid on the Ark of the Covenant, the sprinkled blood that *kappers* on the altar, the ransom (*kofer*) that redeems a life, the peasant (*kāfir*) who buries the seed, the ungrateful person who *covers* the gift received, the denier who veils the truth — all these gestures mobilize the *same* root and *one* identical material gesture.

This polysemy is not a lexical accident. It is the expression of a *Semitic meditation on the anthropological status of recovering*. The gesture is neutral in its materiality; it tilts into two opposed economies according to what the hand expects: that something *rise* (the seed, the effaced sin, the sealed ark) or that something *be silenced* (the spilled blood, the denied sign, the ingratitude that erases the memory of the gift).

The programme of this article is to follow this root from its oldest attestations — the Atrahasis epic, the Gilgamesh epic — to the ritual, narrative, and juridical strata of the Tanakh, the Gospels, the Qur'an, and classical Islamic jurisprudence. The aim is not to prove a linear transmission (the dating forbids any simplification) but to bring to light a *shared semantic operator*, of which each tradition has held together several poles — whereas dominant modern usage will, as we shall see, reduce that richness to a single polemical register.

## 2. Material stratum: *kupru* and *kofer* {#s2}

### 2.1 Atrahasis and Gilgamesh

The Babylonian Atrahasis epic (Old Babylonian recension, c. 18th century BCE) describes the antediluvian hero's construction of the ark with bitumen:

> *kupru qadu šassūru ibašši* … *šaman ʿuṣiyatti ibakkir*
>
> *"The bitumen (*kupru*) and the matrix were there… he was pouring cedar oil."* (Atrahasis III ii 9-13, ed. Lambert-Millard 1969)

The Gilgamesh epic (standard recension, c. 1200 BCE) takes up exactly the same technique:

> *iḫrî ina qerbiša ina kuprim apzuq*
>
> *"Within, I caulked it with bitumen (*kupru*)."* (Gilgamesh XI 53-58, ed. George 2003)

The Akkadian verb *kapāru* (*"to wipe, to rub, to ritually purify"*) and the noun *kupru* (*"bitumen"*) belong to one and the same root. The gesture is *technical* before being *moral*: one *applies a layer* to make watertight, to purify, to bring across.

### 2.2 Genesis 6:14 — the Hebrew caulking of the ark

The Hebrew flood narrative takes up the Mesopotamian formula without any rupture:

> Make yourself an ark of gopher wood; make rooms in the ark, and **cover (*kafarta*) it inside and out with pitch (*kofer*)**. (Gen 6:14)

The root appears twice in a single verse: verb (*kafarta*) and noun (*kofer*). The Akkadian-Hebrew cognate is strict; the Hebrew narrative takes up the Mesopotamian formula of ritual ark-caulking.

### 2.3 Genesis 11:3 — Babel and the lexical shift

At Babel, the same bitumen receives a different name:

> Brick served them as stone, and **bitumen (*ḥemar*)** served them as mortar. (Gen 11:3)

The word here is not *kofer* but *ḥemar* — an Aramaic-Akkadian loan (Akk. *ḫemāru*), designating the same substance. The lexical shift is significant: *the ark is *kofer* (cultic root), Babel is *ḥemar* (mere material substance)*. The same bitumen separates two theologies of recovering. Noah's ark saves through covering; the tower of Babel fails because its covering is severed from any relation to God. Language itself, already, distinguishes the saving seal from the Promethean seal.

## 3. Cultic stratum: *kapporet*, *kippur*, blood {#s3}

### 3.1 The *kapporet* of the Holy of Holies

The great Hebrew innovation is to make *kpr* the central operator of the priestly sacrificial system. Leviticus 1-7, 16, 17 articulate the entire expiatory liturgy around the verb *kipper*. The **kapporet** (Ex 25:17-22; Lev 16:2) is the gold lid surmounting the Ark of the Covenant, *the most holy object of the Tabernacle*. The Septuagint translates it as ἱλαστήριον — *"propitiatory object"* —, a term Paul will take up in Romans 3:25 to qualify the Christ.

Leviticus 16:14 describes the central ritual of Yom Kippur:

> He shall take some of the blood of the bull and sprinkle it with his finger on the front of the *kapporet* on the east side.

The gesture is precise: blood is sprinkled *on* the *kapporet*. The verb *kipper* then means *to interpose a layer* between the sin and the divine gaze. Priestly theology posits a *physics of the sacred*: sin leaves a material trace contaminating the sanctuary; an act is required that *covers* this trace.

### 3.2 Leviticus 17:11 — blood that *kappers*

> For the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it for you on the altar **to *kapper*** for your souls; for it is the blood that *kappers* by the life.

The verse is one of the densest of priestly theology. Blood acts not by its material value but by the life (*nefesh*) it carries. *Kpr* is here the *transfer* of a life onto the altar — interposition of an animal life between the human sin and divine holiness.

### 3.3 Exodus 30:12 — the *kofer* as ransom

> Each shall give a ransom (*kofer*) for his life to the LORD… so that no plague will come upon them when they are numbered.

The same word — lid, expiation, ransom — is brought back to a single gesture: *interposing a substitute between subject and sanction*. The juridical pole shares the grammar of the cultic pole. *Kofer-nefesh*: the life redeemed by a gift that *takes its place*.

## 4. Agrarian stratum: Isaac sows, Lazarus reaps {#s4}

### 4.1 Genesis 26:12 — the hundredfold

> Isaac sowed in that land, and reaped in that year **a hundredfold** (*meʾah sheʿarim*); and the LORD blessed him.

The Hebrew verb for "to sow" is *zaraʿ*, not *kafar*. But the agrarian gesture — *burying the seed so that it may rise* — is semantically akin to *recovering*: the sower applies a layer of earth on the seed for it to germinate. It is a *kpr* in agrarian *execution*, even if not in lexicon. The Isaac verse posits the founding motif of *recovering-for-rising*.

### 4.2 Psalm 126 and Hosea 10 — sowers of deferred salvation

> Those who sow in tears shall reap with shouts of joy. He who goes out weeping, bearing the seed for sowing, shall come home with shouts of joy, bringing his sheaves with him. (Ps 126:5-6)

Hosea 10:12: *"Sow for yourselves righteousness."*

The agrarian gesture becomes a figure of blessing and *deferred salvation*. It structurally prepares the synoptic parable of the sower and the archaic sense of the Arabic *kāfir*.

### 4.3 The synoptic parable of the sower (Matt 13:3-9)

> Behold, a sower went out to sow. And as he sowed, some seeds fell along the path, and the birds came and devoured them… Other seeds fell on rocky ground… Other seeds fell among thorns… Other seeds fell on good soil and produced grain, some a hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty.

The hundredfold (ἑκατόν) explicitly cites Gen 26:12 (*meʾah sheʿarim*). But the structurally decisive element lies elsewhere: **the evil is concentrated in three figures of non-reception** — the bird that devours the unburied seed, the rock that prevents rooting, the thorn that chokes the shoot. **The sower himself is exempt from any reproach.** He sows broadly, without condition.

This apparently innocuous philological datum will be, as we shall see in the final section, the pivotal argument of the whole dossier.

### 4.4 John 12:24 — the grain that dies

> Truly, truly, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.

A decisive Johannine displacement: Christ is no longer the sower (Matt 13), he is *the seed*. The archaic *kpr* gesture (covering for rising) becomes the christological image of death-and-resurrection. Joseph of Arimathea (Matt 27:60; John 19:38-42), who *wraps* the body in a shroud and rolls the stone before the tomb, structurally performs the gesture of the *kāfir*-peasant: he covers the seed so that it may sprout. The stone rolled before the sepulchre is, in this grammar, an *inverted kapporet* — a lid that, when opened, will release the resurrection.

## 5. Pre-Islamic Arabic stratum: the polysemous *al-kāfir* {#s5}

### 5.1 The poetry of the *Muʿallaqāt*

Pre-Quranic Arabic knows the root *k-f-r* in senses where religious pejoration is absent. Several poetic occurrences of the 6th century attest this neutral or positive sense:

- *al-kāfir* = the night (which *covers* the day) — Labīd, *Muʿallaqa*; Imruʾ al-Qays, *Muʿallaqa*;
- *al-kāfir* = the dark cloud (bearer of rain) — Tarafa, *Muʿallaqa*;
- *al-kāfir* = the sea (which covers everything) — al-Aṣmaʿī, *Aṣmaʿiyyāt*;
- *al-kāfir* = the cuirass, the sheath — ʿAntara, *Muʿallaqa*;
- *al-kāfir* = the sower (who buries the seed) — sense preserved in the classical lexicography and attested by Q 57:20 (cf. § 6).

### 5.2 Classical lexicography

Ibn Manẓūr, in his *Lisān al-ʿArab* (composed at the end of the 13th century, but compiling earlier authorities), gathers these senses under the entry *kafara*:

> *"al-kāfir: al-layl al-muẓlim* (the dark night); *al-kāfir: al-baḥr* (the sea); *al-kāfir: al-saḥāb al-muẓlim* (the dark cloud); *al-kāfir: al-zāriʿ* (the sower); *al-kāfir: al-darʿ* (the cuirass); *al-kāfir: kullu mā ghaṭṭā shayʾan* (everything that covers something else)."*

E. W. Lane, in his *Arabic-English Lexicon* (vol. VII, pp. 2620-2622), confirms all these senses.

**Philological conclusion.** Pre-Islamic Arab society massively knew the root *k-f-r* in descriptive and technical uses. The pejorative religious sense — *"unbeliever"* — is *not* the primary sense: it is a *specialization* that occurs in the Qur'anic preaching, against the polemical background of the Quraysh idolaters.

## 6. Qur'anic stratum: the threefold polarity preserved {#s6}

### 6.1 Q 57:20 — the philological fossil

> Know that the life of this world is but play and amusement… like rain whose vegetation amazes **the *kuffār* (the sowers)**, then it withers, and you see it turn yellow, then it becomes broken straw. (Q 57:20)

Tabarī, Zamakhsharī, Rāzī are unanimous: here *al-kuffār* = *al-zurrāʿ*, the labourers, those who *cover* the seed. No classical reader has been mistaken on this point. This occurrence is a *fossil*: it attests, at the very heart of the canon, that the word can mean *peasant*. It preserves, within the sacred text, the memory of an earlier state of the root.

### 6.2 Q 14:7 — the great *shukr / kufr* opposition

> If you are grateful, I will surely give you more; but if you **cover up** (*kafartum*), surely My punishment is severe. (Q 14:7)

*Kufr* is here the *fundamental ingratitude* — the one who *covers* the gift received in order no longer to owe it. Antonym: the *shākir* who *manifests* recognition. The pair *shukr/kufr* is the first major opposition of Meccan preaching. Before being a word of anathema, *kufr* is an anthropological flaw: forgetting the gift.

### 6.3 Q 2:7 — the constellation of recovering

> God has **sealed** (*khatama*) their hearts and their hearing; over their eyes there is a **veil** (*ghishāwa*). (Q 2:7)

*Kufr* is here associated with a whole lexical constellation of recovering: *khatm* (seal), *ghishāwa* (veil), *labbasa* (to clothe, Q 2:42), *katama* (to stifle, Q 2:42), *ḥijāb* (curtain, Q 17:45). The grammar of Qur'anic *kufr* is not that of logical negation but of *occultation*.

### 6.4 Q 5:65 — divine *takfīr*

> If only the People of the Book had believed and feared God, **We would have covered** (*lakaffarnā*) their evil deeds. (Q 5:65)

A capital verse. Here the verb *kaffara* has *God* as its subject, and it means *"to efface by covering."* The expiatory pole of the root — *strict equivalent of the Hebrew *kipper*** — is therefore preserved in the Qur'anic canon and explicitly attributed to God. *Takfīr*, in the Qur'an, is a *divine and merciful* act. This datum will be of the highest importance for the rest of the dossier.

### 6.5 Q 5:89 — the ritual *kaffāra*

> Its expiation (*kaffāra*) is to feed ten poor persons.

The ritual expiation for a broken oath — *kaffārat al-yamīn* — is the juridical application of the positive pole of the root. Classical *fiqh* will devote entire chapters to *kaffārāt* (expiations): for broken oaths, for involuntary homicide (Q 4:92), for *ẓihār* (Q 58:3-4), for breaking of the fast. All function on the model of Hebrew *kpr*: interposing an act (gift, fasting, manumission) that *covers* the fault.

### 6.6 Recapitulation

The same root, in Qur'anic Arabic, thus designates *three simultaneous gestures*:
- the *negative* human gesture of *kufr* — covering the truth, the gift, the sign;
- the *positive* divine gesture of *takfīr* — covering the fault, that is, effacing it;
- the ritual act of expiation (*kaffāra*) — strict equivalent of the Hebrew *kapparah*.

The Qur'anic canon thus preserves, *within itself*, the threefold polarity of the root. **It is post-Qur'anic usage that will reduce this threefold to a single pole.**

## 7. The semantic doublet: good and bad recovering {#s7}

At every register of the root, the gesture can tilt into two opposed economies: *covering-to-make-live* (sealing the ark, protecting the seed, atoning for the fault, ransoming a life); *covering-to-make-disappear* (concealing murder, masking the sign, stifling truth, sealing the heart). It is *the same gesture*, but inversely oriented.

| Pole | Good recovering | Bad recovering |
|---|---|---|
| Material | *Kofer* of the ark (Gen 6:14) | *Ḥemar* of Babel (Gen 11:3) |
| Cultic | *Kapporet* (Lev 16) | Blood of Abel uncovered (Gen 4:10-11) |
| Juridical | *Kofer-nefesh* (Ex 30:12) | False payment, false oath |
| Agrarian | Sower (Gen 26; Matt 13) | Bird, rock, thorn (Matt 13) |
| Ethical | *Shukr* (gratitude) | *Kufr* / ingratitude (Q 14:7) |
| Theological | Confession (*shahāda*) | Refusal of the sign (Q 6:27) |
| Mystical | Effacement of the ego | Pretended sectarianism (Hallāj, Ibn ʿArabī) |

The anthropological drama of the root is this bifurcation. The material gesture is rigorously identical; everything depends on what the hand expects (rising or silence).

**The Cainite connection.** Negative Qur'anic *kufr* is structurally the gesture of Cain in Gen 4:10-11: covering the brother's blood with earth — a recovering *that does not hold*, because the earth, unlike the seed, does not *hide* the blood: *it cries it out*. *"The voice of your brother's blood cries out to me from the adamah!"* Qur'anic *kāfir*, in its pejorative sense, is the *linguistic Cain* of the root: the one who practices bad recovering, recovering-that-wants-to-make-disappear. This articulation will be central to the later articles of the dossier.

## 8. Pauline christologization: Romans 3:25 {#s8}

Romans 3:25 contains one of the most striking hermeneutic operations of the New Testament:

> Whom God put forward as a propitiation (*hilastērion*) by his blood, to be received by faith.

The word ἱλαστήριον is *exactly* the Septuagint translation of *kapporet*. Paul thus performs a *christological translation* of the *kapporet* onto the body of Christ. The blood of Christ becomes what the blood of the bull and the goat were doing on the *kapporet*: the central *kpr* gesture, but now *accomplished once and for all*. The Letter to the Hebrews (9:5-12) redoubles this translation and makes the typology explicit.

For Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*, 2012), Bauckham (*Jesus and the God of Israel*, 2008), Witztum (*Syriac Milieu*, 2011), this translation is conscious and midrashic. Origen, in his *Homilies on Leviticus* IX, reads Lev 16 as *"hidden sacrament of salvation"* (*sacramentum reconditum*) — the Hebrew *kpr* as a *"veil"* under which the Christ may be glimpsed. A typological procedure rigorously rabbinic in its structure.

Joseph of Arimathea, in this grammar, is not a mere secondary character of the Passion: he is *the priest-recoverer* who performs the *kpr* of the tomb-furrow. The shroud he wraps, the stone he rolls before the sepulchre — every gesture is a *kapparah*. Christian soteriology thus integrates *kpr* at its core, by making *recovering-for-rising* the archetype of Easter.

## 9. Philological capture: the sower becomes a renegade {#s9}

The Arabic evolution of the root calls for a final remark that constitutes one of the most striking knots of the dossier.

In pre-Quranic Arabic, *al-kāfir* is, among other things, *the sower* — the one whose gesture is *protective* of the seed. Q 57:20 preserves this usage in the canon. But the post-Quranic evolution has gradually crushed all the other poles under the theological-polemical pole: *kāfir* has become, for the majority of speakers, a quasi-univocal word meaning *"unbeliever,"* with a stable and negative polemical charge.

**Yet — the coincidence is philologically vertiginous — the synoptic parable of the sower (Matt 13) places the evil *not in the sowing gesture, but in the figures of non-reception*: the bird, the rock, the thorn. The sower himself is *the friend of the seed*.**

An unexpected consequence: contemporary usage of polemical *kāfir* has, by semantic capture, *given the name of the evangelical sower to the bird that devours the seed*. The word that designated *the hand that covers to make live* has been redeployed to designate *the one who covers to make silent*. It is the same material operation (covering), but in two opposed finalities (life / death). Language itself protests: Q 57:20 attests, within the canon, that there is *another* *kāfir*.

This inversion will be at the heart of article 5 of the dossier, which develops the evangelical grammar of the sower applied to the contemporary debate on *takfīr*.

## 10. Conclusion: seven poles to hold together {#s10}

At the end of this philological journey, the root *k-p-r / k-f-r* presents itself as a *semantic operator of rare richness*. Seven attested poles, articulated around a single gestural core:

1. **Material pole** — *kupru*, *kofer*: to seal, to caulk (Atrahasis; Gen 6:14).
2. **Cultic-expiatory pole** (human → God) — *kapporet*, *kipper*, *hilastērion*: to efface the fault by interposition (Lev 16; Rom 3:25).
3. **Juridical-ransom pole** — *kofer-nefesh*, *kaffāra*: to substitute a payment (Ex 30:12; Q 5:89).
4. **Expiatory pole** (God → human) — divine *takfīr*, *yukaffir*: merciful effacement (Q 5:65; 8:29).
5. **Agrarian pole** — *al-kāfir* peasant, *ho speirōn*: to bury the seed for rising (Q 57:20; Matt 13).
6. **Ethical-relational pole** — *kufr* against *shukr*: to forget the gift (Q 14:7).
7. **Theological-polemical pole** — *kāfir* against *muʾmin*: to refuse the truth (Q 47:1-3).

Four of these poles are positive or neutral; three are negative. **The native balance of the root is not negative.**

The whole history of the root, in the inheriting traditions, will be the history of the choices they make among these seven poles — which to privilege, which to efface, which to articulate. This is the history reconstituted by article 2 of the dossier, *A History of Readings*. And this richness, as we shall see, is what dominant modernity will have flattened.
