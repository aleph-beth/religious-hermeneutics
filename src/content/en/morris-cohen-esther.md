## 1. Telling Cohen, China and Esther apart {#s1}

**The most accurate historical version is this: Morris “Two-Gun” Cohen really was a close associate and bodyguard of Sun Yat-sen, but nothing establishes that he single-handedly changed China's 1947 vote on the partition of Palestine.** The China that sat at the UN at the time was the nationalist Republic of China, not the People's Republic of China founded two years later. It abstained when Resolution 181, which recommended a partition plan, was adopted; it neither “voted for Israel” that day nor gave up a veto in the Security Council. The story of a decisive intervention by Cohen exists in biographies and in communal memory, but the best available studies place it within a wider web of Zionist contacts, Chinese diplomatic choices and American pressure.

**The comparison with Esther does not claim a historical link between the two stories.** It works on a broader Jewish imagination: that of salvation arriving, in a moment of peril, through the action of one singular person. Esther, Mordecai, the righteous one, the deliverer and, in another register, the Messiah: these figures do not merge, but they give shape to one and the same question. How can a people's history turn on the word, the courage or the presence of an individual? The historical record supplies the material of Cohen's story; the Book of Esther and the messianic horizon supply the language in which that question can be thought.

## 2. Cohen was genuinely close to Sun Yat-sen {#s2}

Morris Abraham Cohen, born in 1887 and died in 1970, arrived in Shanghai in 1922. Serious biographical sources present him as **bodyguard, aide-de-camp and collaborator of Sun Yat-sen**, then of Soong Qingling, Sun's widow; they also report his later ties with Chinese nationalist circles (National Library of Israel). His nickname “Two-Gun” refers to the reputation as an armed man that he himself cultivated; a photograph shows him behind Sun, and a study by the Society for Anglo-Chinese Understanding documents both that closeness and the origin of the nickname (SACU, *China Eye*).

This biographical foundation is not enough, however, to make Cohen a maker of Chinese policy. The stories around him were embellished very early, sometimes by Cohen himself. The SACU study stresses that there is no evidence he commanded troops in battle and notes the exaggerations repeated by his first biography; the historian Aron Shai, in his history of China–Israel relations, describes a tradition largely shaped by oral histories and colourful characters (Shai, *China and Israel*). **Cohen is therefore a credible intermediary, not proof of a personal ability to steer the vote of Beijing or Nanjing.**

---

## 3. San Francisco in 1945 is not New York in 1947 {#s3}

The legend arises partly from two episodes being superimposed. An academic study by Meron Medzini establishes that Cohen was called to **San Francisco in spring 1945**, when the UN was being founded, to help Zionist diplomats secure Chinese support for maintaining the British Mandate over Palestine. At that point there was not yet any vote on the creation of a Jewish state (Medzini, *Between Mumbai and Manila*, pp. 115-116).

In 1947, Zionist organisations sought the support of the Republic of China, or failing that its abstention. Later accounts claim that Cohen presented Wu Tieh-cheng with a letter from Sun Yat-sen favourable to Zionism and thereby obtained a reversal; this version is explicitly reported “according to Cohen” and, in the accessible sources, does not rest on any independent diplomatic archive (Jewish Policy Center). Medzini, by contrast, judges the effect of this action marginal: he links China's abstention to the work of diplomats such as Wellington Koo and T. F. Tsiang, in a context of **strong American pressure**.

The historical conclusion is clear: **Cohen may have served as a contact and a lobbyist; a decisive causal role in 1947 is not demonstrated.** The legend is meaningful, because it shows what a community chooses to remember of a figure; it must not replace the documentary chain of state decisions.

## 4. The Republic of China abstains, without holding a veto {#s4}

On 29 November 1947, the General Assembly adopted Resolution 181 (II) by **33 votes in favour, 13 against and 10 abstentions**. China was among those ten abstentions (official voting record and UN history). That China was the **Republic of China**, the nationalist government then holding China's UN seat; the People's Republic of China was proclaimed only on 1 October 1949 and took over China's representation at the UN only in 1971. Speaking of “communist China” in connection with this vote is therefore anachronistic.

A second correction is essential. The partition plan did not go through a hypothetical Chinese veto in the Security Council: it came from UNSCOP, was examined by an ad hoc committee of the General Assembly, and was then submitted to its plenary session. Resolution 181 **recommends** that the United Kingdom and the other members adopt and implement the plan; it then provides a role for the Security Council on matters of implementation (*Yearbook of the United Nations 1947-48*). China therefore did not “give up a veto” that would have blocked the project from reaching the Assembly. Its abstention carried political weight, but on the final result alone, a Chinese “no” would not have pushed the 33 favourable votes below the two-thirds majority of those voting.

The later chronology is also more nuanced than the legend. The Republic of China voted **in favour** of the Security Council's recommendation to admit Israel on 4 March 1949, then for Israel's admission in the General Assembly on 11 May, adopted by 37 votes to 12 with 9 abstentions (Security Council Resolution 69; UN record of the admission). It nonetheless expressed reservations about the Arab refugees (*Yearbook of the United Nations 1948-49*). **The 1947 abstention and the 1949 favourable vote are two distinct acts.**

## 5. A Jewish imagination of the saviour {#s5}

The Book of Esther provides a powerful narrative grammar. Haman obtains an edict “to destroy, massacre and exterminate” the Jews of the kingdom; Mordecai then asks Esther to use her access to the king to plead their cause (Esther 3; Esther 4:7-8). Esther takes a personal risk: appearing before the sovereign without being summoned can mean death if he does not extend his sceptre (Esther 4:11). The reversal culminates when the king authorises a new edict in favour of the Jews, rather than formally annulling the first (Esther 8:6-8).

The midrashic work does not seek to identify Cohen with Esther, or with Mordecai. Nor does it ask whether Cohen knew or imitated the biblical story. It starts from a Jewish imagination in which the question of the saviour is essential: in the midst of a collective threat, one person can become the instrument of a reversal of history. That person is not necessarily a Messiah; it may be a queen at first concealing her identity, an adviser who sounds the alarm, a resistance fighter, a diplomat, or a marginal figure placed in the right place at the right time.

In Esther, salvation takes the form of a human intervention. Esther agrees to expose herself before the king; Mordecai recognises that her position cannot be separated from the fate of her people. The sentence “who knows whether you have not come to royal estate for such a time as this?” (Esther 4:14) gives this thought a sharp form: a singular position can become a historical responsibility. The story does not make Esther the Messiah; it makes it thinkable that an individual, within ordinary history and within the structures of power, might become a bearer of salvation.

The legend of Morris Cohen can be read within this same imaginative method. After the Shoah, in the collective narrative that accompanies the possibility of a Jewish state, Cohen appears as the Jewish man placed close to a distant power and able to tip a decision. It matters little here that he is neither a double of Esther nor a messianic figure: the legend shapes him as an agent of salvation. It answers the same question as the story of Esther: how can a person, seemingly secondary in the face of empires and institutions, help change the course of Jewish history?

The parallels — Jewish identity in the diaspora, proximity to a foreign power, intervention at the moment of a collective decision, the passage from deadlock to opening — are therefore not proofs of kinship between the two stories. They reveal one and the same way of telling: faced with collective vulnerability, the narrative looks for the figure through whom action becomes possible again. Esther, Mordecai and Cohen do not play the same role, and the Messiah is not simply an amplified version of Cohen. But these figures inhabit one imagination: history is never entirely closed; a human subject can become within it the site of salvation.

---

## 6. The limits this method requires {#s6}

The difference in moral and historical scale remains essential. Esther tells of an explicit and immediate threat of annihilation (Esther 3:13); the Cohen episode concerns an alleged diplomatic action around an international vote, whose effectiveness cannot be established. This difference does not forbid midrash; it sets its ethics. Placing stories in resonance means neither reducing one to the other nor turning Resolution 181 into the equivalent of Haman's decree.

The point is therefore not to demonstrate that Cohen was a “modern Esther” or a hidden Messiah. Cohen is indeed present in a popular Jewish and Sino-Israeli memory: the Jewish press was already portraying him in 1932 as a former adviser to Sun (Jewish Telegraphic Agency), and present-day publications readily present him as an unsung hero. The work concerns the imaginative function his legend can take on: not to repeat Esther, but to bring the Jewish question of the saviour to life again in a modern story.

---

## 7. Conclusion {#s7}

Historical research does not drain the story of its force: it clarifies what belongs to the archive and what belongs to legend. Morris Cohen is a genuine figure of passage between the Jewish and Chinese worlds, whose legend condenses the hope that a personal relationship might move History. But China's 1947 decision proceeded from institutions, diplomats and balances of power that Cohen did not, on his own, control.

The work of midrash then begins, on another plane. It does not aim to say that Cohen is Esther, nor to make diplomatic history a repetition of biblical history. It places both stories in the same imagination of salvation: at certain moments, a singular person can carry a people's hope and help bend its history. That is where Cohen, Esther and the messianic horizon can be studied together: not as the same characters, but as different figures of one Jewish question — who can save, and how does salvation become thinkable within history?
