## 1. The thesis in one sentence {#s1}

**Dominant contemporary usage of the word *kāfir* — that which circulates in a significant part of globalized Islamic discourse and is mirrored by Western Islamophobia — constitutes a modern and minoritarian rupture with the deep memory of the Semitic root *k-p-r / k-f-r*, whose plurality of meanings (material, cultic, expiatory, juridical, agrarian, mystical, theological) has been held together by all the great inheriting traditions (rabbinic, Christian, Sufi, falsafian, classical Sunni and Shīʿī) until the turning point of the 18th-20th centuries.**

This thesis is neither apologetic (it does not defend an ideal Islam against deviants) nor polemically external (it does not attack Islam from outside). It is *philological and comparative*. It rests on primary texts, critical translations, and the received history of the root, as the entire *Kafir / Kippur* dossier has established.

---

## 2. Position of the problem: the two mirrors {#s2}

Anyone who today opens a Western newspaper, a jihadist website, a Saudi schoolbook, a televised sermon or a public conversation on Islam will encounter the word *kāfir* with one *single* dominant meaning: *the unbeliever*, the infidel, the non-Muslim, in a stable and negative polemical charge. On the radical Islamist side, this word grounds military operations; on the Islamophobic side, it grounds essentializations. From both sides, it is *the same word* in *the same single meaning*.

**Yet no reader who has frequented the primary sources of pre-Islamic Arabic, the Qur'an itself, classical *kalām*, *falsafa*, Sufism, can accept this reduction.** The root *k-f-r* is, in these sources, *plurivocal*. Reducing it to a single sense belongs to a discursive operation that is neither philological nor theological, but political and identitarian.

The present article recapitulates the argument diffused through the four preceding articles and concentrates it into a *grid of semantic reopening*. Its intention is explicitly *engaged*: not in the sense of partisan polemic, but in the sense of an *engaged comparative philology*, in continuity with the tradition of Massignon, Corbin, Arkoun, Reynolds, Boyarin.

## 3. The seven attested poles {#s3}

Article 1 of the dossier established that the root *k-p-r / k-f-r* deploys seven attested poles around a unique gestural core (*to cover*). Recapitulation:

**3.1 Material pole** — Akkadian *kupru*, Hebrew *kofer* (Gen 6:14), *ḥemar* at Babel (Gen 11:3, neighbouring root), *kāfir* = cloud / sea / armour in pre-Islamic Arabic. *To cover as a technical act of protection or sealing.*

**3.2 Cultic-expiatory pole** — Hebrew *kapporet* (Lev 16), *kipper* (Lev 17:11), Pauline *hilastērion* (Rom 3:25), Qur'anic divine *takfīr* (Q 5:65; 8:29), *fiqh* *kaffāra* (Q 5:89). *To cover as merciful effacement of fault.*

**3.3 Juridical-ransom pole** — Hebrew *kofer-nefesh* (Ex 30:12; Num 35:31), Arabic *kaffāra* (Q 4:92; 58:3-4). *To cover as substitutive payment for a debt of blood or oath.*

**3.4 Agrarian pole** — Qur'anic *kuffār* = peasants (Q 57:20), Hebrew *zerāʿīm* (Gen 26:12; Ps 126), synoptic *ho speirōn* (Matt 13:3-9). *To cover as fertile burial of the seed for rising.*

**3.5 Ethical-relational pole** — Meccan Qur'anic *kufr* opposed to *shukr* (Q 14:7; 27:40; 76:3), *kafūr* (Q 22:66) = fundamental ingratitude. *To cover as refusal of recognition, forgetting of the gift.*

**3.6 Theological-polemical pole** — *kufr bi-l-āyāt* (Q 6:27), *kufr bi-llāh* (Q 16:106), *kāfir* opposed to *muʾmin* (Q 47:1-3), late Mishnaic *kofer ba-ʿiqqar* (Sanh. 99a). *To cover as refusal of the revealed truth.*

**3.7 Paradoxical mystical pole** — *kufr* of the self in Hallāj, *taʿdād al-ʿuqūd* in Ibn ʿArabī, sectarian *kufr* in Rūmī. *To cover as blindness to transcendence, paradoxically performed by the one who claims to fix it.*

### Recapitulative table

| Pole | Gesture | Polarity | Subject |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material | Seal | Neutral/positive | Human |
| Cultic-expiatory | Efface fault | Positive | God (or priest by delegation) |
| Juridical | Substitute a payment | Positive | Human (redemption) |
| Agrarian | Bury the seed | Positive | Human (sower) |
| Ethical | Forget the gift | Negative | Human (ingrate) |
| Theological | Refuse truth | Negative | Human (denier) |
| Paradoxical mystical | Reduce God to a form | Negative (but inversive) | Allegedly believing human |

**Decisive observation.** Of the seven poles, **four are positive or neutral**, and three are negative. *The native balance of the root is therefore not negative.*

## 4. The proof from primary sources {#s4}

### 1 Philological argument

Any student of Semitic languages familiar with Lane (*Arabic-English Lexicon* VII), Ibn Manẓūr (*Lisān al-ʿArab*), Zabīdī (*Tāj al-ʿArūs*), confirms that poles 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4 are massively attested in pre-Quranic Arabic and preserved in the classical commentaries. **Exclusively polemical reading of the word does not stand against the lexicography.**

### 2 Internal Qur'anic argument

The Qur'an itself *preserves* plurality. Divine *takfīr* (Q 5:65; 8:29; 64:9; 4:31) and the *kaffāra* (Q 5:89; 4:92; 58:3-4) are *positive* there. Q 57:20 employs *al-kuffār* in its archaic agricultural sense. **To reduce Qur'anic *kufr* to a single pole is therefore an operation that *contradicts the canonical text itself*.**

### 3 Argument from classical *kalām*

Al-Ghazālī, in his *Faysal al-tafriqa* (11th c.), writes explicitly:

> *"One must abstain to the maximum from takfīr, for declaring a Muslim kāfir exposes one to becoming oneself kāfir if the accusation is false."*

The majority position of classical Sunnism (Ashʿariyya) and Hanafism (Murjiʾa) is *restraint*. To practise extensive *takfīr* is *in disagreement with the majoritarian Islamic tradition itself*.

### 4 Argument from *fiqh*

Classical *fiqh* rigorously preserves the expiatory pole (the *kaffārāt*) and has never reduced the root to the polemical pole. Every practising Muslim who breaks an oath and redeems it through alms (Q 5:89) performs a *takfīr* — and the word, in his mouth, means *"merciful covering by God,"* not anathema.

### 5 Sufi argument

Classical Sufism *inverted* the root. Hallāj, Ibn ʿArabī, Rūmī, ʿAṭṭār consider *true kufr* precisely as sectarianism and extended *takfīr* — the thought that *reduces* God to a single form.

### 6 Comparative argument

The Jewish and Christian traditions, which inherit the same root, have massively preserved its positive pole: Yom Kippur remains the summit of the Jewish calendar; the *kapporet* was christologized in Paul; the sower and the grain nourished the evangelical imaginary. A root that, in the sister tradition, is *salvific* cannot, in the Islamic tradition, be *univocally anathematic* — except by performing an explicit rupture.

### 7 Partial conclusion

Across the six arguments, the exclusively polemical use of *kāfir* is:
- philologically unfounded,
- Qur'anically unfaithful,
- theologically minoritarian in *kalām*,
- juridically truncating,
- mystically contradictory,
- comparatively aberrant.

## 5. The proof from genealogy {#s5}

### 1 Historical argument

The contemporary extension of *takfīr* has an identifiable *date*. It begins with post-Ibn Taymiyya radicalization (14th c.), intensifies with Wahhabism (18th c.) and reaches its conceptual inversion point with Mawdūdī and Quṭb (20th c.). **Before Quṭb (and more precisely before *Maʿālim fī al-ṭarīq*, 1964), no major Sunni theologian holds that contemporary Muslim societies live in collective *jāhiliyya*.**

### 2 Sociological argument

The *al-Takfīr wa-l-Hijra* movement (1971-1977) is numerically marginal but *ideologically matricial*. Daesh (2014-2019) is the most radical expression of the takfīrī trajectory. None of these movements represents global Islam: they represent a *minoritarian militant fraction*, whose media amplification has distorted external (and sometimes internal) perception.

### 3 Doctrinal argument

The Amman Declaration (2005) — signed by 552 ulemas from 84 countries, including the heads of the principal Sunni, Shīʿī and Ibāḍī juridical schools — *explicitly condemns* extensive *takfīr*. **Mainstream religious authority of the Muslim world has therefore, collectively, disavowed the takfīrī doctrine.** Dominant contemporary use of *kāfir* in public discourse is therefore not the use of Islam as religious institution, but the use of a militant faction.

---

## 6. The springs of discursive capture {#s6}

How can it be explained that this *minoritarian and recent* sense has acquired such hegemony in public perception?

**6.1 Media concentration.** Polemical *kāfir* makes noise; peasant, expiatory, mystical *kāfir* does not make headlines. Media coverage selects the spectacular use and amplifies it (Roy, *L'islam mondialisé*, 2002).

**6.2 Clash of fundamentalisms.** On the radical Muslim side and the Western Islamophobic side, *both camps have an interest in freezing the word in its polemical sense*: for one, it is the identity weapon; for the other, it is the proof of Islam's essential alterity. These two uses *mutually reinforce* (Cesari 2013, Tibi 2008).

**6.3 Loss of classical formation.** A significant portion of Muslims trained since the 20th century in modernized curricula (state schools, universities, preaching by cassette then by Internet) no longer receives classical formation in *kalām*, advanced *fiqh*, philology. *Kāfir* reaches them amputated of its six other poles (Roy, *La sainte ignorance*, 2008; Lacroix 2011).

**6.4 Crisis of contemporary Muslim societies.** Quṭb writes in Nasser's prison; Maqdisī writes after the Gulf War; Daesh emerges in post-2003 Iraqi chaos. Extensive *takfīr* is tied to a *historical moment* in which collective Muslim identity is perceived as besieged, and the rhetoric of communal purity becomes psychologically attractive. This does not legitimize it; it helps to understand why it has seduced (Filiu 2009; Khatab 2006).

## 7. Reading grid for reopening {#s7}

The preceding argument allows the proposal of a practical grid, applicable to any occurrence of the word *kāfir* in contemporary debate.

**Step 1 — historicize.** What is the speaker's historical horizon? Does he use *kāfir* in the grammar of al-Ghazālī (reluctance, restraint) or in that of Quṭb (generalized *jāhiliyya*)? The distinction is *philologically* objectifiable: the context of use reveals it.

**Step 2 — pluralize.** Recall the six other poles. Q 57:20 is a philological weapon: a single Qur'anic verse suffices to show that the word *can* mean something else. The *kaffārāt* of *fiqh* are another. Hebrew *kapporet* is a third.

**Step 3 — compare.** Semitic *kāfir* has brothers: Hebrew *kofer*, Greek *hilastērion*, Syriac *kappōrā*. This transversality compels exit from strict intra-Islamic reading. It is *also* Islamic to wrest the word from its polemical enclosure: this is the operation performed by Massignon, Arkoun, Talbi, Soroush, Abou Zayd.

**Step 4 — locate politically.** Distinguish *Islam* (1.9 billion people, diverse juridical schools, a millennia-old tradition) from *contemporary takfīrī use* (a militant fraction, a short historical trajectory, a contestable discursive hegemony). Reject the Islamophobic mirror that essentializes.

**Step 5 — propose.** What is at stake is not only a philological question: it is the possibility of public discourse in which the word *kāfir* may rediscover its depth. This presupposes that one teach, that one write, that one preach in the rabbinic, patristic, Sufi, falsafian language. That the *kapporet*, the divine *takfīr*, the sower, the expiation be put back into circulation. **That the word be repluralized.**

## 8. The comparative coup de théâtre: the *kāfir* is the sower {#s8}

The most striking argument of the entire dossier is doubtless the following — it deserves to be posited as a thesis in its own right.

### 1 The synoptic parable of the sower

The synoptic parable of the sower (Matt 13:3-23 ǁ Mark 4 ǁ Luke 8) places evil **not in the sowing gesture, but in the figures of non-reception**: the bird that devours the unburied seed, the rock that prevents rooting, the thorn that chokes the shoot. *The sower himself is exempt from any reproach: he sows broadly, without condition.*

### 2 The *kāfir*-peasant in pre-Quranic Arabic

Yet — a massive and too-ignored philological fact — **the sower is the *kāfir*** in pre-Quranic Arabic. Q 57:20 attests the use within the canon. *Lisān al-ʿArab* confirms it under the entry *kafara*. Lane VII catalogues it. The *kāfir*-peasant is *the friend of the seed*, the one whose gesture *protects* the seed and allows it to rise.

### 3 The unexpected consequence

Contemporary use of polemical *kāfir* has, by semantic capture, **given the name of the evangelical sower to the bird that devours the seed**. The word that designated *the hand that covers to make live* has been redeployed to designate *the one who covers to make silent*. It is the same material operation (covering), but in two opposed finalities (life / death). Language itself protests: Q 57:20 attests, within the canon, that there is *another* *kāfir*.

### 4 Theological implication

If one takes the synoptic parable seriously, the *true "evil of the root"* is not in the gesture of covering but in the bird, the rock, the thorn — that is, in *snatching, sealing and choking*. And contemporary takfīrī use, which *snatches* the word from the other, *seals* the heart in the pure community, *chokes* diversity — practises exactly the three gestures that the parable assigns to evil.

**The *takfīrī*, in evangelical grammar, is the bird, the rock and the thorn.** The sower is what he could be if he rediscovered the original sense of his name: a *kāfir*-peasant, friend of the seed, who covers so that it may rise.

### 5 The comparative reading at its sharpest

The operation is exactly that which article 1 of the dossier identifies as *philological capture*: the word of *good recovering* has become, in dominant modern usage, the word of *bad recovering*. The sower has become the bird. The hand that saves has become the hand that effaces. **It is the grammar of Cain, who covers Abel's blood with earth — recovering-to-make-disappear, and not recovering-to-make-rise.** The modern takfīrī city reconducts, in this sense, the city founded by Cain (Gen 4:17) on uncovered blood.

## 9. Synthesis in seven theses {#s9}

**Thesis 1.** The Semitic root *k-p-r / k-f-r* has a unique gestural core (*to cover*) that unfolds into seven registers (material, cultic, juridical, agrarian, ethical, theological, paradoxical mystical).

**Thesis 2.** Four of the seven registers are positive or neutral; three are negative. The native balance of the root is not negative.

**Thesis 3.** All the great inheriting traditions (rabbinic, Christian, Sufi, falsafian, classical Sunni and Shīʿī) preserved the plurality of registers until modernity.

**Thesis 4.** The extension of *takfīr* to entire societies is a *modern innovation* (18th-20th centuries), datable, attributable to specific authors (Ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, Mawdūdī, Quṭb, Maqdisī), and explicitly disavowed by the majority of mainstream Islamic religious authority (Amman Declaration 2005).

**Thesis 5.** Dominant contemporary use of *kāfir* in global public discourse is in *rupture* with the Qur'an itself, classical *kalām*, Islamic jurisprudence, Sufism, and the sister traditions.

**Thesis 6.** This rupture is sustained by a *double mirror* — Islamist radicalism and Western Islamophobia — that *reduces the word to its sole polemical pole* by effacing the six others.

**Thesis 7.** Semantic reopening is possible, from the sources themselves, through a work of *pluralization* mobilizing comparative philology, classical theology, and transversal reading Tanakh-Gospels-Qur'an. This reopening is *internal* to Islam as tradition, and not an external critique.

## 10. Conclusion: repluralizing the word {#s10}

At the end of this dossier, what appears is not a critique of Islam but a *defence* of the Qur'an and the classical Islamic tradition against a *modern capture* that has reduced their richness. The voice that critiques extended *takfīr* is, *first and foremost*, a classical Muslim voice: it is Ghazālī who posits the rule of restraint, it is Ibn ʿArabī who inverts the root, it is Rūmī who says that the true *kufr* is sectarianism, it is the 552 ulemas of Amman who doctrinally disavow extended *takfīr*. The philologist only *makes that voice audible*.

To repluralize the word is not to betray Islam: it is to listen to it in the depth of its root. It is to recall that the synoptic parable's sower is *also* the *kāfir*-peasant of Q 57:20. It is to recall that the *takfīr* of the Qur'an itself is, in Q 5:65, the *merciful* effacement of fault by God. It is to recall that the *kapporet* of the Holy of Holies has become, in Paul, Christ-as-propitiatory. Three gestures that resemble each other like brothers: covering the seed, covering the fault, covering the ark. They are the gestures of life that *protects*.

The contemporary *takfīrī*, who claims to denounce the *kāfir*, does not know that he bears the same name. He does not know that the hand he denounces is, in the memory of his language, the hand of the sower. To remind him — through philology, through text, through history — is to open, perhaps, a door. A door in the wall that radical modernity has erected. *A narrow door, as the other dossier would say* — that which leads to the memory of the seven poles, to the plurality of senses, to the shared grammar of recovering-for-life.
