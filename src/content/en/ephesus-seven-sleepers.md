## 1. The story {#s1}

Under the persecution of Emperor Decius (around 250), seven young Christians of high birth flee Ephesus and take refuge in a cave on Mount Pion; the emperor has them walled in alive. Put to sleep by God, they awake nearly two centuries later, under Theodosius II, at the height of the controversy over the resurrection of bodies: their waking becomes the living proof that God raises the dead. The Qur'an (surat al-Kahf) makes them the People of the Cave (*Ashāb al-Kahf*), naming none of the characters and fixing neither their exact number nor the place, making the story available to every land.

## 2. A masculine story run through by the feminine {#s2}

All the visible heroes are men: seven soldiers, an emperor, a bishop. But on examination, every active and benevolent power in the story is feminine or maternal.

### The cave as matrix

The central act is a falling asleep in the belly of the mountain, then a waking: a second birth. The cave of Pion works as a telluric womb: the seven youths enter alive in one era and emerge alive in another, without having aged. The cave dug into the mother-earth takes over the function the Ephesian goddess fulfilled in the valley: to contain, to protect, to give life back. The story secretly opposes the matrix that protects to the throne that hunts: against the emperor's masculine power, which demands to be honoured as a god, salvation comes from the mountain's feminine refuge.

### The baker-woman: the woman of the waking

In the ancient versions (Syriac, then Jacobus de Voragine's *Golden Legend*), when Malchus, the youngest, goes into town to buy bread, a baker-woman is astonished by the coins he holds out, minted two centuries earlier: "Where did you find this ancient treasure?" This dialogue with a woman is the pivot of the story: it is she who, without knowing it, tells the Sleepers that they slept not one night but centuries. The masculine had fallen asleep in the cave; the feminine, remaining in the world, wakes it by telling it who it is. It is the function of a sibyl, a prophetess: telling time.

### The healer of folklore

Local tradition calls the cave the "witch's cave": a woman healer is said to have performed cures there since ancient times. The Christian story did not create the cave's sacredness; it overlaid a feminine memory of care. That memory continues still: the women of the region came to the cave seeking healing and fertility, tying ribbons, exactly as at Meryem Ana. The cave of Pion and the house on Coressos are the two poles of a single feminine pilgrimage on either side of the valley.

### The guardian of the threshold

In the Qur'anic version, the dog Qitmir lies on the threshold of the cave. Now the function of guardian of thresholds and crossroads belonged, in Ephesian antiquity, to Hecate, associated with Artemis at Ephesus itself, where she had her sanctuary in front of the city, and where statues of Artemis often carry figures of Hecate. The guardian of the cave's threshold discreetly takes the place of the goddess of thresholds.

## 3. Synthesis: the apparent rejection of the feminine and its persistence {#s3}

| Element | Appearance | Real function |
|---|---|---|
| The cave | Rocky refuge | Telluric matrix that protects and gives life back |
| The baker-woman | Market extra | She who reveals time and wakes |
| The healer | Folkloric "witch" | Feminine memory of care and fertility |
| The dog at the threshold | Picturesque detail | Guardian of the threshold, heir of Hecate |

The paradox: a story born of the masculine persecution par excellence (the deified emperor, the very thing Christians refuse to venerate) finds its whole apparatus of salvation in feminine figures. Emperor Decius is dead; Rome has fallen; the Sleepers, for their part, wake. The story figures what the feminine tradition of the sacred does at Ephesus: ensuring continuity when masculine powers collapse and succeed one another.

## 4. Threads left open {#s4}

The feminine never appears in the foreground of the Sleepers' story: neither virgin, nor goddess, nor even named. Should we see in this a deliberate erasure of the feminine by the monotheistic traditions, or on the contrary its underground persistence, more effective for being discreet, deposited in the stone, the threshold, the bread and the memory of women?
