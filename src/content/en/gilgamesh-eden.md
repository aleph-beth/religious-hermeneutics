## 1. Opening — A Hypothesis about Two Narratives {#s1}

There is a simple but consequential hypothesis behind these two founding narratives, Babylonian and Hebrew, separated by several centuries and irrigated by one and the same Near Eastern semantic basin: **death is where justice is revealed, and eternity is where the lie is spoken.**

Not that death is desirable — both texts say the opposite with equal violence. But death is that by which the human being, stripped of the illusion of infinite time, is thrown back upon the responsibility of building, here below and only once, something that will hold. Eternity, by contrast, never appears in these texts as an honest promise: it is withdrawn at the very moment it seemed within reach, and the serpent, in both cases, is the agent through which it slips away from man — a silent thief among the Babylonians, a lying oracle in Genesis.

A word of precision first, because the shortcut is common. The hero Gilgamesh is indeed a Sumerian king of Uruk (Sumerian poems about Bilgames circulate from the late third millennium onward), but **the epic that concerns us is Akkadian**: an Old Babylonian version around the eighteenth century BCE, then the twelve-tablet "Standard" version, attributed to the scholar Sîn-lēqi-unninni and fixed toward the end of the second millennium. The Eden narrative (Genesis 2–3), whatever dating one adopts for its oldest stratum — between the tenth and the sixth century — is later by at least several centuries, and by as much as a millennium if one counts from the Old Babylonian version. The two traditions moreover share, through the Babylonian *Atrahasis*, a common narrative kernel: the flood, which Tablet XI of Gilgamesh takes over almost word for word, and which Genesis reworks in its turn. The ground of the comparison is therefore no fancy: it is a documented inheritance.

## 2. Šiduri's Words — and Their Echo in Qohelet {#s2}

Gilgamesh only seeks eternity after losing Enkidu. As long as the friend lives, death is merely one fact among others; his disappearance makes it unbearable, and it is this unbearability — not some disinterested metaphysical curiosity — that drives the king of Uruk onto the road to Uta-napishti, the one mortal whom the gods made immortal, his reward for having survived the flood.

On that road stands Šiduri, the tavern-keeper at the edge of the sea. In the Old Babylonian version she delivers the most lucid speech in all Mesopotamian literature:

> "Gilgamesh, where are you wandering? The life you pursue you shall not find. When the gods created mankind, for mankind they established death; life they kept in their own hands. As for you, Gilgamesh, let your belly be full, day and night make merry… Gaze on the child who holds your hand, let your wife delight in your embrace — for such is the portion of mankind."
> — OB VA+BM (the Sippar tablet), col. iii

The Standard version abridges this passage; it is the Old Babylonian tablet that preserves it whole. And the biblical echo of these words is found not in Genesis but in Ecclesiastes: "Go, eat your bread with joy, and drink your wine with a merry heart… Enjoy life with the wife whom you love, all the days of your vaporous life" (Qohelet 9:7-9). The parallel has long been noted by Assyriologists and biblical scholars alike: the same refusal of immortality as a horizon, the same folding of meaning back onto given, measured, daily time. Near Eastern wisdom holds this thread from end to end: it is not in flight out of time that man finds his portion, but in counted time.

## 3. Gilgamesh: Sleep, the Plant, the Serpent {#s3}

Having reached Uta-napishti, Gilgamesh fails a first time. Challenged to stay awake six days and seven nights, he falls asleep at once — and Uta-napishti has a loaf of bread baked beside his sleeping body each day, so that the moulding of the loaves will count the days in his stead and forbid him to deny his sleep. The simplest of trials, staying awake, reveals the obvious: a body that cannot conquer sleep, that small daily death, will not conquer death.

Uta-napishti then offers him, at his wife's prompting, a consolation prize: a thorny plant at the bottom of the Apsû, the primordial freshwater ocean. Gilgamesh ties stones to his feet, dives, tears it loose. And he names it — a sovereign gesture — before his ferryman:

> "Ur-šanabi, this plant is the plant against the shortness of breath… I will carry it to Uruk-the-Sheepfold; I will have an old man eat some, to put the plant to the test. Its name shall be 'The Old Man Grows Young.' I too shall eat it, and return to my youth."
> — Tablet XI (after A. R. George's translation)

Note the letter of the text, for it matters: Gilgamesh does not renounce the plant for himself — he plans to eat of it "in his turn." But he plans *first* to have it tested by an elder of Uruk: the king's first gesture, faced with rejuvenation itself, is to bring it into the city's circuit, to submit it to a public trial, to share it. This is not yet distributive justice in the full sense; it is already its outline — and it suffices to distinguish this Gilgamesh from the tyrant of the prologue, who took everything for himself.

Then comes the scene every reader knows. On the way home, Gilgamesh bathes in a cool spring; a snake smells the fragrance of the plant, comes up in silence, carries it off — and, slipping away, sheds its skin. This, the etiological text implies, is why the serpent grows young by changing its skin, and why man, for his part, ages and dies. Gilgamesh sits down and weeps, "his tears streaming down the side of his nose" — not with rage, but with the bitter recognition that the game was lost from the start: "For whom, Ur-šanabi, have my arms grown weary?"

## 4. The Return to Uruk: the Walls and *kittu u mīšaru* {#s4}

He returns to Uruk and, instead of lamenting further, shows his ferryman the walls of the city:

> "Go up, Ur-šanabi, onto the wall of Uruk and walk around! Examine its foundations, inspect its brickwork: is its masonry not of kiln-fired brick? Did the Seven Sages not lay its foundations?"

The epic closes on this image — which also opened its prologue, in a perfect inclusio. It is no longer the eternity of the body that the king seeks: it is the duration of a city, whose ramparts, laws and name will outlive him. The only immortality available is that of the common work.

It is not indifferent that Gilgamesh, throughout his quest, walks under the protection of Shamash, the sun god. For Shamash is not only light: he is the divine judge par excellence, the one who sees everything, and his two attendants — sometimes said to be his children — are named **Kittu and Mīšaru**, personifications of truth and equity. The pair *kittu u mīšaru*, "truth and equity," is the very formula by which Mesopotamian kings, from Hammurabi to the Neo-Babylonian rulers, legitimize their reign: to establish *mīšarum* is to proclaim justice. Against the nocturnal serpent who carries off eternal life, it is the solar light of royal justice that alone remains Gilgamesh's to keep. The symbolism is coherent from end to end: eternity belongs to night and cunning; justice, to day and measure.

## 5. Evening before Morning: the Hebrew Calendar of Revelation {#s5}

The Hebrew world nevertheless inverts this relation to light without betraying its logic — and here lies a calendrical detail too often overlooked.

The day, in Genesis, begins neither at dawn nor at midnight: it begins in the evening. "There was evening and there was morning: day one" (Genesis 1:5 — the Hebrew says *yom eḥad*, "day one," not "first day"; the nuance has occupied rabbinic commentary at length). The formula recurs at every stage of creation, and Judaism has preserved it in its liturgical usage: the new day opens at the fall of the preceding one, the Sabbath begins on Friday evening.

Every Hebrew day is thus born in darkness, without light as yet, and only in its course does it disclose itself to daylight. Night is not the end of a cycle burning out: it is the formless beginning from which the day rises as an unveiling. This structure — darkness first, revelation after — announces precisely the movement of the Eden narrative: a world first suspended in ambiguity, in ignorance of good and evil, in the serpent's double speech; then the fault itself, heavy as it is, raising a day of knowledge and, in the end, of justice. Where Gilgamesh places justice under the sun of Shamash, Genesis has it born *out of* the night — but in both cases it stands on the side of time unfolding, never on the side of time abolished.

## 6. The Serpent of Eden: the Lie Concerns Eternity {#s6}

The serpent of Eden operates with the very weapon that escapes Gilgamesh: life without end. God had said to the man: "on the day you eat of it you shall surely die" (*mot tamut*, Genesis 2:17); the serpent answers, word for word in reverse: "you shall surely not die" (*lo-mot temutun*, Genesis 3:4). The Hebrew makes the symmetry audible as no translation can: the serpent's negation copies the infinitive absolute of the divine sentence in order to overturn it term by term. It is a counter-oracle.

A literalist reader stops here, embarrassed, since Adam does not die that very day — he will live, says Genesis 5:5, nine hundred and thirty years. And it is precisely this embarrassment that forces one out of the letter and into the symbol. The promised death is not an immediate decease: it is the loss of an access. In verse 22, once the fault is committed, God says:

> "Behold, the man has become like one of us, knowing good and evil; and now, lest he stretch out his hand and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live forever—" (Genesis 3:22)

And it is for this reason alone, explicitly, that the couple is driven from the garden, cherubim and "the flame of the whirling sword" posted "to guard the way to the tree of life" (Genesis 3:24). The text says in black and white that the expulsion has one function: to bar the road to eternity. The serpent's lie did indeed concern eternity — and it is mortality now conscious of itself, sealed by toil, by childbearing in pain, by the return to dust (Genesis 3:16-19), that comes to sanction, with an almost geometric rigor, the initial deceiving word. Man was not immortal by nature: he had access to a tree. The lie cost him the access, not an attribute.

## 7. *Naḥash*: Divination, Bronze, Polysemy {#s7}

Hebrew adds here a thickness that translation erases. **נָחָשׁ** (*naḥash*), the serpent, shares its consonants with the root **נחשׁ** (*n-ḥ-sh*) of divination, the art of reading omens: it is the verb used by Joseph's steward about the cup — "the one from which my lord drinks, and by which he indeed divines (*naḥesh yenaḥesh*)" (Genesis 44:5) — and by Laban himself: "I have learned by divination (*niḥashti*) that the LORD has blessed me because of you" (Genesis 30:27). Whether the kinship is etymological or a simple homonymy exploited by the text, the wordplay is there, available, and the tradition heard it: the serpent of Eden is no mere lying animal. It speaks as a diviner; it pronounces a false augury — an utterance with inverted oracular status, aping the divine word ("you shall not die") the better to contradict it.

And the same animal, later, will save rather than kill. In the desert, when the burning serpents bite the people, God commands Moses to set a *saraph* on a pole; Moses fashions a **bronze serpent** — *neḥash neḥoshet*, the text playing a second time on the root, serpent and bronze sharing the same consonants — and "whoever was bitten, when he looked at the bronze serpent, lived" (Numbers 21:8-9). Centuries later, Hezekiah will break it in pieces, because the people had ended up burning incense to it as to an idol, under the name **Neḥushtan** (2 Kings 18:4).

The biblical serpent is thus never univocal: poison and remedy, lie and healing, death and life — according to whether it is allowed to speak on its own authority or lifted up as a sign. This is exactly the polysemy that a fundamentalist reading, in search of a purity of meaning that never existed, cannot tolerate.

## 8. The Serpent's Moult: Cyclical Time versus Counted Time {#s8}

What the two traditions share, at bottom, is one and the same intuition about the moult. This animal that seems to live again by changing its skin becomes, in the Near Eastern imagination — and far beyond, as Mircea Eliade showed in his analysis of lunar symbolism, where the serpent figures among the animals of the moon, a "force that regenerates itself" — the embodiment of cyclical time, of an eternal return in which nothing is ever truly lost. Tablet XI gives the etiological version: the serpent took the plant, therefore it moults; man did not keep it, therefore he dies.

It is precisely this circular temporality that both narratives **refuse** to man. Man receives a linear, irreversible time — the time in which an act has consequences that do not fade. And it is this irreversibility that makes justice possible, indeed necessary: where everything returns, nothing is paid for; where nothing returns, everything counts.

The sign of it is almost too neat in Genesis. Hardly have Adam and Eve left the garden when the text moves on, without transition, to Cain and Abel: the first murder in human history and, in the same gesture, the first scene of justice — divine interrogation ("Where is Abel your brother?", Genesis 4:9), inquest ("the voice of your brother's blood cries out to me from the ground"), sentence, and a mark as protective as it is punitive (Genesis 4:15). Death, having barely entered the world through the serpent's lie, at once produces its tribunal.

In Gilgamesh the mechanism is identical though slower: it is Enkidu's death that sets the king in motion, and it is by accepting his own finitude that he becomes, at last, a builder — the one of whom the Standard version's prologue says that he "brought back knowledge from before the flood" and engraved his trials on a stele. The hunter of eternity dies on the riverbank, with the plant; it is the just king who returns to Uruk.

## 9. Checking the Sources — What the Parallel Says, and What It Does Not {#s9}

A parallel is worth only as much as the limits one marks around it. Three safeguards.

> **The serpent of Gilgamesh does not lie.** It does not speak; it steals, in silence, while the hero bathes. The "deceiving voice" is proper to Eden. What the two texts share is therefore not the serpent's lie but its **function**: in both cases it is the agent through which eternity, for an instant within reach, definitively escapes man — and the operator of the transfer between cyclical time (the moult, which falls to the serpent) and linear time (death, which falls to us). The lie, in Gilgamesh, lies elsewhere: in the hero's own hope, which Šiduri and Uta-napishti refute in turn.

> **The plant is not a pure gesture of altruism.** The letter of Tablet XI has Gilgamesh say that he will first have an elder eat of it, to test it, and that he will then eat of it himself. To read a renunciation of self into this passage would be to strain the text; to read in it the entry of rejuvenation into the public circuit of the city — trial, sharing, naming — is, by contrast, grounded in its very letter.

> **Ricœur compares Eden not to Gilgamesh, but to the theogonies.** In *The Symbolism of Evil* (the second part of *Finitude and Guilt*), the typology opposes the Adamic myth to the "drama of creation," whose model is the *Enuma Elish* — where Marduk carves the world out of Tiamat's corpse: there, evil is older than man; here, it enters a world created good, through a freedom that fails. The epic of Gilgamesh is not a theogony of primordial combat; it is a wisdom of finitude. If it meets the Adamic myth, it is along that path — death as the portion of the human — and not through cosmogony.

These reservations made, the common kernel holds: two neighboring literatures, nourished from the same stock (the *Atrahasis* flood is the textual proof), which both refuse to make eternity a promise for man, and which bring forth the figure of the just one — builder-king or Cain's tribunal — immediately after the loss of immortality.

## 10. Conclusion — Finitude as the Condition of Justice {#s10}

We find here, formulated in a far older mythological language, an intuition that philosophy will restate in its own way. It is Heidegger who will make being-toward-death (*Sein zum Tode*, *Being and Time*, §§ 46-53) the condition of all authentic existence — the one that tears the individual from the anonymity of the "they" (*das Man*) and returns him to his own responsibility. It is Ricœur who will show that the Adamic myth yields its meaning only when confronted with the neighboring narratives of Mesopotamia, in a cross-reading whose slope the present essay follows.

The lie, in both narratives, always has the same structure: it promises a time without remainder, without loss, without account to render. Justice can only be born of counted time — of the awareness that one day an answer must be given for what was done with the little time granted. Šiduri says it in her way, tavern-keeper at the world's edge: the life you pursue you shall not find; gaze on the child who holds your hand. Genesis says it in its own: the way to the tree of life is guarded, but the soil is there, to be worked, and the brother is there, for whom an answer will be required.

That is perhaps the whole lesson that Gilgamesh and the garden of Eden, each in its own manner, attempt to transmit: lucidity before finitude is not resignation. It is the sole condition under which a man — or a city — can become just.
